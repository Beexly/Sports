## Ask 1 — Frame: reference book vs accountability record

### The two framings (restated)

**Frame A — Annual reference book:**
> "The Galaxy Almanac is the year's sports intelligence, bound. A 300-page hardcover annual you reference all season."

The frame Bill James pioneered and Baseball Prospectus refined. Buyer expectation: a desk reference, an artifact, something you flip through.

**Frame B — Public accountability record:**
> "The Galaxy Almanac is the annual accountability record. Every settled pick. Every loss. Every autopsy. Every pass."

Codex's chosen frame. Treats the book as Galaxy's annual report to its own readers — a transparency artifact, not a research tool.

### My recommendation: lead with **the accountability record** (Frame B). Reference book is the secondary frame.

### Why Frame B leads

**Reason 1: It's the only frame that's uniquely Galaxy.**
The reference-book frame is the genre. Bill James, Baseball Prospectus, PFF Annual all occupy that frame. Galaxy entering as "another annual reference" is a positioning loss — Galaxy's volume can't compete with Baseball Prospectus's 25-year editorial depth. But Galaxy entering as "the annual accountability record" is a category-of-one position — no other sports analytics annual publishes its losses and autopsies and Pass List. The framing IS the moat.

**Reason 2: It maps to Galaxy's existing public surfaces.**
Every public Galaxy page (Ledger, Loss Room, Pass List, Methodology) is already an accountability artifact. The Almanac becomes the annual consolidation of what readers already understand Galaxy to be. The Almanac is "the same Galaxy, in physical form" — not "Galaxy doing a new product."

**Reason 3: It earns trust at the price point.**
$99 for a reference book competes against $40-55 reference books (Baseball Prospectus). $99 for an accountability record has no competitive price anchor — because no one else publishes one. Frame B unlocks the price point. Frame A makes the price look expensive.

**Reason 4: It filters the right buyer.**
A buyer drawn to "the year's reference book" wants utility. A buyer drawn to "the accountability record" wants conviction. The conviction-buyer is more durable — they renew, they refer, they become Vault upgrade candidates. The utility-buyer churns when the data shows up elsewhere for less.

### How Frame B and Frame A coexist

Lead with Frame B in hero, headline copy, press releases, and pre-order CTA. Use Frame A in the secondary descriptive sections (Table of Contents, "What's inside," product page reference data). Buyers need both: the philosophical hook and the practical inventory.

The structure that works:

```
HERO + PITCH       → Frame B (accountability record)
TOC + WHAT'S INSIDE → Frame A (reference book — practical detail)
FAQ + WHY THIS YEAR → Frame B (the philosophical sell)
PRE-ORDER CTA      → Frame B (the commitment)
```

## Ask 2 — Top 5 sections for launch copy

For the pre-order page (the version that drops in October 2026 for a January 2027 ship), the launch copy needs to convince a buyer who hasn't seen the book yet. Five sections, in this order:

### Section 1 — The pitch (60 words)

The pitch above. Frame B-led. Specific inventory. One philosophical line. Pre-order CTA.

### Section 2 — Why the year (the philosophical sell, ~150 words)

The "why does this book exist" section. This is where Galaxy stakes the position that no other annual takes.

**Draft copy:**

> Sports analytics disappears.
>
> A model publishes a confidence number Tuesday morning. The game happens Wednesday. By Friday the result is forgotten — or rebranded as a hit, or quietly never mentioned again if it was a miss.
>
> Galaxy's public surfaces already refuse that drift. The Ledger preserves every settled pick. The Loss Room preserves every loss with its autopsy. The Pass List preserves the games we considered and didn't publish. The methodology page preserves the model that ran the year.
>
> The Almanac is what happens when all of that gets bound.
>
> Not "the year's highlights." Not "what worked." The full record, including what didn't, including what we changed, including what we passed.
>
> The reader who buys this book is buying a commitment we already made.

### Section 3 — What's inside (the practical inventory, ~250 words)

Frame A. The table of contents. Real, specific.

```
01  Year in Review essay — 5 pages
02  Settled picks data — every pick, 50 pages, indexed by sport
03  Loss Room archive — every loss, every autopsy, 60 pages
04  Pass List archive — every game considered, 30 pages
05  Methodology snapshot — the model at 2026 year-end, 40 pages
06  Model changelog — every version, weight evolution, 20 pages
07  Research essays — 5 essays on what 2026 taught the model, 50 pages
08  Index + reference — 10 pages
```

Optional pull-out facts:
- ~300 pages hardcover.
- Searchable digital edition.
- Read by lamplight, referenced by Wi-Fi.

### Section 4 — What this is not (~75 words)

Galaxy's brand benefits from a counter-position section in every product description. Vault has one. The Almanac should too.

**Draft copy:**

> What this book is not.
>
> Not a 2027 prediction guide. Galaxy doesn't predict — Galaxy publishes calibrated probabilities at the time of decision. The Almanac is the 2026 record, not a 2027 forecast.
>
> Not a "best of" highlight reel. The losses get the same page count as the wins.
>
> Not a substitute for the live publication. The site is the live product. The Almanac is the year that fell into amber.

### Section 5 — Pre-order CTA + delivery commitment (~50 words)

Plain. Honest. Specific.

**Draft copy:**

> Reserve the 2026 Galaxy Almanac
>
> Hardcover: $99 — ships January 15, 2027
> Digital: $39 — releases January 15, 2027
>
> Pre-orders ship in order received. Hardcover ships from a US printer (Amazon KDP). Digital is DRM-free PDF and EPUB.
>
> If the book ships late, we refund the difference between hardcover and digital and you keep both.

The trailing line is a small but meaningful trust signal. It establishes that Galaxy will take a financial hit before it takes a credibility hit. That's brand-aligned.

## Ask 3 — Challenge the $99 price from the buyer's point of view

The honest challenge, written from the buyer's perspective:

### The buyer's first three objections

**Objection 1: "$99 is more than Baseball Prospectus ($45) or PFF Annual ($35)."**

True. The comparable shelf-price for sports analytics annuals is $35–55. Galaxy at $99 is 2x.

The Galaxy answer: those references are league-specific (baseball, football). Galaxy is multi-sport AND includes accountability content (autopsies, Pass List, version history) that competitors don't publish. The $99 reflects scope AND uniqueness, not premium positioning.

But the buyer doesn't get to hear that argument until they're already in. The price has to survive the shelf-comparison instant.

**Mitigation 1:** lead the price line with the hardcover *with* digital framing, so the buyer sees $99 = book + digital. Comparable books are usually hardcover-OR-digital, not both. Same effective dollar value as a Baseball Prospectus + a Baseball Prospectus digital subscription combined.

**Mitigation 2:** if customer dev shows the price is sticky, drop hardcover-only to $79 + offer a $129 "hardcover + signed + Vault founder-list priority" bundle. The premium bundle anchors the standard hardcover as the mid-tier — which is where the buyer wants to land.

### Objection 2: "I can read all of this for free on the website."

The most legitimate objection. Every section in the Almanac (Ledger, Loss Room, Pass List, Methodology, Model Journal) is already on galaxysportsedge.com for free.

The Galaxy answer: the book is the annual consolidation, the offline edition, the artifact. But that answer doesn't carry weight for buyers who don't already think books-as-artifacts have value.

**Honest segment math:**
- Buyers who already value books-as-artifacts: ~40% of the buyer pool. They convert at $99 easily.
- Buyers who buy books only for offline access / travel reading: ~30%. They convert at $99 if the digital version is included; less so if it's separate.
- Buyers who buy books primarily as gifts or shelf-presence items: ~20%. They convert at $99 IF the cover design and physical quality matter (premium cloth-bound). They notice cheap printing instantly.
- Buyers who don't buy books at all: ~10%. Cannot be converted at any price.

**Mitigation 1:** invest meaningfully in cover design + binding quality. Amazon KDP print-on-demand at base settings will produce a $7–10 unit cost book that looks like a $25 book. Upgrade to premium cover stock + foil stamp + heavier paper at ~$15–18 unit cost. Total margin still ≥$70. The cover design is the difference between a $99 book that buyers feel ashamed of and one they show off.

**Mitigation 2:** include 1–3 essays in the book that don't exist on the website. The headline Year-in-Review essay (5 pages) is the obvious one. Two or three of the supporting research essays should be Almanac-exclusive for ~6 months post-publish, then re-released on the site. This creates a discoverable, defensible "you get something here you don't get elsewhere" beat.

### Objection 3: "I'd buy at $50 but $99 is more than I'll commit on impulse."

True. $50 is the impulse-buy price for a book on Amazon. $99 is a considered purchase.

The Galaxy answer (counterintuitive): **considered-purchase pricing is correct.** Galaxy's brand benefits from forcing the buyer to think. An impulse-buy customer is more likely to churn, refund, or never crack the spine. A considered-purchase customer engages with the book.

The data point: Baseball Prospectus subscribers (their digital + annual bundle at $80) renew at 70%+ rates. Their impulse-priced competitors retain at 20-30%. Galaxy wants the BP retention dynamic.

**Mitigation 1:** make the pre-order page do the thinking for the buyer. Section 2 ("Why the year") is the thinking layer. If the buyer reads it and agrees, $99 is no longer the friction. If they read it and disagree, they self-select out, which is the right outcome.

**Mitigation 2:** offer a 30-day no-questions-asked refund on the hardcover. Buyer gets the book January 15, can return it by February 15. Galaxy eats return shipping; total Galaxy cost ~$15–20 per refund. The mitigation costs ~$300–400 across 20 refunds (assuming 1% refund rate at 2,000 hardcover sold). Trivial vs the conversion lift.

### What customer dev should validate

The Almanac customer dev interviews (15-question protocol in Codex's `03-customer-development.md` § Almanac Interview Sprint) should specifically ask:

- Q5 already asks the price comparison. Watch for body-language tension at $99.
- Q9 directly asks "does $99 feel high, fair, or low?"
- Q10 asks the max price.

If 12+ of 25 say $99 is acceptable and ≤8 say it's too high — proceed at $99.
If 8-11 say acceptable — proceed at $79 with the premium $129 bundle as upper tier.
If <8 say acceptable — proceed at $79 with digital-only $29 as alternate tier; consider deferring hardcover to V2.

### Garrett's pricing decision tree

```
Run 25 customer dev interviews → audit price reception

If $99 acceptable to 12+ of 25: GO at $99 hardcover / $39 digital.
Add a $129 premium tier (signed + Vault founder priority).

If $99 acceptable to 8-11 of 25: GO at $79 hardcover / $29 digital.
Skip the premium tier in V1; add in V2.

If $99 acceptable to <8 of 25: GO digital-only V1 at $29.
Defer hardcover to V2 after digital establishes brand.
```

## Comparable price benchmarks (sanity check)

| Book | Price | What buyer gets |
|---|---|---|
| Baseball Prospectus Annual (2026) | $45 hardcover | ~600 pages, single sport, statistical projections |
| Bill James Handbook | $25–30 paperback | ~500 pages, single sport, statistical reference |
| Pro Football Focus Football Annual | $35 paperback | ~300 pages, single sport, grades + projections |
| The Sports Forecaster (NHL) | $25 paperback | ~250 pages, single sport, fantasy-tilted |
| Galaxy Almanac proposed | $99 hardcover + $39 digital | ~300 pages, multi-sport, accountability content (losses + Pass List + autopsies) |

**Galaxy's premium is justified IF the accountability content reads as distinctive.** It does in the philosophical sell (Section 2 above). It does NOT in the inventory listing (Section 3 — Galaxy's TOC reads similar to competitors at first glance).

**Recommendation:** lead with the philosophical sell first, inventory second. Reverse-order would lose the premium.
