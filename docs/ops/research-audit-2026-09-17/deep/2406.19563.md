## Exact statistical contract

### Data and likelihood

Section 3.2: I judges/observations assessing J objects; observation i supplies ordering `Pi_i`, number ranked `R_i`, and **known choice set S_i**. Conditional comparisons follow a common worth vector `omega > 0` and are independent (the paper writes iid conditional on the varying sets). Eq. 1 uses the convention `i ≺ j` for i **preferred over** j:

`P(i preferred to j | omega) = omega_i / (omega_i + omega_j)`.

The Plackett–Luce likelihood is the product, over observations and selection stages, of

`omega_(chosen item) / sum_(remaining eligible items) omega_item`.

For complete rankings all J items are initially eligible. In a top-R ranking, unranked eligible items remain in the denominator. In a groupwise comparison only the assessed subset is eligible. For an NBA game, encode a comparison **only of the two participating teams**, not a top-two ranking of the league. The package's `groupwise` option changes exactly those denominators.

A synthetic strengths vector `(2,1,100)` illustrates the difference: A beating B has likelihood `2/3`; wrongly treating the result as top-two A then B among all three has likelihood `2/10403`. These are different observations, not implementation-equivalent representations.

No score-margin model, home advantage, roster dynamics, injury covariates, schedule-selection model or odds likelihood is included in the demonstrated NBA fit. Section 6 explicitly leaves covariate extensions and disconnected comparison sets for future work.

### Estimand and ties

PSSF (Partition-based Spike-and-Slab Fusion), Eqs. 4 and 8:

1. Draw a partition G of J objects into nonempty groups.
2. Draw each group's unique worth `nu_k` iid from `Gamma(a_gamma,b_gamma)` (shape, rate).
3. Set `omega_j = nu_(group containing j)`.
4. Generate ordinal comparisons from the appropriate BTL-family likelihood.

Targets include the joint posterior of the partition, K, group worths, object worths and their ordering. A pair co-clusters iff its worth parameters are **exactly equal**. Continuous shrinkage alone would assign zero probability to equality; PSSF adds positive probability mass on equality subspaces. This is clustering **objects**, not judges into preference subpopulations.

For any pair, these quantities differ:

- `C_ab = P(G_a=G_b | data)` — co-clustering probability;
- `Q_ab = P(omega_a>omega_b | data)` — latent superiority probability;
- `p_ab = E[omega_a/(omega_a+omega_b) | data]` — posterior predictive binary event probability under the model.

Within a shared cluster the model gives a fair binary matchup. Across posterior draws, mixture averaging is necessary; plugging posterior median strengths into a ratio is not generally the posterior predictive mean. Pairwise co-cluster marginals form an uncertainty summary, not automatically a valid hard partition after arbitrary thresholding. A displayed consensus/MAP partition discards uncertainty and needs its decision rule documented.

Rank ties are **not tied observed games, football draws, betting pushes or refunded contracts**. Those require a suitable likelihood and settlement target. Likewise a tied neutral-strength tier does not preclude a home/context-adjusted favorite or positive value at an advantageous price.

### Prior precision: a material paper/code ambiguity

Section 3.2.1 Eq. 9 literally specifies **per-partition** mass

`f_G(g) proportional to Poisson(K_g | lambda)`.

This gives equal mass to each partition with the same K, but it is **not** the same as first drawing K from a truncated Poisson and then drawing a uniform partition. There are `S(J,K)` set partitions of size K (Stirling number of second kind); therefore Eq. 9 implies

`P(K=k) proportional to S(J,k) * lambda^k/k!`.

A K-first hierarchy instead gives per-partition mass proportional to `lambda^k / [k! * S(J,k)]`. Calling lambda the literal expected number of clusters is therefore unsafe (even truncated Poisson alone does not have expectation exactly lambda).

**Author code discrepancy:** at pinned SHA below, `R/fit_RCBTL.R` builds `logprior_partition <- dpois(1:J, lambda, log=T)`. `R/sample_partition.R` adds differences of these per-partition log weights in birth/death acceptance, with no Stirling correction. This is consistent with Eq. 9 at the prior-weight level, subject to a full detailed-balance audit. But `R/rRCBTL.R` first samples K with `dpois(1:J,lambda)` and then rejection-samples assignments conditional on all groups being occupied. That generator implements the K-first hierarchy, not Eq. 9. It is not evidence the sampler has the wrong posterior; it is a **generator-versus-target discrepancy** to resolve before prior-predictive validation. The later PR paper's Appendix F also describes the K-first version too casually.

Exact synthetic enumeration with **J=4, lambda=1**:

| K | Eq. 9 marginal K probability | `rRCBTL` generator K probability |
|---|---:|---:|
| 1 | 24/133 | 24/41 |
| 2 | 12/19 | 12/41 |
| 3 | 24/133 | 4/41 |
| 4 | 1/133 | 1/41 |

Conditional uniform partitions also imply combinatorial cluster-size preferences; “no explicit size parameter” does not mean uniform size profiles. The paper recognizes an implicit size/K connection. The later PR paper's Appendix G makes the balanced-size effect explicit.

The Gamma hyperparameters also require sensitivity analysis. Although the BTL likelihood is invariant to common scaling and the common Gamma **rate** cancels from normalized strengths, the **shape** is not scale-only. For two independent singleton strengths `Gamma(a,b)`, their ratio probability has distribution `Beta(a,a)` with variance `1/[4(2a+1)]`: **1/12 for a=1, 1/44 for a=5**. Thus the paper's “generally non-influential” wording for both hyperparameters must not be treated as a theorem.

## Inference and reported evidence

Section 4 alternates reversible-jump split/merge updates of the partition and worths with data-augmentation Gibbs updates of group worths. The trans-dimensional step has acceptance/rejection; calling it only “conjugate Gibbs” omits important machinery. Gamma conjugacy helps worth updates, not all clustering decisions. Likelihood strengths are scale-nonidentifiable; the prior anchors scale, and results normalize object strengths after sampling.

**Simulation, Section 4.2:** J=8, planted K in {1,2,4,8}, successive distinct strengths separated fourfold; I in {50,200,800}; lambda in {0.1,2,4,8}; 20 datasets for each combination; `a_gamma=5,b_gamma=3`; `T1=5000,T2=2`, 10,000 samples per chain, first half discarded. Worth MAE and recovery of co-clustering are the evaluated quantities, not calibration of gambling contracts. The paper recommends prior sensitivity when K is unknown.

**NBA, Section 5.4:** 1,230 games; 30 teams; 82 games per team; 2023–24 regular-season retrospective analysis, `a_gamma=5,b_gamma=3,lambda=1`. The reported **80% posterior credible interval for K is 6–9**, and every team's singleton-cluster posterior probability is **less than 0.037**. These are probabilities/intervals about partition structure, not team win probabilities. The four strongest teams appear co-clustered; the six bottom teams have substantial clustering uncertainty. The authors describe postseason prediction as a **future** use. No held-out NBA Brier/ECE or betting ROI is reported. Appendix A's trace plots support the authors' convergence claim; this audit did not rerun those chains or calculate ESS/R-hat from samples.

The paper also uses complete sushi rankings, top-three mayoral ballots, and partially completed policy rankings, demonstrating why correct choice-set handling matters. It notes preference heterogeneity simplification. A minor source inconsistency exists in Section 5.3: the displayed survey lists information campaigns as option 1, but the interpretation paragraph calls it option 2; verify original labels before reproducing that application.

## Executed synthetic falsifier

`sources/rankreview-counterexamples.py` (output JSON adjacent) passes all assertions. These are **synthetic mathematical constructions, not fitted paper outputs or empirical edge evidence**.

- Posterior A puts mass 9/10 on BT event probability 11/20 and mass 1/10 on 9/20. Posterior B puts the same masses on 9/10 and 1/10. Both have latent rank-superiority probability **9/10**, but predictive probabilities are **27/50 versus 41/50**.
- A mixture placing **4/5** probability on equal strengths and **1/5** on BT probability 9/10 predicts **29/50**. Thus high co-clustering mass does not logically forbid a 58% model predictive; its empirical calibration remains a separate question.
- Certain latent superiority with event probability **51/100** shows epistemic rank certainty is not outcome certainty.
- Prior enumeration, Gamma-ratio sensitivity and choice-set likelihood checks above run in the same script.

These examples refute equating rank posterior with event probability, not the usefulness of rank-clustering. Posterior predictive probabilities can be derived from the model, but calibration is not guaranteed when the likelihood, stationarity or choice-set assumptions fail.
