## Correct core method

The empirical object is **economic forecasts**, not bookmaker prices. The authors analyze the Philadelphia Fed Survey of Professional Forecasters, chiefly nominal GDP predictions for current, two-quarters-ahead and four-quarters-ahead horizons, covering 1968Q4–2019Q4. These provide **205 short-, 203 medium-, 196 long-range** distributions, averaging about **37 forecasters**. They also pool **8,650 experiments over ten indicators and five horizons**.

The crowd estimate is the **arithmetic mean** of individual numerical estimates. They test three explanations: Page's diversity-prediction identity, the psychophysical augmented-quincunx model, and the unbiased-estimator assumption. For true value G and estimates g_i, define crowd error gamma=G−mean(g), mean squared individual error epsilon, and estimate variance delta. The identity **gamma² = epsilon − delta** is retained, not refuted. It guarantees mean-estimate squared error does not exceed mean individual squared error; it does **not** guarantee beating every individual, the median individual, or a preselected expert.

They measure Spearman associations of diversity/skewness with crowd error and the fraction of individuals outperforming the crowd. Appendix A compares the real forecasts with virtual unbiased Gaussian forecasters using corresponding experiment sizes/dispersion; Appendix B checks four student guessing experiments. This is not a betting simulation, CLV estimator, or a causal intervention on forecast dependence.

## Exact reported results

- **Figure 6 / section IV:** across **8,650** real experiments, the crowd beats **every individual in 1.7%** and **most individuals in 66.8%**. Thus the paper reports a **moderate advantage**, not zero aggregation benefit.
- **Figure 5:** for nominal-GDP horizons, the mean beats a majority in **150/205 (~0.73)** short-range, **145/203 (~0.71)** medium-range and **130/196 (~0.66)** long-range experiments. One short-range experiment has **85%** of individuals beating the crowd; the text identifies **39 participants with four large outliers**.
- **Figure 2:** Spearman correlation between relative dispersion and relative absolute collective error is **rho=0.31, p<10^-6** short-range; **0.25, p<10^-6** medium-range; **0.14, p=0.05** long-range. More dispersion was not associated with lower error in these data.
- **Figure 4:** skewness does not reliably predict signed collective error: **rho=0.008, p=0.91**; **rho=−0.03, p=0.62**; **rho=−0.12, p=0.09**, respectively. These are observational associations, not an impossibility theorem for aggregation.
- **Appendix A / Figure A4:** virtual unbiased crowds beat all individuals in **16.3%** and most in **99.8%** of experiments. The unbiased-null test yields **p<0.05 in 85%** of real experiments.
- **Appendix B:** candies **N=105, truth=636, crowd=531**, crowd better than **70%**, unbiased-null **p<10^-6**; paper strip **N=139, truth=22.4 cm, crowd=22.0 cm**, better than **85%**, **p=0.14**. These contrasting cases demonstrate that successful aggregation can occur; the authors argue against selective promotion of such successes as universal.

## Dossier contradictions and unsupported transfers

**Ranked by Relevance row 53 (High):** unlike the reconciled dossier, this row correctly identifies Federal Reserve survey data and offers an ensemble audit rather than claiming sports-market inefficiency. A useful hypothesis, but this audit downgrades relevance to 4/10 because the transfer to probabilistic betting ensembles is indirect and identical-row comparisons already exist.

Compared with `live-export.json`, **Grok 58 Dossiers row 53**, and **Build Plan row 27**:

1. **“Often the crowd is just a worse bookmaker” — unsupported/domain substitution.** No bookmaker is studied. The units are economic point forecasts and layperson quantity estimates, not probabilistic sports prices.
2. **“Aggregation gains disappear under realistic dependence and selection” — overstated and contradicted by the reported moderate benefit.** The crowd beats most individuals in 66.8% of the pooled experiments. The identity guaranteeing improvement relative to mean individual squared error still holds. The paper critiques selective attention and biased forecasts; it does not identify dependence as a causal mechanism eliminating all gains.
3. **“Challenges CLV benchmark / market-efficiency assumption” — unsupported.** No CLV, opening/closing odds, Brier score, bookmaker margin, or realized betting return is evaluated. This paper cannot be used as a second independent sports-market-inefficiency finding beside 1710.02824.
4. **“Twitter/Reddit consensus more likely injects noise; market close is the crowd that matters” — hypotheses/policy preferences, not paper results.** Avoid adding such feeds without a held-out ablation, but do not attribute that conclusion to this text.
5. **Bad repository URL** is confirmed, with the valid source-derived alternative above.

The defensible lesson is: do not confuse a mean-square identity with universal superiority or assume an ensemble's diversity proves useful skill. Compare forecasts on identical observations with proper held-out evaluation.

- `apps/web/lib/calibration/identical-row-bakeoff.ts:1–50,83–99`: aligns all compared scores on the same settled two-way-moneyline population and applies paired bootstrap indices to **Brier gap versus market**. It specifically avoids comparing different score-availability cohorts. This is stronger task-specific prior art than a generic new “crowd benchmark.”
- `bootstrap-calib-ci.ts`, `bootstrap-metric-ci.ts`, `stationary-bootstrap.ts`: existing map/metric uncertainty primitives; only the last is the stationary-bootstrap implementation. No need to build generic bootstrap anew.
- `aci-state.ts` / `aci-durable.ts`: flag-isolated show/abstain and persistence; not evidence that a consensus signal adds skill or that model diversity reduces error.
