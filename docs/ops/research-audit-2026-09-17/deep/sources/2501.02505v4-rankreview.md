###### Abstract

Ranking items based on pairwise comparisons is common, from using match outcomes to rank sports teams to using purchase or survey data to rank consumer products. Statistical inference-based methods such as the Bradley-Terry model, which extract rankings based on an underlying generative model, have emerged as flexible and powerful tools to tackle ranking in empirical data. In situations with limited and/or noisy comparisons, it is often challenging to confidently distinguish performance of different items based on evidence available in the data. However, most inference-based ranking methods choose to assign each item to a unique rank or score, suggesting a meaningful distinction when there is none. Here, we develop a principled nonparametric Bayesian method, adaptable to any statistical ranking method, for learning partial rankings (rankings with ties) that distinguishes among the ranks of different items only when there is sufficient evidence available in the data. We develop a fast agglomerative algorithm to perform Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) inference of partial rankings under our framework and examine the performance of our method on a variety of real and synthetic network datasets, finding that it frequently gives a more parsimonious summary of the data than traditional ranking, particularly when observations are sparse.

## I Introduction

In a broad range of applications it can be useful to rank a set of items or players according to some predetermined notion of importance or strength. For example, rankings of players or teams, such as FIFA rankings in soccer or Elo ratings in chess, are used to determine match pairings and tournament seedings. Search engines rank web pages so as to deliver the most relevant results to users. In market analytics, products are often ranked based on sales, reviews, and customer satisfaction. In academia, research funding is often allocated by ranking grant applications, while in finance, credit scoring ranks individuals by creditworthiness, significantly influencing their access to loans, mortgages, and other financial services.

Given the prominence of rankings across domains, it has long been of interest to develop algorithms that automatically rank items based on some set of data about the items and their associations \[ [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib1 ""), [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib2 ""), [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib3 ""), [4](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib4 ""), [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib5 "")\]. An extensively studied subset of ranking problems is that of ranking from _pairwise comparisons_\[ [6](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib6 ""), [7](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib7 ""), [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib5 ""), [8](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib8 ""), [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib1 ""), [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib9 "")\], where rankings are inferred from comparisons among pairs of entities. In this approach, the outcome of a single interaction between two items—for example, a win or loss in sports or one product being preferred over another—serves as the basis for the ranking. This approach allows one to infer rankings in situations where underlying ratings of the entities are unavailable, unobserved, or difficult to compute. For example, instead of trying to assess the absolute skill of individual tennis players, we can infer their relative rankings from their head-to-head match results, assuming that matches are more often won by the better player.

Since pairwise comparisons inherently involve some degree of uncertainty due to
randomness, noise, or incomplete information in the observed outcomes, pairwise
ranking models are generally formulated as probabilistic models whose
parameters can then be estimated, for example, via Maximum Likelihood
Estimation (MLE) or Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) inference \[ [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib1 ""), [10](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib10 ""), [11](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib11 ""), [12](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib12 ""), [13](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib13 ""), [14](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib14 "")\]. It is also useful to model pairwise comparison data as a network in which items are nodes and each comparison is an edge between its participating nodes, pointing from the winner of the corresponding match/preferred item to the loser of the match/non-preferred item. Multiple edges may exist between a pair of nodes if multiple comparisons were performed, each edge providing evidence for the relative ranking of the two items on its endpoints. Therefore, we will use the network-centric terminology “nodes” interchangeably with the entities being ranked (e.g. players, teams, etc) and “edges” interchangeably with the pairwise comparisons used for ranking (e.g. matches, hiring flows, etc).

In the classic Bradley-Terry (BT) model \[ [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib1 "")\], the outcome of a comparison between two nodes is modeled as a Bernoulli random variable with a probability that depends on the latent scores associated with the items. After inference with the model, one obtains a set of continuous scores that can be ordered to obtain a final ranking. The BT model has inspired numerous extensions \[ [15](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib15 ""), [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib9 "")\] to more flexibly model variations in match outcomes, including the possibility of ties in the match outcomes \[ [16](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib16 ""), [17](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib17 ""), [18](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib18 ""), [19](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib19 "")\] (rather than ties in the final _rankings_ as we study here). Another popular inference-based method for ranking from pairwise comparisons is SpringRank \[ [12](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib12 "")\], which ranks nodes by finding the ground state of a physical system which models the comparison network as a set of directed springs and has also inspired numerous generalizations \[ [20](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib20 ""), [21](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib21 "")\]. These methods can parsimoniously model heterogeneity and noise in match outcomes but do not allow for the possibility of ties in the inferred rankings and, as such, are unable to determine when there is enough structure in the comparison data to justify distinguishing the rankings of different items. (In principle, two nodes can have identical scores under such methods, but this is rarely ever achieved in practice numerically.) Such methods can therefore infer rankings that overfit the data when limited observations are available, resulting in rankings that are highly sensitive to small changes in outcomes. Several methods have been proposed to reduce this overfitting and promote ties in the inferred rankings (see Sec. [II.2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.SS2 "II.2 Partial Rankings ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")). However, these models often require extensive parameter tuning, a priori knowledge about the number and size of the groups being inferred, or incorporate information other than the outcomes of the pairwise comparisons, making them difficult to adapt to a broad range of existing ranking methods.

In this work, we introduce a statistical inference-based ranking framework that intrinsically incorporates partial rankings---rankings where multiple nodes can have the same rank---into its underlying generative model.
111



In the context of ranking algorithms, the term _partial ranking_ can generally refer either to rankings where only a subset of the items are fully ordered \[ [23](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib23 ""), [22](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib22 "")\], or where some of the items are tied in ranking \[ [25](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib25 "")\]. In this work, we explicitly refer to partial rankings as rankings with ties.
This is accomplished by introducing a hierarchy of uniform Bayesian priors for the underlying rankings of the nodes, which encourages parsimony in the final scores or ranks inferred using the model. This allows us to account for the uncertainty introduced by sparsity in the data by grouping players into the same rank when the data does not provide enough evidence to separate them. Furthermore, our method can be extended to accommodate any inference-based ranking method that models comparison outcomes as a function of the underlying scores or ranks of the nodes involved  \[ [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib1 ""), [12](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib12 ""), [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib9 "")\]. We provide a fast nonparametric agglomerative algorithm to infer the partial rankings according to our method, and by fitting our model to a wide range of synthetic and real-world datasets, we find that it often provides a more parsimonious description of the data in the regime of sparse observations. As a case study, we apply our method to a faculty hiring network among Computer Science departments at U.S. universities \[ [26](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib26 "")\]. The picture that emerges is that of a well-separated hierarchy dominated by a small group of elite universities whose rankings are not meaningfully different statistically, with very little upward mobility across the ranking groups.

## II Methods

### II.1 Bayesian Ranking and the Bradley-Terry Model

For illustrative purposes, it will be convenient to discuss ranking in the context of matches among individuals—for example, tennis matches among tennis players. But all upcoming discussion can be equivalently framed in the context of comparing two items, in which case each match is a comparison (e.g. from a respondent in a marketing survey or a consumer purchasing one of multiple products), and the winner of the match is the preferred item. All such entities can be captured by the nodes in a network of the pairwise comparisons.

Let 𝑾\\bm{W} be an N×NN\\times N matrix of match outcomes for a set of NN competing individuals (nodes) such that wi​jw\_{ij} is the number of matches in which node ii beat node jj. For simplicity, we will assume that ties in the matches are not allowed. The problem we want to solve is that of inferring a ranking 𝒓=\[r1,…,rN\]\\bm{r}=\[r\_{1},...,r\_{N}\] of the NN individuals such that ri<rjr\_{i}<r\_{j} indicates a higher probability of ii beating jj than jj beating ii. Given the inherently probabilistic nature of match outcomes, a common approach is to assign a latent real-valued score si∈ℝs\_{i}\\in\\mathbb{R} to each player such that the probability pi​jp\_{ij} that player ii beats player jj is given by some function of the difference of their scores: pi​j=f⁡(si−sj)p\_{ij}=f(s\_{i}-s\_{j}). For f⁡(s)f(s) to be a suitable scoring function, it must satisfy a set of constraints \[ [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib9 "")\]: (1) It must be a monotonically increasing function of ss, ensuring that stronger players are assigned a higher probability of winning; (2) It must be bounded within \[0,1\]\[0,1\] as it must represent a valid probability; (3) It must be antisymmetric around zero, satisfying f⁡(−s)=1−f⁡(s)f(-s)=1-f(s) to ensure that the probability of losing is one minus the probability of winning. While these requirements do not uniquely determine f⁡(s)f(s), a commonly adopted choice is the logistic function, f⁡(s)=1/(1+e−s)f(s)=1/(1+e^{-s}), which gives

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | pi​j=esiesi+esj.p\_{ij}=\\frac{e^{s\_{i}}}{e^{s\_{i}}+e^{s\_{j}}}. |  | (1) |

For convenience, one generally introduces the (non-negative) quantities πi=esi\\pi\_{i}=e^{s\_{i}}, which, keeping with previous literature, we shall call the player _strengths_\[ [27](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib27 ""), [28](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib28 "")\]. Eq. ( [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.E1 "In II.1 Bayesian Ranking and the Bradley-Terry Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) can then be written as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | pi​j=πiπi+πj.p\_{ij}=\\frac{\\pi\_{i}}{\\pi\_{i}+\\pi\_{j}}. |  | (2) |

This win probability is the basis of the well known Bradley-Terry (BT) model, first introduced by Zermelo \[ [27](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib27 "")\] and then, independently, by Bradley and Terry \[ [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib1 "")\].

Given the matrix 𝑾\\bm{W} and assuming all match outcomes are independent of each other, the likelihood of observing 𝑾\\bm{W} is given by

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | P⁡(𝑾\|𝝅)=∏i​j(πiπi+πj)wi​j.P(\\bm{W}\|\\bm{\\pi})=\\prod\_{ij}\\left(\\frac{\\pi\_{i}}{\\pi\_{i}+\\pi\_{j}}\\right)^{w\_{ij}}. |  | (3) |

Differentiating the log-likelihood (i.e. the logarithm of Eq. ( [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.E3 "In II.1 Bayesian Ranking and the Bradley-Terry Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"))) with respect to the πi\\pi\_{i} and equating the result to zero, we obtain the following set of self-consistent equations:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | πi=∑j=1Nwi​j∑j=1N(wi​j+wj​i)/(πi+πj),\\pi\_{i}=\\frac{\\sum\_{j=1}^{N}w\_{ij}}{\\sum\_{j=1}^{N}(w\_{ij}+w\_{ji})/(\\pi\_{i}+\\pi\_{j})}, |  | (4) |

which can be solved by iteration to obtain the MLE estimates of the player scores. Then, these scores can be converted to an implied ranking 𝒓\\bm{r} by simply taking the ordering of the nodes with respect to the inferred strengths 𝝅\\bm{\\pi}\[ [27](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib27 ""), [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib1 ""), [29](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib29 ""), [28](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib28 "")\]. This iterative procedure is known as Zermelo’s algorithm \[ [27](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib27 "")\]. More generally, Zermelo’s algorithm has been shown to belong to a larger family of iterative algorithms of the form

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | πi=∑j=1Nwi​j​(α​πi+πj)/(πi+πj)∑j=1N(α​wi​j+wj​i)/(πi+πj),\\pi\_{i}=\\frac{\\sum\_{j=1}^{N}w\_{ij}(\\alpha\\pi\_{i}+\\pi\_{j})/(\\pi\_{i}+\\pi\_{j})}{\\sum\_{j=1}^{N}(\\alpha w\_{ij}+w\_{ji})/(\\pi\_{i}+\\pi\_{j})}, |  | (5) |

which have all been proven to converge to the same MLE solution (if one exists) for 0≤α≤10\\leq\\alpha\\leq 1. Furthermore, it has been shown that the convergence of this family of algorithms becomes monotonically slower with increasing α\\alpha, with α=0\\alpha=0 converging fastest and Zermelo’s algorithm (corresponding to the α=1\\alpha=1 case) being the slowest to converge. Eq. ( [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.E5 "In II.1 Bayesian Ranking and the Bradley-Terry Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) was first derived in \[ [28](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib28 "")\], and we use its α=0\\alpha=0 realization throughout this work.

The MLE approach described above presents a series of drawbacks. First, we note that Eq. ( [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.E2 "In II.1 Bayesian Ranking and the Bradley-Terry Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")), and therefore Eq. ( [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.E3 "In II.1 Bayesian Ranking and the Bradley-Terry Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")), is invariant under a constant rescaling of all player strengths, meaning that player scores are not uniquely identifiable. To resolve this ambiguity, one generally imposes a suitable normalization. A common choice is to fix the average player score to zero

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | ⟨s⟩=1N​∑i=1Nsi=0.\\langle s\\rangle=\\frac{1}{N}\\sum\_{i=1}^{N}s\_{i}=0. |  | (6) |

This choice centers the log-strengths around zero, making them interpretable as relative to an average player. In turn, this ensures that the strength of the average player is given by ⟨π⟩=1\\langle\\pi\\rangle=1, so that a player with πi>1\\pi\_{i}>1 is stronger than average, and one with πi<1\\pi\_{i}<1 is weaker than average. This normalisation choice also has the advantage of providing an intuitive interpretation of the player’s strengths. Following \[ [28](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib28 "")\], let p1p\_{1} be the probability that a player with strength π\\pi has of beating the average player of strength one. Then, according to the BT model, p1p\_{1} is given by p1=π/(π+1)p\_{1}=\\pi/(\\pi+1), which in turn implies that π=p1/(1−p1)\\pi=p\_{1}/(1-p\_{1}). A player’s strength is then simply the odds that the player has of beating the average player.

More problematic is the fact that Eq. ( [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.E5 "In II.1 Bayesian Ranking and the Bradley-Terry Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"))—and all MLE methods for that matter—have been shown to converge only under the stringent condition that the underlying network of matches is strongly connected, i.e. there exists a directed path between any pair of nodes in the network \[ [27](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib27 ""), [29](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib29 ""), [30](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib30 "")\]. If this is not the case, then no maximum of the likelihood function exists, and the scores in Eq. ( [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.E5 "In II.1 Bayesian Ranking and the Bradley-Terry Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) diverge.

Both of these issues can be addressed by adopting a Bayesian perspective and imposing a prior P⁡(𝝅)P(\\bm{\\pi}) on the strengths 𝝅\\bm{\\pi} so that a full posterior distribution over 𝝅\\bm{\\pi} can be inferred \[ [6](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib6 ""), [28](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib28 ""), [7](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib7 ""), [31](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib31 "")\]. A common choice for this prior is to assume a uniform distribution over the probability p1p\_{1} that a player with strength π\\pi defeats the average player, P⁡(p1)=1P(p\_{1})=1. By probability density transformation rules, this is equivalent to setting a logistic prior on the scores si=log⁡πis\_{i}=\\log\\pi\_{i}. Indeed,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | P⁡(s)=P⁡(p1)⋅d​p1d​s=d​p1d​π​d​πd​s=π(π+1)2=es(1+es)2,P(s)=P(p\_{1})\\cdot\\frac{dp\_{1}}{ds}=\\frac{dp\_{1}}{d\\pi}\\frac{d\\pi}{ds}=\\frac{\\pi}{(\\pi+1)^{2}}=\\frac{e^{s}}{(1+e^{s})^{2}}, |  | (7) |

which is a logistic distribution with mean zero and scale one. If we assume the scores of all players to be independently distributed, we have the following prior on the player strengths

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | P⁡(𝝅)=∏i=1Nπi(πi+1)2.P(\\bm{\\pi})=\\prod\_{i=1}^{N}\\frac{\\pi\_{i}}{(\\pi\_{i}+1)^{2}}. |  | (8) |

The resulting posterior probability for the scores is then given (up to a multiplicative constant) by

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | P⁡(𝝅\|𝑾)∝∏i,j=1N(πiπi+πj)wi​j​∏i=1Nπi(πi+1)2.P(\\bm{\\pi}\|\\bm{W})\\propto\\prod\_{i,j=1}^{N}\\left(\\frac{\\pi\_{i}}{\\pi\_{i}+\\pi\_{j}}\\right)^{w\_{ij}}\\prod\_{i=1}^{N}\\frac{\\pi\_{i}}{(\\pi\_{i}+1)^{2}}. |  | (9) |

Eq. ( [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.E9 "In II.1 Bayesian Ranking and the Bradley-Terry Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) removes the invariance under a rescaling of the scores. Furthermore, it also prevents the scores from diverging. As pointed out in \[ [6](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib6 "")\], the prior for an individual ii can be written as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | πi(πi+1)2=πiπi+1⋅1πi+1,\\frac{\\pi\_{i}}{(\\pi\_{i}+1)^{2}}=\\frac{\\pi\_{i}}{\\pi\_{i}+1}\\cdot\\frac{1}{\\pi\_{i}+1}, |  | (10) |

which corresponds to the probability that player ii plays two matches against the average player, winning one and losing the other. Eq. ( [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.E9 "In II.1 Bayesian Ranking and the Bradley-Terry Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) can then be seen as the likelihood of a BT model where two pseudo-games have been added for each player, one won and one lost. The resulting network of interactions is now strongly connected, ensuring that the maximum of the posterior always exists, irrespective of whether the original interaction network was fully connected or not.

For this Bayesian model, Maximum A Posteriori (MAP) estimates for 𝝅\\bm{\\pi} can be inferred by solving the following optimisation problem:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | 𝝅^=arg​max𝝅⁡P​(𝝅\|𝐖)=arg​max𝝅⁡P​(𝐖\|𝝅)​P​(𝝅).\\bm{\\hat{\\pi}}=\\argmax\_{\\bm{\\pi}}P(\\bm{\\pi}\|\\bm{W})=\\argmax\_{\\bm{\\pi}}P(\\bm{W}\|\\bm{\\pi})P(\\bm{\\pi}). |  | (11) |

Again, differentiating with respect to the πi\\pi\_{i} and equating the result to zero, we obtain the following set of self-consistent equations for the MAP estimates of the strengths:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | π^i=1+∑j≠iwi​j​πj/(πi+πj)2/(πi+1)+∑j≠iwj​i/(πi+πj),\\hat{\\pi}\_{i}=\\frac{1+\\sum\_{j\\neq i}w\_{ij}\\pi\_{j}/(\\pi\_{i}+\\pi\_{j})}{2/(\\pi\_{i}+1)+\\sum\_{j\\neq i}w\_{ji}/(\\pi\_{i}+\\pi\_{j})}, |  | (12) |

which is the generalization of Eq. ( [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.E5 "In II.1 Bayesian Ranking and the Bradley-Terry Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) (with α=0\\alpha=0) to the MAP setting and can be solved in the same way as for the MLEs. The primary advantage of Eq. ( [12](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.E12 "In II.1 Bayesian Ranking and the Bradley-Terry Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) over the MLE updates is that the iterative updates described by Eq. ( [12](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.E12 "In II.1 Bayesian Ranking and the Bradley-Terry Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) are guaranteed to converge, and do so quite efficiently in practice \[ [28](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib28 "")\]. The inferred strengths are then converted into an implied ranking 𝒓\\bm{r} by ordering the nodes with respect to the inferred strengths 𝝅^\\bm{\\hat{\\pi}}.

### II.2 Partial Rankings

As discussed in Sec. [I](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S1 "I Introduction ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"), a limitation of standard MAP estimation for the BT model is that it lacks a mechanism to handle partial rankings. In principle, one could apply a heuristic to assign the same rank to nodes ii and jj when their scores satisfy \|si−sj\|<ϵ\|s\_{i}-s\_{j}\|<\\epsilon for some small ϵ\\epsilon. However, this approach does not offer any statistical justification for grouping ii and jj together (i.e., whether the observed score difference is simply due to statistical noise). Alternatively, one could apply 1D numerical clustering to the final score values, but this approach would not account for the significance of score differences in the context of the model’s likelihood or prior. More principled approaches have been developed for 1D numerical clustering of the scores by applying L​1L1 regularization to the adjacent score differences \[ [32](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib32 "")\], analogous to the fused lasso \[ [33](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib33 "")\]. This _ranking lasso_ has been extended in a number of different ways to increase its flexibility for different applications \[ [34](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib34 ""), [35](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib35 ""), [32](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib32 ""), [36](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib36 ""), [37](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib37 ""), [38](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib38 ""), [36](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib36 ""), [39](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib39 ""), [36](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib36 ""), [40](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib40 "")\].

Despite their widespread application, regularized ranking models suffer from significant drawbacks. The most obvious is the need to tune the lasso penalty parameter, which requires us to fix the level of sparsity a-priori, forcing the groupings in a predefined way and complicating the interpretation of the inferred parameters. Furthermore, as mentioned previously, maximum likelihood estimates for the Bradley-Terry model (and general Bradley-Terry-Luce family of models) without a score prior can be difficult and computationally expensive to perform, although considerable work has been dedicated to developing efficient algorithms \[ [30](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib30 ""), [41](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib41 ""), [42](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib42 ""), [43](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib43 ""), [28](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib28 "")\]. Also, as pointed out in \[ [23](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib23 "")\], uncertainty quantification can be hard to perform in lasso-based methods \[ [34](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib34 "")\], and prior knowledge on the number or size of the rank groups cannot be incorporated.

Many of these issues can be resolved by resorting to Bayesian approaches. However, these approaches generally require Monte Carlo estimation, which can be slow for estimation \[ [7](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib7 ""), [31](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib31 "")\]. In \[ [23](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib23 "")\], Pearce and Erosheva propose the Rank-Clustered Bradley-Terry-Luce (RC-BTL) model, a Bayesian method based on the use of a spike-and-slab prior \[ [44](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib44 ""), [45](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib45 ""), [46](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib46 "")\] to induce parameter fusion. Unlike other methodologies, Pearce and Erosheva’s model requires neither the specification of the number or sizes of the rank cluster nor the tuning of lasso-type penalties, while exploiting conjugacy relationships for estimation of the full posterior.
However, this model still requires the selection and tuning of a series of hyperparameters, among which is setting the expected number of rank clusters. Although this choice can be advantageous, as it allows the user to tune the desired level of rank clustering, poor choices of this parameter could potentially introduce unwanted biases in the inferred results.

An alternative approach is to formulate fully nonparametric models, which do not require any hyperparameter tuning. In the ordered Stochastic Block Model (OSBM) \[ [13](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib13 "")\], Peixoto proposes a nonparametric model based on a modification of the Stochastic Block Model \[ [47](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib47 ""), [48](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib48 "")\] in which nodes are grouped according to both the mixing structure of the network and hierarchies among the nodes as evidenced by edge directionality. Although the OSBM does allow nodes to be grouped in part by the edge hierarchy, it is heavily influenced by the mixing structure of the nodes, which largely depends on the positions of the edges rather than their directionality (the latter aspect being of primary interest for pairwise ranking). In contrast, the method we propose here focuses only on the directionality of the observed edges and so is adaptable to a broad class of ranking methods, including most popular generalizations of the BT model \[ [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib9 "")\].

### II.3 Bayesian Nonparametric Model

A principled approach to handling partial rankings is to extend the Bayesian hierarchy for the prior on the strengths P⁡(𝝅)P(\\bm{\\pi}) by making the strength of each node a function of its underlying ranking 𝒓\\bm{r}. This results in the following new MAP objective:

|     |     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | 𝒓^,𝝅^\\displaystyle\\bm{\\hat{r}},\\bm{\\hat{\\pi}} | =arg​max𝐫,𝝅⁡P​(𝐫,𝝅\|𝐖)\\displaystyle=\\argmax\_{\\bm{r},\\bm{\\pi}}P(\\bm{r},\\bm{\\pi}\|\\bm{W}) |  |
|  |  | =arg​max𝐫,𝝅⁡P​(𝐖\|𝝅)​P​(𝝅\|𝐫)​P​(𝐫),\\displaystyle=\\argmax\_{\\bm{r},\\bm{\\pi}}P(\\bm{W}\|\\bm{\\pi})P(\\bm{\\pi}\|\\bm{r})P(\\bm{r}), |  | (13) |

where 𝒓^\\bm{\\hat{r}} represents the optimal partial ranking to be estimated from the observed data and 𝝅^\\bm{\\hat{\\pi}} is the optimal set of scores, as defined previously.

Given Eq. ( [13](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.Ex1 "In II.3 Bayesian Nonparametric Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")), we now need to define the functional forms of the likelihood and priors. The easiest choice is to use the BT likelihood of Eq. ( [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.E3 "In II.1 Bayesian Ranking and the Bradley-Terry Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")), although any choice of likelihood is possible to incorporate into this MAP estimation objective so long as it is a function of latent strengths—i.e., P⁡(𝑾\|𝝅)P(\\bm{W}\|\\bm{\\pi})—or ranks—i.e., P⁡(𝑾\|𝒓)P(\\bm{W}\|\\bm{r}) (the latter allowing us to remove the prior P⁡(𝝅\|𝒓)P(\\bm{\\pi}\|\\bm{r})). This flexibility allows for the inclusion of a wide range of ranking objectives, including extensions of BT models \[ [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib9 "")\] and SpringRank \[ [12](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib12 "")\]. For our experiments in Sec. [III](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3 "III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"), we will proceed with using the BT likelihood of Eq. ( [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.E3 "In II.1 Bayesian Ranking and the Bradley-Terry Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) for subsequent analysis, which facilitates an efficient optimization scheme and a simple comparison with the original BT model.

The generative process described above, and the associated objective, are similar to those of the RC-BTL model introduced by Pearce and Erosheva \[ [23](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib23 "")\], which places a Gamma prior on the unique cluster strengths. The main difference between our model and RC-BTL lies in the prior over partitions. In our Partial Ranking (PR) formulation, players are assigned to groups via a hierarchy of uniform priors over the number, sizes, and compositions of the clusters. By contrast, RC-BTL employs a Poisson prior on the number of clusters and assigns equal probability to all partitions with the same RR, regardless of cluster sizes. As a result, PR and RC-BTL encode different structural biases over the space of partitions. In RC-BTL, the indifference with respect to cluster sizes simplifies posterior sampling, since all partitions with the same number of groups are equally likely, but it also implicitly favors balanced groups: if partitions are drawn uniformly conditional on RR, the overwhelming majority will allocate players approximately equally across groups \[ [48](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib48 ""), [78](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib78 "")\]. Depending on the amount of available data and prior knowledge about the items being ranked, this may introduce artificial structure into the inferred rankings. In contrast, the PR formulation explicitly samples the group sizes as part of the prior hierarchy, remaining agnostic about balance until the data are observed. For a more detailed comparison between the PR and RC-BTL models see Appendix [F](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A6 "Appendix F Comparison with the RC-BTL model ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons").

By minimizing Eq. ( [22](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.Ex4 "In II.3 Bayesian Nonparametric Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")), we can determine the optimal ranks 𝒓^\\bm{\\hat{r}} and the corresponding optimal strengths 𝝈^={σ^r^i}i=1N\\bm{\\hat{\\sigma}}=\\{\\hat{\\sigma}\_{\\hat{r}\_{i}}\\}\_{i=1}^{N} that best fit the observed data. It is important to emphasize that, although this work focuses primarily on MAP estimates of the strengths and rankings, Eq. ( [22](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.Ex4 "In II.3 Bayesian Nonparametric Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) defines a well-posed (negative) log-posterior distribution. This posterior can be explored using sampling techniques such as Markov Chain Monte Carlo (MCMC), enabling estimation of the full posterior distribution and associated uncertainty quantification rather than relying solely on point estimates.

We can see that the first term in Eq. ( [22](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.Ex4 "In II.3 Bayesian Nonparametric Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) is a constant independent of the underlying node ranking. The second and third terms synergistically penalize having a large number of rankings as they will increase ℒ⁡(𝒓,𝝈)\\mathcal{L}(\\bm{r},\\bm{\\sigma}). Indeed, it is easy to prove that the sum of these two terms

has a global minimum when R=1R=1, see Appendix [A](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A1 "Appendix A Minimization of 𝑓(𝑅) ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"). The final two terms are harder to interpret, as they will depend on the particular realization of the matches. However, empirically, we observe that the fourth term will also penalize having a large number of rankings, while the last term will tend to favor them.

An advantage of working with a Bayesian framework is that it provides us with a principled way to compare results obtained by different models. Suppose we have two different ranking models, which we call ℋ1\\mathcal{H}\_{1} and ℋ2\\mathcal{H}\_{2}, that correspond to two different hypotheses for the generating mechanism that produced some observed network of outcomes 𝑾\\bm{W}. Let 𝒓1\\bm{r}\_{1} and 𝒓2\\bm{r}\_{2} be the corresponding most likely rankings obtained by maximizing their respective posterior distributions. If E⁡(𝒓)E(\\bm{r}) is the number of non-zero entries in the matrix 𝝎\\bm{\\omega} when the ranks are equal to 𝒓\\bm{r}, then the complexity of this algorithm is O⁡(E⁡(𝒓))O(E(\\bm{r})). These iterative updates for the player’s strengths are of the same form as those introduced in Eq. ( [12](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.E12 "In II.1 Bayesian Ranking and the Bradley-Terry Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) and benefit from the same convergence guarantees.

To update 𝒓\\bm{r} given 𝝈\\bm{\\sigma}, we can greedily identify the pair of consecutive ranks r<r′r<r^{\\prime} that produces the largest decrease Δ​ℒ​(r,r′)\\Delta\\mathcal{L}(r,r^{\\prime}) in the negative log-posterior when the two ranks are merged. To do this, it is useful to rewrite the negative log-posterior as follows:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | ℒ⁡(𝒓)=C⁡(R)+∑r=1Rg⁡(r)+∑r,r′=1Rf⁡(r,r′)\\mathcal{L}(\\bm{r})=C(R)+\\sum\_{r=1}^{R}g(r)+\\sum\_{r,r^{\\prime}=1}^{R}f(r,r^{\\prime}) |  | (29) |

where

|     |     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  |  | C⁡(R)=log⁡N+log⁡(N−1R−1)+log⁡N!,\\displaystyle C(R)=\\log N+\\log\\binom{N-1}{R-1}+\\log N!, |  | (30) |
|  |  | g(r)=−∑rlognr!+∑rlog\[(σr+1)2σr\],\\displaystyle g(r)=-\\sum\_{r}\\log n\_{r}!+\\sum\_{r}\\log\\left\[\\frac{(\\sigma\_{r}+1)^{2}}{\\sigma\_{r}}\\right\], |  | (31) |
|  |  | f⁡(r,r′)=∑r,r′ωr,r′​log⁡\[σr+σr′σr\]\\displaystyle f(r,r^{\\prime})=\\sum\_{r,r^{\\prime}}\\omega\_{r,r^{\\prime}}\\log\\left\[\\frac{\\sigma\_{r}+\\sigma\_{r^{\\prime}}}{\\sigma\_{r}}\\right\] |  | (32) |

and we have dropped the dependence on the strength parameters as we consider them fixed. C⁡(R)C(R) accounts for the contribution to the negative log-posterior given by the number of rankings RR, g⁡(r)g(r) describes contributions from group sizes and strengths, and f⁡(r,r′)f(r,r^{\\prime}) captures the interaction terms between the rank groups based on the comparison matrix 𝝎\\bm{\\omega} among the rank groups.

Let RR represent the current number of ranks, and let 𝒏\\bm{n} denote the sizes of the rank groups before the merge. Then the change in the negative log-posterior resulting from merging rr and r′r^{\\prime} is given by:

|     |     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  |  | Δ​ℒ​(r,r′)=C⁡(R−1)−C⁡(R)+g⁡((r,r′))−g⁡(r)−g⁡(r′)\\displaystyle\\Delta\\mathcal{L}(r,r^{\\prime})=C(R-1)-C(R)+g((r,r^{\\prime}))-g(r)-g(r^{\\prime}) |  |
|  |  | +f⁡((r,r′),(r,r′))−f⁡(r,r)−f⁡(r′,r′)−f⁡(r,r′)−f⁡(r′,r)\\displaystyle+f((r,r^{\\prime}),(r,r^{\\prime}))-f(r,r)-f(r^{\\prime},r^{\\prime})-f(r,r^{\\prime})-f(r^{\\prime},r) |  |
|  |  | +∑r′′≠r,r′\[f((r,r′),r′′)+f(r′′,(r,r′))−f(r,r′′)−f(r′,r′′)\\displaystyle+\\sum\_{r^{\\prime\\prime}\\neq r,r^{\\prime}}\\left\[f((r,r^{\\prime}),r^{\\prime\\prime})+f(r^{\\prime\\prime},(r,r^{\\prime}))-f(r,r^{\\prime\\prime})-f(r^{\\prime},r^{\\prime\\prime})\\right. |  |\

where (r,r′)(r,r^{\\prime}) represents the new rank group formed by merging groups r,r′r,r^{\\prime}.

Once the ranks to merge have been identified, the optimal strength σ(r,r′)\\sigma\_{(r,r^{\\prime})} for the newly merged rank can be determined by solving the following equation:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | ∂Δ​ℒ​(r,r′)∂σ(r,r′)=0,\\frac{\\partial\\Delta\\mathcal{L}(r,r^{\\prime})}{\\partial\\sigma\_{(r,r^{\\prime})}}=0, |  | (34) |

which leads to

|     |     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  |  | σ(r,r′)=1+∑r′′≠r,r′ω(r,r′)​r′′​σr′′/(σ(r,r′)+σr′′)2/(σ(r,r′)+1)+∑r′′≠r,r′ωr′′​(r,r′)/(σ(r,r′)+σr′′),\\displaystyle\\sigma\_{(r,r^{\\prime})}=\\frac{1+\\sum\_{r^{\\prime\\prime}\\neq r,r^{\\prime}}\\omega\_{(r,r^{\\prime})r^{\\prime\\prime}}\\sigma\_{r^{\\prime\\prime}}/(\\sigma\_{(r,r^{\\prime})}+\\sigma\_{r^{\\prime\\prime}})}{2/(\\sigma\_{(r,r^{\\prime})}+1)+\\sum\_{r^{\\prime\\prime}\\neq r,r^{\\prime}}\\omega\_{r^{\\prime\\prime}(r,r^{\\prime})}/(\\sigma\_{(r,r^{\\prime})}+\\sigma\_{r^{\\prime\\prime}})}, |  | (35) |

which is the standard iterative update of Eq. ( [28](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.E28 "In II.4 Optimization ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) and can be solved efficiently with a computational complexity of O⁡(E⁡(𝒓)/R)O(E(\\bm{r})/R), where E⁡(𝒓)E(\\bm{r}) is now the number of non-zero entries in the matrix 𝝎\\bm{\\omega} for the current ranks 𝒓\\bm{r}. When applied across all R−1R-1 possible adjacent rank pairs (r,r′)(r,r^{\\prime}), the total complexity for this update and merge process is then O⁡(E⁡(𝒓))O(E(\\bm{r})).

Having computed the strength for the merged cluster (r,r′)(r,r^{\\prime}), we can repeat the process and do another full update for the strengths 𝝈\\bm{\\sigma} given the new ranking 𝒓\\bm{r} after the merge. Again, this requires using the previous iterative update with complexity O⁡(E⁡(𝒓))O(E(\\bm{r})). We can then continue alternating the updates 𝝈\|𝒓\\bm{\\sigma}\|\\bm{r} and 𝒓\|𝝈\\bm{r}\|\\bm{\\sigma} until all groups have been merged, at which point we can inspect all the examined rankings and select the ranking 𝒓^\\bm{\\hat{r}} associated with the highest posterior probability (the lowest value of ℒ⁡(𝒓,𝝈)\\mathcal{L}(\\bm{r},\\bm{\\sigma})). Pseudocode for the algorithm is shown in Algorithm [II.4](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.SS4 "II.4 Optimization ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons").

The overall complexity of this algorithm is given by:

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | O⁡(∑R=N2E⁡(𝒓))=O⁡(N2+α),\\displaystyle O\\left(\\sum\_{R=N}^{2}E(\\bm{r})\\right)=O(N^{2+\\alpha}), |  | (36) |

where NN is the number of nodes or players in the network, 𝒓\\bm{r} is the optimal rank vector for each value RR of the number of unique ranks during the merge process, and α∈\[0,1\]\\alpha\\in\[0,1\] depends on the density of the matrix 𝝎\\bm{\\omega} as the ranks are progressively merged. In the worst-case scenario, where the matrix 𝝎\\bm{\\omega} is maximally dense (E⁡(𝒓)=R2E(\\bm{r})=R^{2} at every step), the complexity would scale as O⁡(N3)O(N^{3}). However, such maximal density is highly unlikely for R≈NR\\approx N, especially in real, sparse networks. In practice, we observe a runtime scaling of approximately O⁡(N2)O(N^{2}) with the network size NN; see Fig. [7](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A2.F7 "Figure 7 ‣ Appendix B Time Complexity ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons") in Appendix [B](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A2 "Appendix B Time Complexity ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"). We note that this algorithm is greedy in nature, and therefore is only guaranteed to identify a local optimum for the MAP estimates 𝒓^\\bm{\\hat{r}}, 𝝈^\\bm{\\hat{\\sigma}}. However, exact optimization over node groupings is likely NP-hard (as is the case with many other clustering problems \[ [51](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib51 "")\]), making exact inference intractable in all but the smallest of networks. Greedy agglomerative algorithms have been shown to closely approximate the optimal log posterior probabilities obtained through exact enumeration and simulated annealing for other network clustering tasks in which we expect to preserve spatial contiguity \[ [52](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib52 ""), [53](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib53 "")\] or an initial temporal ordering \[ [54](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib54 "")\], similar in nature to preserving the initial ordering of the BT scores under the final inferred partial rankings, which is overwhelmingly the case in practice.

Fig. [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.F1 "Figure 1 ‣ II.4 Optimization ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons") shows the results of applying both the BT model and our Partial Rankings (PR) algorithm (with the BT likelihood of Eq. ( [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.E3 "In II.1 Bayesian Ranking and the Bradley-Terry Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"))) to a small dataset of dominance interactions in a pack of wolves \[ [55](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib55 "")\]. We notice that our model offers a more concise interpretation of the data by grouping individuals with similar BT scores into the same rankings.

Code implementing our partial ranking method can be found in an updated release of the PANINIpy package for nonparametric network inference \[ [56](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib56 "")\] and at [https://github.com/seb310/partial-rankings](https://github.com/seb310/partial-rankings "").

### III.1 Synthetic Match Datasets

We begin our analysis by evaluating the performance of our algorithm in recovering partial rankings from synthetic data. Specifically, we examine two key sources of uncertainty in ranking recovery: the number of matches played and the separation in player strengths. To achieve this, we design a synthetic model with two tunable parameters, MM and σ\\sigma, which control the number of matches played and the strength separation between players, respectively. The model requires a set of NN players and generates matches as follows:

1. 1.


For a given value of σ\\sigma, assign three scores \[−σ,0,σ\]\[-\\sigma,0,\\sigma\] (representing player strengths \[e−σ,1,eσ\]\[e^{-\\sigma},1,e^{\\sigma}\]) to define the planted partial rankings.

2. 2.


Randomly assign each of the NN players to one of the three rankings, ensuring that each rank includes at least one player.

3. 3.


For a chosen number of matches MM, randomly select pairs of players (with repetition) and simulate match outcomes using the BT model.


By varying MM and σ\\sigma, we generate networks of matches with different levels of sparsity and strength separation, allowing us to assess the performance of our algorithm across a wide range of scenarios. While the networks produced by this synthetic model differ from those typically observed in real data—in terms of density, clustering, or other structural features—working with such simplified models allows us to isolate and systematically examine how specific factors, namely the number of matches per pair and the separation of underlying player strengths, influence the algorithm’s performance. To that end, we adopt the simplest possible null model, in which matches are generated as random graphs, leaving all other structural features unspecified.

Results, shown in Fig. [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ III.1 Synthetic Match Datasets ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"), illustrate the algorithm’s ability to recover the planted rankings as a function of σ\\sigma and the average number of matches played per pair of nodes222



In a simple graph—i.e., a network with no self-loops or multi-edges—⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle corresponds to the _density_ of the network, measuring the fraction of realized edges out of all possible edges.
In our case, however, the network of matches is a _directed multigraph_, where multiple games may occur between the same pair of teams.
Hence, ⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle quantifies the expected number of matches played by a pair of nodes chosen uniformly at random from the network.
This can be seen as a natural generalization of network density to multigraphs, and we adopt it throughout this manuscript.

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | ⟨m⟩=M(N2),\\langle m\\rangle=\\frac{M}{\\binom{N}{2}}, |  | (37) |

for N=50N=50 and different total numbers of matches MM.

For each (σ,⟨m⟩)(\\sigma,\\langle m\\rangle) pair, 2020 different networks were generated, and the results were then averaged. The ranges of values for ⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle and σ\\sigma were chosen so as to capture all the relevant behaviors of the model.

As expected, we observe that for low values of ⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle, there is not enough evidence to recover the planted rankings regardless of the strength separation between players. In such sparse conditions, the algorithm assigns all players to the same rank, as shown in Fig. [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ III.1 Synthetic Match Datasets ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(a). This behavior contrasts sharply with the BT model, which still produces a complete ranking of the nodes even in these highly sparse conditions. As the number of matches increases and the networks become denser, the model gains sufficient statistical evidence to distinguish between the rankings and eventually recovers the planted structure. Notably, the number of matches required to correctly recover the rankings decreases rapidly with greater strength separation between players. In these cases, stronger players consistently outperform weaker ones, creating clearer signals that enable the ranking structure to be resolved with less data.

Perhaps more notable is the fact that our model does not consistently outperform the BT model in scenarios where players are nearly equal in strength. Fig. [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ III.1 Synthetic Match Datasets ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(b) shows a heatmap of the log posterior odds ratio, defined as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | Δ​ℒ=log⁡PP​R​(𝝅\|𝑨)PB​T​(𝝅\|𝑨),\\Delta\\mathcal{L}=\\log\\frac{P\_{PR}(\\bm{\\pi\|A})}{P\_{BT}(\\bm{\\pi\|A})}, |  | (38) |

as a function of ⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle and σ\\sigma. We observe that, when the separation in player strengths is small, our partial rankings model outperforms BT in sparse networks. In these cases, the PR model effectively leverages its ability to group players into shared ranks, avoiding overfitting. However, as the networks become denser, while our model accurately recovers the planted rankings even in this low-separation regime, it does not offer a more parsimonious description of the data (in terms of the posterior odds ratio of Eq. ( [38](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.E38 "In III.1 Synthetic Match Datasets ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"))) compared to the standard BT model which, in contrast, infers a fully ranked structure for the nodes with R=N=50R=N=50. This suggests a noisy regime where the evidence supporting partial rankings is relatively weak. The BT likelihood provides a more flexible fit for fine-gained variations in the data in these dense regimes (effectively overfitting the data), even if its prior poorly reflects the underlying data.

In Fig. [3](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.F3 "Figure 3 ‣ III.1 Synthetic Match Datasets ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons") we analyze the performance of both models in the absence of partial rankings, using networks of N=150N=150 players, each assigned a unique score sampled from a logistic distribution with mean zero and scale one—a setting in which the BT model is expected to accurately capture the data. Averaged over 20 network realizations, the results show the behaviour of our PR model and the _best performing model_, defined as the model (BT or PR) with the smallest negative log-posterior. For small values of ⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle, our PR model offers a more parsimonious fit, as the limited number of interactions prevents reliable inference of a complete ranking. Consequently, the log-posterior-odds ratio is positive in this regime, and the best performing model coincides with PR. As ⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle increases, however, we observe a sharp transition in which the BT model quickly surpasses the PR model in explanatory power—the log-posterior odds ratio becomes negative, and the best performing model switches to BT, which infers R=N=150R=N=150 distinct rankings. We also observe that the PR model continues to infer more rankings as ⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle grows, albeit very slowly, indicating that, in principle, it is capable of recovering the full ranking given sufficient data. We note that care has to be taken with large values of RR at high ⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle, as, in these large data regimes the influence of the prior terms on the log posterior diminishes and so does the regularization they provide. See Appendix [C](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A3 "Appendix C Increasing 𝑅 ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons") for a more detailed analysis of the behavior of the model for large RR and OPEN⟨m⟩)\\langle m\\rangle).

Interestingly, as the inferred number of groups increases with ⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle, the log posterior odds ratio decreases. This is somewhat counterintuitive, as one might expect the posterior odds ratio to improve as the number of rankings inferred by the PR model approaches the true number of rankings. However, as mentioned in Sec. [II](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2 "II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"), the PR model tends to heavily penalize the presence of a large number of rankings so that the net effect of increasing the number of rankings is a reduction in the posterior odds ratio, with the PR model becoming a less efficient encoding for the rankings as the number of inferred groups grows. In practice, the best practice is to evaluate both an original ranking method and our corresponding partial ranking method to determine which provides a more parsimonious fit to the data in terms of log-posterior odds.

To assess the goodness of the recovered partitions, in Appendix [D](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A4 "Appendix D Tau Recovery ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons") we compute the Kendall rank correlation coefficient (τB\\tau\_{B}), adjusted for ties, between the ground truth rankings and the rankings inferred by the BT and PR models. This comparison is performed both when partial rankings are present and when they are not. We observe that when partial rankings are present in the network, the PR model can quickly attain perfect recovery as the networks become denser. On the other hand, while the BT model still achieves high τB\\tau\_{B} scores, it never achieves perfect ordinal association and always underperforms relative to the PR model. However, when no partial rankings are present, and each player is assigned a unique score, the roles are reversed, and BT always displays a higher ordinal association with the ground truth rankings. We also compare the player strengths inferred by the PR and BT models to the ground truth, finding that both models recover scores that closely match the planted values. However, the PR model is able to leverage the coarse grained structure of the player strengths to group them into partial rankings.

We also compare our model with the partial rankings derived by applying 1D mean shift (MS) clustering \[ [57](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib57 "")\] to the rankings inferred by the BT model in Appendix [E](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A5 "Appendix E Comparison with 1D Clustering ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"). We observe that our algorithm can always recover the correct number of partial rankings when these are present, while MS fails to do so. More importantly, our algorithm can adjust the number of inferred clusters as more data becomes available, even in cases when no partial rankings exist, resulting in a more accurate description of the rankings. In contrast, mean shift clustering consistently infers roughly the same number of partial rankings regardless of the evidence provided by the data.

In this section, we focus on how our PR algorithm may be used to derive patterns in real-world data. We consider the datasets compiled in \[ [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib9 "")\] and available at \[ [74](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib74 "")\]. In addition, we also consider a dataset of social hierarchies among a pack of wolves \[ [55](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib55 ""), [28](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib28 "")\], and other sets of directed networks available at \[ [75](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib75 "")\]. The datasets are summarized in Table [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.T1 "Table 1 ‣ III.2 Real Match Datasets ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons").

We apply our PR algorithm and the BT model to all match lists in the dataset and compare the results. In Fig. [4](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ III.2 Real Match Datasets ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(a), we show the number of inferred ranks per node and the value of the log posterior odds ratio per node as a function of ⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle for each network. To standardize the number of ranks per node within the interval \[0,1\]\[0,1\], we rescale it as (R−1)/(N−1)(R-1)/(N-1), where NN is the number of nodes in the network and RR is the total number of ranks inferred by the best-performing model. This quantity will be 11 for perfect orderings in which R=NR=N. It will be 00 if no rankings are inferred and all nodes are assigned the same strength, and it will take intermediate values when non-trivial partial rankings are present.
The data reveals three distinct phases based on the density of the underlying network. For low densities, ⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle, the evidence is insufficient to support any meaningful ranking, leading to all actors being assigned the same strength. As connectivity increases, we observe an intermediate phase, in which partial rankings can often offer a more parsimonious description of the data compared to a perfect ordering of the nodes. In this intermediate regime, non-trivial subdivisions emerge, where actors are grouped by strength: all nodes within a group share the same strength, but strengths vary across groups. Finally, as the networks become sufficiently dense, enough data is available to support a perfect ordering of the nodes, and the BT model provides a more parsimonious description of the data.

To evaluate the homogeneity in the distribution of rank sizes, we adapt the concept of the _effective number of groups_ from the community detection literature \[ [76](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib76 "")\] and define the _effective number of rankings_. If RR is the number of rankings inferred by the PR algorithm, we can express the effective number of rankings R∗R^{\*} as

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | R∗=exp(−∑r=1RnrNlognrN),R^{\*}=\\exp\\left(-\\sum\_{r=1}^{R}\\frac{n\_{r}}{N}\\log\\frac{n\_{r}}{N}\\right), |  | (39) |

where nrn\_{r} is the size of rank rr and NN is the total number of nodes in the network. This measure reflects the balance in the size distribution of rankings: R∗R^{\*} will achieve a maximum value of RR when all the rankings are of equal size. In contrast, it approaches a minimal value close to one when the distribution is highly imbalanced, with most nodes concentrated in a single rank. Fig. [4](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ III.2 Real Match Datasets ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(b) shows R∗R^{\*} as a function of the inferred partial rankings RR for the PR model. While for most datasets R∗≃RR^{\*}\\simeq R, indicating that in most cases the PR algorithm infers roughly equally sized rankings, there are some notable exceptions, most notably the dataset of ATP tennis matches between 2010 and 2019, which displays a considerably smaller effective number of groups, reflecting a pronounced imbalance in the sizes of the inferred ranks. Specifically, the strongest 15 players are distributed across three partial rankings, while over 84%84\\% of all players are grouped into the weakest rank. This skewed decomposition likely stems from the structure of professional tennis tournaments. ATP matches are generally organized in a knock-out format, where losers are progressively eliminated. While this structure is efficient and straightforward, it can result in noisy rankings, as strong players may be eliminated early due to random fluctuations or unfavorable matchups. To mitigate this, the ATP organizes multiple knock-out tournaments throughout the year, ensuring that the strongest players consistently rise to the top despite potential variability in individual tournaments. However, this system disproportionately concentrates matches among the strongest players, who predominantly compete against one another as they progress through the brackets.
Consequently, the dataset contains ample information to distinguish the strongest players but far less data to differentiate among weaker players, leading to highly unbalanced partial rankings in terms of size.

Again, we compare the results obtained via our PR algorithm with those obtained by applying 1D Mean Shift clustering to the rankings inferred by the BT model. In Fig. [4](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ III.2 Real Match Datasets ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(c), we plot the number of partial rankings, RP​RR\_{PR}, inferred by the PR algorithm against the number of partial rankings, RM​SR\_{MS}, inferred by Mean Shift. While a general positive correlation is observed between the two quantities, there is significant variability in the number of rankings inferred by the two methods. Moreover, even when the total number of rankings inferred by both algorithms is similar, the rankings themselves may differ substantially, as evidenced by the Kendall rank correlation (τB\\tau\_{B}) scores also shown in Fig. [4](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ III.2 Real Match Datasets ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(c). Specifically, τB\\tau\_{B} scores appear to increase with the total number of groups inferred by both algorithms, regardless of how close RP​RR\_{PR} and RM​SR\_{MS} are. This suggests that the increase in τB\\tau\_{B} scores is likely an artefact resulting from the fact that the algorithms are likely to align on most pairwise relationships as the number of groups grows rather than reflecting a similarity in the group structures inferred by the two approaches.

Finally, we also compare the results inferred by our algorithm with those obtained by the BT model. Fig. [4](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.F4 "Figure 4 ‣ III.2 Real Match Datasets ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(d) shows the number of nodes NN in each network as a function of the rankings RR inferred by the partial rankings algorithm, where the points are colored according to the τB\\tau\_{B} score between the rankings inferred by the PR and BT models. Again, we observe a general tendency for the τB\\tau\_{B} scores to increase as RR increases.

Across real datasets from various domains we have found the emergence of three distinct regimes in rank detectability as a function of the network density. For low densities, there is too little statistical evidence available for distinguishing rankings and we infer a single partial rank, with the partial rankings model being preferred over the standard BT model. In an intermediate density regime of around ⟨m⟩≃0.02\\langle m\\rangle\\simeq 0.02 matches per node pair, we find a modest number of partial ranks much less than NN, and that the partial rankings model is often more parsimonious than BT in terms of posterior odds. Finally, for higher densities, we find that the BT model is most parsimonious, so that partial rankings are no longer needed as there is sufficient evidence in the data from the matches to distinguish the rankings of the nodes. These results are consistent with those in Sec. [III.1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.SS1 "III.1 Synthetic Match Datasets ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"). We also find of interest the considerable heterogeneity in inferred cluster sizes, suggesting that the underlying topology of the interaction network can influence the inferred partial rankings. This is of particular interest for ranking models such as BT that do not model edge placement, i.e. how the matches are decided. We additionally identify substantial discrepancies between the results of our pairwise partial ranking and those obtained by simply performing 1D clustering of the ranks, which highlights the importance of properly regularized ranking approaches that infer ties simultaneously while inferring the rankings. These partial rankings are shown to have increased correlation with the standard BT ranks as the number of ties approaches zero.

As a case study, we apply our algorithm to a dataset of faculty hires in Computer Science (CS) departments at U.S. higher education institutions, originally compiled in \[ [26](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib26 "")\] for analyzing hierarchies in academic hiring using a different ranking methodology. Each node in the network represents one of the 205 PhD-granting institutions included in the study, and a directed edge (i,j)(i,j) is included for each individual who received their doctorate from institution ii and held a tenure or tenure-track faculty position at institution jj during the data collection period (May 2011 to March 2012). For 153 of these institutions, we have access to their U.S. News & World Report (USN) rankings, which we use as node metadata to compare against the rankings and partial rankings inferred by the two pairwise ranking methods explored in this study.

The USN rankings are constructed from a broad set of indicators—including reputation surveys, research output, student selectivity, and institutional resources—and therefore reflect a metrics-based assessment of institutional prestige. In contrast, the rankings inferred from hiring data capture a more behavioral notion of prestige, one that emerges from the collective decisions of faculty members and hiring committees. Comparing these two types of rankings allows us to identify not only their areas of agreement but also systematic differences between them, revealing how the hierarchies implied by hiring dynamics may diverge from those defined by traditional metrics. These differences highlight the distinction between perceived prestige, i.e., how academics act upon their professional judgments when hiring or accepting positions, and evaluated prestige, i.e., how institutions are ranked based on quantifiable performance indicators such as publications, grants, and resources. Additionally, we manually collected endowment data for 151 of the 153 institutions with USN rankings to explore potential correlations with inferred prestige.

Fig. [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ III.3 Case Study: CS Faculty Hiring ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(a) shows the results of applying the BT model and our PR algorithm to the entire set of 205 PhD-granting institutions. Each bar represents an institution, ordered by its BT rank, with bar heights representing the BT strength scores. Bars are color-coded according to their PR rankings, with corresponding strengths shown in the legend in Fig. [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ III.3 Case Study: CS Faculty Hiring ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(b). The rankings are dominated by a small group of elite universities displaying significantly higher strengths, followed by a sharp decline in strength values further down the list. The PR and BT rankings show strong ordinal association (τB=0.82\\tau\_{B}=0.82; p-value =1.2×10−53=1.2\\times 10^{-53}) with only a small number of rank violations—instances where rankB​T​(A)>rankB​T​(B)\\text{rank}\_{BT}(A)>\\text{rank}\_{BT}(B) but rankP​R​(A)<rankP​R​(B)\\text{rank}\_{PR}(A)<\\text{rank}\_{PR}(B)— at the boundary between the two lowest-scoring PR groups, possibly indicating a lack of information to accurately determine the group boundaries.

In Fig. [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ III.3 Case Study: CS Faculty Hiring ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(b), we compare the partial rankings inferred by our model with the U.S. News & World Report rankings. Each bar in the plot represents a PhD-granting institution, ordered by its USN rank, with bar heights corresponding to the inverse of their USN rank. Again, bars are color-coded according to their partial rankings inferred by the PR model.

One first thing to notice with regards to the USN rankings is that they allow for partial rankings. For example, the four highest-scoring universities according to the USN methodology are all assigned an equal rank of 1. Fig. [5](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.F5 "Figure 5 ‣ III.3 Case Study: CS Faculty Hiring ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(b) reveals that institutions with equal USN ranking often belong to the same partial rankings according to the PR model. However, the PR model generally produces broader rankings, grouping institutions that span a wider range of USN ranks, suggesting that hiring-based prestige hierarchies are consistent with, but less granular than, those defined by metrics-based rankings, reflecting a structure of academic reputation that operates at the level of broad institutional tiers rather than fine rank distinctions. Another striking feature is the significantly higher number of rank violations, although the overall ordinal association remains strong (τB=0.76\\tau\_{B}=0.76; p-value =1.37×10−33=1.37\\times 10^{-33}). Unlike in the BT case, where rank violations likely result from limited information, the presence of rank violations across the entire ranking spectrum suggests that they reflect genuine differences between evaluated prestige—as measured by metrics-based rankings—and perceived prestige—as revealed through hiring dynamics. Indeed, some of these rank violations are particularly striking. For example, Harvard University, ranked 5t​h5^{th} in the BT model and belonging to the top-scoring PR group, is ranked 17t​h17^{th} according to the USN rankings, behind institutions including the University of Maryland, College Park (26t​h26^{th} in the BT model and in the third PR group) that scored significantly lower according to the hiring network structure. Similarly, the University of Chicago is ranked 19t​h19^{th} by the BT model and belongs to the second-highest-scoring PR group, but is ranked 35t​h35^{th} in the USN rankings, suggesting that these factors may not directly align with academic job market preferences. In the case of Harvard University or the University of Chicago, one might surmise that the prestigious reputation of the institutions might be influencing the job market more than their USN rankings. The opposite effect can also be observed. For instance, Northeastern University, ranked 61s​t61^{st} in the USN rankings, is assigned 178t​h178^{th} place by the BT model and belongs to the lowest PR group despite its considerably higher USN ranking. This suggests that perhaps this program was undervalued in the job market relative to its academic credentials during the period studied.

To evaluate how the BT rankings compare to the USN rankings, Fig. [6](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ III.3 Case Study: CS Faculty Hiring ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(a) plots the BT rank of each institution against its corresponding USN rank, with points color-coded based on their PR rank. The highest-ranking institutions, according to both models, tend to concentrate around the RB​T=RU​S​NR\_{BT}=R\_{USN} line, where RB​TR\_{BT} and RU​S​NR\_{USN} represent the BT and USN rankings respectively—indicating close agreement between the two ranking systems for top-tier institutions. As we move towards lower-ranked institutions, we observe an increasing divergence between the rankings. While the overall ordinal association remains strong (τB=0.73\\tau\_{B}=0.73, p-value =1.07×10−38=1.07\\times 10^{-38}), the spread in rankings widens, and institutions with a given USN rank are often assigned considerably different BT ranks, with the BT model frequently assigning these institutions higher positions compared to the USN rankings. This discrepancy is particularly pronounced among institutions in the two lowest-scoring PR groups. In general, these results suggest that, outside the top-ranked institutions, subjective perceptions of prestige can significantly shape professional mobility, elevating or diminishing institutions relative to their metric-based ranking. Likewise, the observation that institutions with similar BT ranks span a broad range of USN ranks indicates that perceived prestige, as expressed through hiring and faculty mobility, does not always correspond directly to quantitative measures of institutional performance.

An interesting question about the pairwise ranking methods considered in this work is whether an institution’s rank benefits more from “who you win against” than from the sheer number of “wins”. In other words, does an institution’s ranking improve more by producing a large number of doctoral graduates who secure tenure-track positions across a wide range of institutions or by producing fewer graduates who secure positions at highly ranked institutions? To address this, in Fig. [6](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ III.3 Case Study: CS Faculty Hiring ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(b), we plot the average BT strength of an institution’s out-neighbors (capturing the quality of an institution’s “wins”) as a function of its out-degree (representing the total number of “wins” an institution has). We observe a clear positive correlation (Pearson p=0.78p=0.78; p-value =1.32×10−43=1.32\\times 10^{-43}) with the highest ranked institutions having both a large number of “wins” (i.e. producing a large number of graduates that go on to secure tenure-track positions at other institutions), as well as out-neighbors with high BT strengths (i.e. their graduates tend to secure positions at high-ranking institutions). Perhaps more revealing is the fact that the PR groups appear to stratify according to the average BT strength of their out-neighbors, suggesting a well-defined hierarchy characterized by a predominance in horizontal mobility as opposed to vertical mobility.
This observation is reinforced by analyzing the network of mobility flows between the PR groups; see Fig. [6](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ III.3 Case Study: CS Faculty Hiring ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(e). The results reveal a well-defined hierarchy with minimal upward mobility across ranks. Most mobility occurs downward within the hierarchy, a trend partly explained by the relatively small size of the top-ranked groups. Nonetheless, horizontal mobility emerges as a critical driver of hiring dynamics across all levels, particularly within the lower-ranked groups, where it becomes the dominant hiring pattern. Horizontal mobility is also particularly pronounced at the topmost level of the hierarchy, where the top five institutions comprising this elite group hire over twice as many faculty members from within their group as they do from all other PhD-granting institutions combined.

Finally, we note that the ranking patterns possess a moderately strong ordinal association with the endowments of the institutions. Although there is considerable variability, higher-ranked institutions generally tend to have significantly larger endowments, as illustrated in Fig. [6](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.F6 "Figure 6 ‣ III.3 Case Study: CS Faculty Hiring ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons") (c)-(d). The high variability can be partly attributed to differences in the number of institutions contributing to each (USN rank, PR rank) group—i.e., the number of universities represented by each bar. Group sizes vary considerably across the data set, although most bars consist of relatively few institutions (1–25 universities per bar; median = 2, mode = 1). As a result, standard deviations are generally uninformative for most bars, with variability estimates becoming more reliable only for larger groups.

## IV Conclusion

We have introduced a probabilistic generative model for inferring partial rankings in directed networks and a fast, fully nonparametric, agglomerative algorithm for efficient inference. We have shown that, particularly with limited observations available, our model can provide a more parsimonious description of pairwise comparison data than models that inherently assume complete rankings, such as the BT model. Specifically, in extremely sparse regimes, our model effectively recognizes that insufficient evidence exists to infer any meaningful ranking among the compared entities. As the network’s connectivity increases, partial rankings typically emerge as a more compact and accurate description of the data. Finally, as we move towards more dense regimes, we reach a point where sufficient information is available to infer a complete ordinal ranking of all the nodes. When applied to a network of faculty hiring among U.S. computer science departments, our algorithm inferred five distinct partial rankings, which align closely with the ordinal rankings inferred by the BT model and reveal a well-defined hierarchy dominated by a small number of elite universities. The inferred rankings also highlight the limited upward mobility within the hierarchy, with lateral and downward movements being more prevalent.

There are several directions in which our work can be extended. While widely used, the classical BT model represents a relatively simplistic approach to pairwise interactions between entities. Numerous extensions to the model have been proposed, including accounting for ties \[ [16](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib16 ""), [17](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib17 "")\], home-field advantage \[ [15](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib15 "")\], randomness in match outcomes, and imbalances in strength or skill between the average pair of players \[ [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib9 "")\]. Any of these extensions could, in principle, be incorporated into our partial rankings framework, enabling the development of more expressive models.

Another characteristic of both the BT model and our partial rankings framework is that they do not explicitly account for the placement of edges in the network—that is, the likelihood does not include a term that models the probability of observing a given set of matches in the first place. As we observed in Sec. [III.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.SS3 "III.3 Case Study: CS Faculty Hiring ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"), similarly (partially) ranked institutions tend to interact primarily with one another. A similar observation holds for the ATP tennis dataset, where the strongest players, who advance through the tournament, tend to play more matches and preferentially against each other. While other methods explicitly model edge placement \[ [12](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib12 ""), [13](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib13 "")\], this aspect remains absent from the current BT and partial rankings models. Incorporating it into the framework could help to explore how network topology influences ranking recovery.

Our framework could also be extended to consider other cases of interest, such as dynamic rankings, where the ranks of the individual entities can rise or fall over time \[ [21](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib21 "")\], personalised rankings, in which ranking results are tailored to individual users based on their preferences \[ [77](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib77 "")\], or a combination of both.

Finally, while we have developed a fully Bayesian nonparametric framework to infer partial rankings, we have focused on inferring point estimates of these rankings without considering uncertainty quantification as done in other works \[ [23](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib23 ""), [13](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib13 "")\]. However, as noted in Sec. [II.3](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.SS3 "II.3 Bayesian Nonparametric Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"), the Bayesian nature of our model lends itself naturally to the exploration of the full posterior, which can be explored via the development of suitable inference algorithms—such as Expectation-Maximization (EM) or MCMC methods—to enable posterior sampling or approximation, thereby allowing for principled uncertainty quantification in inferred rankings.

## Appendix A Minimization of f⁡(R)f(R)

We prove that the function

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | f⁡(R)=log⁡(N−1R−1)+log⁡(Nn1​…​nR)f(R)=\\log\\binom{N-1}{R-1}+\\log\\binom{N}{n\_{1}\\ldots n\_{R}} |  | (40) |

has a global minimum at R=1R=1.

We begin proving that the second term in Eq. ( [40](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A1.E40 "In Appendix A Minimization of 𝑓(𝑅) ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) is maximized when all groups are of equal size such that nr=n=N/R∀rn\_{r}=n=N/R~~\\forall r.

###### Proposition 1.

Let N∈ℤ+N\\in\\mathbb{Z}^{+} and let {nr}∈ℤ+\\{n\_{r}\\}\\in\\mathbb{Z}^{+} be a set of positive integers such that ∑r=1Rnr=N\\sum\_{r=1}^{R}n\_{r}=N. Then the multinomial coefficient (Nn1​…​nR)\\binom{N}{n\_{1}\\ldots n\_{R}} is maximized when nr=n=N/R∀rn\_{r}=n=N/R~~\\forall r.

###### Proof.

By definition

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | (Nn1​…​nR)=N!n1!​…​nR!.\\binom{N}{n\_{1}\\ldots n\_{R}}=\\frac{N!}{n\_{1}!\\ldots n\_{R}!}. |  | (41) |

Using Stirling’s approximation of the factorial,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | log⁡k!≈k​log⁡k−k+12​log⁡(2​π​k),\\log k!\\approx k\\log k-k+\\frac{1}{2}\\log(2\\pi k), |  | (42) |

we have that, to first order

|     |     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | (Nn1​…​nR)\\displaystyle\\binom{N}{n\_{1}\\ldots n\_{R}} | =exp⁡{N​log⁡N−N−∑r=1R(nr​log⁡nr−nr)}\\displaystyle=\\exp\\left\\{N\\log N-N-\\sum\_{r=1}^{R}(n\_{r}\\log n\_{r}-n\_{r})\\right\\} |  | (43) |
|  |  | =exp⁡{N​log⁡N−∑r=1Rnr​log⁡nr},\\displaystyle=\\exp\\left\\{N\\log N-\\sum\_{r=1}^{R}n\_{r}\\log n\_{r}\\right\\}, |  | (44) |

where we have used the fact that ∑rnr=N\\sum\_{r}n\_{r}=N.

To maximize Eq. ( [41](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A1.E41 "In Proof. ‣ Appendix A Minimization of 𝑓(𝑅) ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")), we then have to solve the following constrained optimization problem

|     |     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  |  | max{nr}⁡(N​log⁡N−∑r=1Rnr​log⁡nr)\\displaystyle\\max\_{\\{n\_{r}\\}}\\left(N\\log N-\\sum\_{r=1}^{R}n\_{r}\\log n\_{r}\\right) |  | (45) |
|  |  | s.t.∑r=1Rnr=N,\\displaystyle\\textrm{s.t.}\\quad\\sum\_{r=1}^{R}n\_{r}=N, |  | (46) |

which corresponds to extremizing the following Lagrangian

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | ℒ({nr},λ)=−∑r=1Rnrlognr+λ(∑r=1Rnr−N),\\mathcal{L}(\\{n\_{r}\\},\\lambda)=-\\sum\_{r=1}^{R}n\_{r}\\log n\_{r}+\\lambda\\left(\\sum\_{r=1}^{R}n\_{r}-N\\right), |  | (47) |

where we have ignored constant terms. Taking the partial derivatives with respect to nrn\_{r} and equating to zero, we have

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | ∂ℒ∂nr=−(log⁡nr+1)+λ=0⟹nr=eλ−1≡C.\\frac{\\partial\\mathcal{L}}{\\partial n\_{r}}=-(\\log n\_{r}+1)+\\lambda=0\\implies n\_{r}=e^{\\lambda-1}\\equiv C. |  | (48) |

From the sum constraint, we then have

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | ∑r=1Rnr=R​C=N⟹C=nr=NR​∀r.\\sum\_{r=1}^{R}n\_{r}=RC=N\\implies C=n\_{r}=\\frac{N}{R}~~\\forall r. |  | (49) |

∎

Given Proposition [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1. ‣ Appendix A Minimization of 𝑓(𝑅) ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons") and in view of the monotonicity of the logarithm, it follows that the second term on the right-hand side of Eq. ( [40](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A1.E40 "In Appendix A Minimization of 𝑓(𝑅) ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) is maximized when all groups are of equal size.

Given this result, we can now prove our original statement.

###### Proposition 2.

The function

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|  | f⁡(R)=log⁡(N−1R−1)+log⁡(Nn1​…​nR)f(R)=\\log\\binom{N-1}{R-1}+\\log\\binom{N}{n\_{1}\\ldots n\_{R}} |  |

has a global minimum at R=1R=1 with f⁡(R=1)=0f(R=1)=0.

###### Proof.

From Proposition [1](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#Thmproposition1 "Proposition 1. ‣ Appendix A Minimization of 𝑓(𝑅) ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons") and by virtue of the monotonicity of the logarithm, it follows that Eq. ( [40](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A1.E40 "In Appendix A Minimization of 𝑓(𝑅) ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) is upper bounded by

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | f⁡(R)≤log⁡(N−1R−1)+log⁡N!n!R,f(R)\\leq\\log\\binom{N-1}{R-1}+\\log\\frac{N!}{n!^{R}}, |  | (50) |

with n=N/Rn=N/R. Eq. ( [50](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A1.E50 "In Proof. ‣ Appendix A Minimization of 𝑓(𝑅) ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) can be written as

|     |     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | f⁡(R)\\displaystyle f(R) | ≤log⁡(N−1R−1)+log⁡N!−R​log⁡n!\\displaystyle\\leq\\log\\binom{N-1}{R-1}+\\log N!-R\\log n! |  | (51) |
|  |  | ≈log⁡(N−1R−1)+N​log⁡N−N−R⁡(NR​log⁡NR−NR)\\displaystyle\\approx\\log\\binom{N-1}{R-1}+N\\log N-N-R\\left(\\frac{N}{R}\\log\\frac{N}{R}-\\frac{N}{R}\\right) |  | (52) |
|  |  | =log⁡(N−1R−1)+N​log⁡R,\\displaystyle=\\log\\binom{N-1}{R-1}+N\\log R, |  | (53) |

where we have once more made use of Stirling’s approximation. The first term on the right-hand side of Eq. ( [53](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A1.E53 "In Proof. ‣ Appendix A Minimization of 𝑓(𝑅) ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) is a concave function of RR, reaching its minimum value of zero at both R=1R=1 and R=NR=N. The second term, however, increases monotonically with RR, starting from a minimum of zero when R=1R=1. Since these two terms combine additively in Eq. ( [53](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A1.E53 "In Proof. ‣ Appendix A Minimization of 𝑓(𝑅) ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")), their sum must attain its global minimum of zero at R=1R=1, where both terms simultaneously achieve their lowest values. Finally, since the binomial and multinomial coefficients are always greater than or equal to one, it follows that Eq ( [40](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A1.E40 "In Appendix A Minimization of 𝑓(𝑅) ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) is also lower bounded by zero, meaning it must attain a global minimum of zero at R=1R=1.
∎

We analyze the recovery performance of our algorithm as the number of partial rankings increases. Fig. [8](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A3.F8 "Figure 8 ‣ Appendix C Increasing 𝑅 ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons") shows the behavior of the model for a network with N=50N=50 nodes and R=15R=15 planted rankings for different values of ⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle and σ\\sigma

By increasing the number of planted rankings RR, we observe that the number of matches required to successfully recover the correct ranking structure also increases, especially at low noise levels (σ\\sigma). This is expected, as distinguishing a larger number of groups demands more evidence in the form of pairwise comparisons.

At low σ\\sigma, the algorithm continues to accurately recover the planted rankings across a wide range of conditions. However, the model still yields a less parsimonious description of the data compared to the BT model as observed in Fig. [2](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S3.F2 "Figure 2 ‣ III.1 Synthetic Match Datasets ‣ III Results ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"). This is due to the inconsistency between the extensive number of rank groups and the prior, and can in principle be addressed with a weaker prior, e.g. one that is uniform over rank partitions rather than the number of ranks.

Increasing the score separation at high RR values pushes the algorithm to find a larger number of ranks than expected. This arises from the behavior of the log-likelihood in the high-⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle regime as the number of planted rankings RR increases. Figure [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A3.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ Appendix C Increasing 𝑅 ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons") illustrates the log posterior ℒ\\mathcal{L}, along with the contributions of its individual components—namely C⁡(R)C(R), g⁡(r)g(r), and f⁡(r,s)f(r,s)—as functions of the number of inferred rankings RR, for varying values of ⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle and planted rankings R∗R^{\*}. Figures [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A3.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ Appendix C Increasing 𝑅 ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(a) and (b) show the behavior of the algorithm at low and high values of ⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle, respectively, for R∗=5R^{\*}=5 planted rankings with equally spaced scores.

At low ⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle, the three terms contribute to produce a global minimum of the log posterior at R=R∗=5R=R^{\*}=5, thereby correctly identifying the true number of rankings. In contrast, in the high-⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle regime, the contributions from C⁡(R)C(R) and g⁡(r)g(r) diminish, and the log-posterior becomes dominated by the likelihood term f⁡(r,s)f(r,s). This marks the transition to a high-data regime where the influence of the prior effectively “washes out”, at which point our PR model behaves similarly to the BT model—which is prone to overfitting. Specifically, the likelihood term f⁡(r,s)f(r,s) decreases monotonically with increasing RR, favoring more complex models.

However, as seen in Fig. [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A3.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ Appendix C Increasing 𝑅 ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(b), this decrease is sufficiently slow that the prior still regularizes the model adequately, and the global minimum remains at R=5R=5. But this balance deteriorates as R∗R^{\*} increases. While the behavior at low ⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle remains qualitatively unchanged (Fig. [9](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A3.F9 "Figure 9 ‣ Appendix C Increasing 𝑅 ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(c)), in the high-⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle regime the monotonic decrease of f⁡(r,s)f(r,s) with RR becomes steeper. As a result, the regularizing influence of the prior weakens further, reducing its ability to constrain model complexity and allowing the algorithm to overfit.

## Appendix F Comparison with the RC-BTL model

The _Rank-Clustered Bradley-Terry-Luce_ model (RC-BTL), introduced by Pearce and Erosheva \[ [23](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib23 "")\], is a Bayesian method to infer rank-clusters which employs a spike-and-slab prior to encourage parameter fusion. Specifically, the generating process behind the RC-BTL is as follows:

1. 1.


Sample the total number of unique ranks (rank-clusters) from a Poisson distribution with parameter λ\\lambda



|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | P⁡(R\|λ)=e−λ​λRR!.P(R\|\\lambda)=\\frac{e^{-\\lambda}\\lambda^{R}}{R!}. |  | (54) |



This controls the “spikiness” of the distribution. Lower values of λ\\lambda favor a smaller number of clusters, encouraging parameter fusion. On the other hand, larger values of λ\\lambda promote a larger number of clusters, decreasing the probability of ties in the observed rankings.

2. 2.


Draw the unique strengths for each cluster from a Gamma distribution with parameters aγa\_{\\gamma} and bγb\_{\\gamma}



|     |     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | P⁡(𝝈\|R,aγ,bγ)\\displaystyle P(\\bm{\\sigma}\|R,a\_{\\gamma},b\_{\\gamma}) | =∏r=1RGamma​(σr\|aγ,bγ)\\displaystyle=\\prod\_{r=1}^{R}\\text{Gamma}(\\sigma\_{r}\|a\_{\\gamma},b\_{\\gamma}) |  |
|  |  | =∏r=1RbγaγΓ⁡(aγ)​σraγ−1​e−bγ​σr.\\displaystyle=\\prod\_{r=1}^{R}\\frac{b\_{\\gamma}^{a\_{\\gamma}}}{\\Gamma(a\_{\\gamma})}\\sigma\_{r}^{a\_{\\gamma}-1}e^{-b\_{\\gamma}\\sigma\_{r}}. |  | (55) |



The parameters aγa\_{\\gamma} and bγb\_{\\gamma} control the distribution of the unique player scores. However, as pointed out by the authors in \[ [23](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib23 "")\], the player strengths are invariant to multiplicative transformations, so that the choice of aγa\_{\\gamma} and bγb\_{\\gamma} is generally non-influential. The primary advantage of this prior is that it allows for a closed-form Gibbs sampling of the posterior via data augmentation, resulting in an efficient MCMC sampler \[ [23](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib23 ""), [31](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib31 "")\].

3. 3.

and the standard BT model can be recovered, up to O⁡(log⁡N)O(\\log N), by setting R=λ=NR=\\lambda=N.

The primary differences between RC-BTL and our PR model lie in the formulation of the partition prior. In RC-BTL, the probability of observing a specific partition into RR clusters is, by design, independent of the cluster sizes, so that any partition into RR groups is equally probable under the model444



As pointed out in \[ [23](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib23 "")\], there still is a certain degree of implicit dependence of the cluster sizes on the number of groups. For example, if R=NR=N, then all clusters must be singletons, such that nr=1​∀rn\_{r}=1~\\forall r.. As discussed in Section [II](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2 "II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"), this indifference with respect to group sizes implicitly biases the model toward balanced partitions, since the vast majority of partitions divide nodes into approximately equal-sized groups purely on combinatorial grounds. See Appendix [G](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A7 "Appendix G Partition sizes under uniform sampling ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"). By contrast, the PR model first samples the distribution of group sizes from a uniform prior over all possible histograms of group sizes, conditioned on RR. Under this construction, clusters of any size are equally plausible a priori, so the model remains agnostic about balance until information from the data is incorporated. Another key difference is that RC-BTL requires us to fix three hyperparameters, aγa\_{\\gamma}, bγb\_{\\gamma}, and λ\\lambda. As previously mentioned, the effect of aγa\_{\\gamma} and bγb\_{\\gamma} on the inference results is limited. λ\\lambda, on the other hand, controls the average number of unique rankings we expect to see and can considerably alter the inferred partitions. On the one hand, this is an advantage, as λ\\lambda can be tuned to encourage or discourage rank-clustering depending on how one sees fit. On the other hand, choosing appropriate values for the hyperparameters can be nontrivial and poor choices could potentially bias the results. In \[ [23](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#bib.bib23 "")\], the authors suggest setting aγ=5a\_{\\gamma}=5, bγ=3b\_{\\gamma}=3, and λ=1\\lambda=1 in a pairwise comparison setting so as to encourage rank clustering, given that pairwise comparisons provide limited ordinal information about the items being ranked. In what follows, we use the same hyperparameter settings for all the considered datasets.

The second crucial difference between the two models is that RC-BTL samples the entire posterior via MCMC, while, in its current implementation, the PR model provides only MAP estimates via the agglomerative greedy algorithm described in Section [II](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2 "II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"). RC-BTL thus has the advantage of yielding a full posterior over the rankings, allowing the user to perform uncertainty quantification. However, this uncertainty quantification comes at the cost of a substantially slower inference process involving MCMC sampling. By contrast, the PR objective can be more easily redefined for different priors and likelihoods, including that of the RC-BTL model as shown below, and provides fast MAP estimates using a greedy agglomerative approach. In principle, different priors could also be used within the MCMC sampling for RC-BTL, but the specific prior structure in RC-BTL is crucial for sampler efficiency as it enables closed-form Gibbs updates of the cluster strengths.

A similar consideration must be made for the strength parameters of the RC-BTL consensus partition. One option is to assign each cluster in the consensus partition a σ^r\\hat{\\sigma}\_{r} value equal to the average strength of all the teams in each cluster, where the strengths of each team can be computed as the median strengths along all the MCMC samples. Another option is to proceed as above and compute the MAP 𝝈\\bm{\\sigma} scores of each cluster in the consensus partition, i.e. the values of 𝝈\\bm{\\sigma} that maximize the posterior given the consensus partition. In what follows we adopt both methods. Finally, we can also use the PR algorithm to compute a MAP estimate of the RC-BTL model by substituting the objective of Eq. ( [13](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#S2.Ex1 "In II.3 Bayesian Nonparametric Model ‣ II Methods ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) with Eq. ( [60](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A6.Ex12 "In F.1 Assessing the Quality of the Partitions ‣ Appendix F Comparison with the RC-BTL model ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) and updating the strength parameters via Eq. ( [62](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A6.E62 "In F.1 Assessing the Quality of the Partitions ‣ Appendix F Comparison with the RC-BTL model ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")).

### F.2 Recovery of Synthetic Datasets

In the above section, we observed that in the RC-BTL model, most MCMC samples will provide a better explanation of the data (in terms of the log-posterior) than the BT model in all of the datasets analyzed. We explore this further by considering three synthetically generated datasets. One dataset consists of N=30N=30 players, each with its own unique strength σi\\sigma\_{i} generated from the BT model. The second and third datasets both consist of three planted rankings, with each team/player assigned to one of these rankings. These two datasets differ only in the heterogeneity of their planted partitions: one has clusters of comparable size, while the other exhibits strong size imbalance.

Importantly, RC-BTL infers the full posterior, so analysis is not limited to the consensus partition. This type of uncertainty quantification is currently absent in the PR model and could substantially enhance its interpretability and robustness.

A slightly different picture emerges when the cluster sizes become more heterogeneous. In the previous dataset, nodes were randomly assigned to clusters, which produced two clusters of roughly equal size and a larger cluster approximately twice as large as the other two (in decreasing order of strength: nr=\[7,15,8\]n\_{r}=\[7,15,8\]). We can consider a more heterogeneous setting in which we explicitly assign nodes to clusters of sizes nr=\[20,8,2\]n\_{r}=\[20,8,2\], again sorted in decreasing order of strength. We find that the PR algorithm eventually recovers both the correct number and the correct composition of rankings. In contrast, the RC-BTL consensus partition never achieves exact recovery. Furthermore, the previously observed gradual reduction in the number of inferred groups with increasing evidence is absent, and the consensus partition consistently identifies between five and six groups. In fact, none of the individual MCMC samples recovers the true number of partitions (see Fig. [21](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A6.F21 "Figure 21 ‣ F.2.2 Recovery of planted PR rankings ‣ F.2 Recovery of Synthetic Datasets ‣ Appendix F Comparison with the RC-BTL model ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")). Nevertheless, examining the posterior rank-clustering probabilities of the RC-BTL model reveals that, although a larger number of matches is required compared to the previous setting in order to detect evidence of a three-cluster structure, very strong evidence does emerge when ⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle is sufficiently large. See Fig. [20](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A6.F20 "Figure 20 ‣ F.2.2 Recovery of planted PR rankings ‣ F.2 Recovery of Synthetic Datasets ‣ Appendix F Comparison with the RC-BTL model ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(b).

![Refer to caption](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4/figures/synthetic_data/2_pr_recovery_heterogeneous_R_countplot.png)Figure 21: Countplot of the number of inferred rankings by the RC-BTL MCMC samples for a synthetic dataset with three planted rankings of sizes nr=\[20,8,2\]n\_{r}=\[20,8,2\] and strengths 𝝈=\[e3,e0,e−3\]\\bm{\\sigma}=\[e^{3},e^{0},e^{-3}\]. We observe that none of the samples is ever able to recover the planted number of rankings.

How, then, can information about the three-cluster structure emerge in the rank-clustering probabilities when it is seemingly absent from every individual MCMC sample? To address this, we examine the partitions inferred by the RC-BTL model at high ⟨m⟩\\langle m\\rangle. We find that the overwhelming majority of sampled partitions correctly identify the two smaller groups. However, inferring a third group of size nr=20n\_{r}=20 would yield partitions with extreme heterogeneity in cluster sizes. To avoid this, the RC-BTL model instead tends to subdivide the largest group into multiple subgroups, thereby reducing size imbalance in accordance with its implicit bias toward balanced partitions. Crucially, these subdivisions are not supported by the underlying data; they arise solely from the model’s preference for homogeneity. As a consequence, each sample partitions the largest group differently, with subdivisions amounting to random reshufflings of the same set of nodes (with minor fluctuations in subgroup size and number). When aggregating across the full posterior, this artificial variability cancels out, and nodes in the largest group appear with roughly equal probability of belonging to the same cluster666



This implies that the RC-BTL results must be interpreted at the level of the full posterior: although the marginal distributions clearly indicate three distinct rank clusters, no single sampled partition will provide a representative realization of the underlying structure.. This effect is also evident in Fig. [19](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A6.F19 "Figure 19 ‣ F.2.1 Recovery of planted BT rankings ‣ F.2 Recovery of Synthetic Datasets ‣ Appendix F Comparison with the RC-BTL model ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")(b), where MCMC samples frequently split the largest group into two nearly equal subgroups in order to enforce cluster size homogeneity. By contrast, the hierarchy of priors in PR is deliberately designed to be as agnostic as possible with respect to the data, enabling recovery of both the correct number and composition of clusters in its MAP partition. At the same time, this result once again underscores the benefits of sampling from the full posterior rather than relying solely on a single MAP estimate, since posterior sampling can mitigate biases inherent in the underlying model. Consequently, we view the development of an MCMC sampler for the PR model as a promising direction for future work. Such an extension would, however, entail a trade-off, as it would likely reduce computational efficiency, which currently makes PR an attractive alternative to fully Bayesian methods that rely on posterior sampling.

## Appendix G Partition sizes under uniform sampling

Given a fixed number of groups RR, we want to evaluate the probability of observing specific distributions of group sizes when sampling partitions of NN objects uniformly at random. If the groups are treated as labeled, the problem reduces to counting how many assignments of objects give rise to a particular size profile {nr}r=1R\\{n\_{r}\\}\_{r=1}^{R}, where ∑r=1Rnr=N\\sum\_{r=1}^{R}n\_{r}=N. The number of assignments consistent with this profile is given by the multinomial coefficient

|     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- |
|  | Ω⁡(n1,…,nR)=N!n1!⋯nR!.\\Omega(n\_{1},\\dots,n\_{R})=\\frac{N!}{n\_{1}!\\cdots n\_{R}!}. |  |

In contrast, we are interested in unlabeled partitions, where only the multiset of group sizes matters. For example, the configurations {1,2,3}\\{1,2,3\\} and {2,3,1}\\{2,3,1\\} are indistinguishable. To correct for this, we must account for the number of distinct permutations of the size vector. This equals the total number of permutations of RR terms divided by the permutations that leave the vector unchanged due to repeated sizes. Thus,

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | Ωlab​(n1,…,nR)=N!∏r=1Rnr!⋅R!∏umu!,\\Omega\_{\\mathrm{lab}}(n\_{1},\\dots,n\_{R})\\;=\\;\\frac{N!}{\\prod\_{r=1}^{R}n\_{r}!}\\cdot\\frac{R!}{\\prod\_{u}m\_{u}!}, |  | (63) |

where mum\_{u} denotes the multiplicity of groups of size uu. However, Eq. ( [63](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A7.E63 "In Appendix G Partition sizes under uniform sampling ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) still counts labeled partitions. To obtain the number of unlabeled partitions with the given size profile, we must divide by the number of ways to relabel the RR groups, i.e. by R!R!. This yields

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | Ωunlab​(n1,…,nR)=N!∏r=1Rnr!​∏umu!.\\Omega\_{\\mathrm{unlab}}(n\_{1},\\dots,n\_{R})\\;=\\;\\frac{N!}{\\prod\_{r=1}^{R}n\_{r}!\\;\\prod\_{u}m\_{u}!}. |  | (64) |

The total probability of observing a partition with a particular size profile is then given by

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | p⁡({nr})=ΩunlabS⁡(N,R),p(\\{n\_{r}\\})=\\frac{\\Omega\_{\\mathrm{unlab}}}{S(N,R)}, |  | (65) |

where S⁡(N,R)S(N,R) are the Stirling numbers of the second kind, which count the number of ways to partition a set of size NN into RR indistinguishable and nonempty groups.

Eq. ( [64](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A7.E64 "In Appendix G Partition sizes under uniform sampling ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) shows that the count decomposes into two competing contributions: the multinomial factor N!/∏rnr!N!/\\prod\_{r}n\_{r}!, which, as shown in Appendix [A](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A1 "Appendix A Minimization of 𝑓(𝑅) ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons"), is maximized when all groups are as balanced as possible (nr≈N/Rn\_{r}\\approx N/R), and the multiplicity factor ∏umu!\\prod\_{u}m\_{u}!, which penalizes size profiles in which multiple groups share the same size, since each repetition reduces the number of distinct unlabeled partitions. The observed distribution of group sizes is therefore determined by a tradeoff: the multinomial term favors near-equal block sizes, while the multiplicity term discourages ties between group sizes.

We can investigate the behvaior of Eq. ( [65](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A7.E65 "In Appendix G Partition sizes under uniform sampling ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) in the limit of large NN. Taking the logarithm of Eq. ( [64](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A7.E64 "In Appendix G Partition sizes under uniform sampling ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons")) we have that

|     |     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | log⁡Ωunlab​({nr})\\displaystyle\\log\\Omega\_{\\mathrm{unlab}}(\\{n\_{r}\\}) | =log⁡N!∏rnr!​∏umu!\\displaystyle=\\log\\frac{N!}{\\prod\_{r}n\_{r}!\\prod\_{u}m\_{u}!} |  | (66) |
|  |  | ≈N(−∑r=1Rqrlogqr)−∑ulogmu!,\\displaystyle\\approx N\\left(-\\sum\_{r=1}^{R}q\_{r}\\log q\_{r}\\right)-\\sum\_{u}\\log m\_{u}!, |  |

where qr=nr/Nq\_{r}=n\_{r}/N are the relative sizes of each group and H(q)=−∑rqrlogqrH(q)=-\\sum\_{r}q\_{r}\\log q\_{r} is the entropy of the relative-size vector. Using the classical asymptotic approximation S⁡(N,R)∼RN/R!S(N,R)\\sim R^{N}/R! for the Stirling numbers of the second kind, we then have

|     |     |     |     |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
|  | log⁡p⁡({nr})≈N⁡(H⁡(q)−log⁡R)−∑ulog⁡mu!+log⁡R!.\\log p(\\{n\_{r}\\})\\approx N(H(q)-\\log R)-\\sum\_{u}\\log m\_{u}!+\\log R!. |  | (67) |

Since there are at most RR terms in the sum over multiplicities and each term in the sum is at most log⁡R!\\log R! (because OPENmu≤R​∀u)m\_{u}\\leq R~\\forall u), the last two terms in Eq. [67](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.02505v4#A7.E67 "In Appendix G Partition sizes under uniform sampling ‣ Estimation of partial rankings from sparse, noisy comparisons") scale as O⁡(R​log⁡R)O(R\\log R) and are negligible compared with the leading O⁡(N)O(N) term for large NN. On the other hand, H⁡(q)≤log⁡RH(q)\\leq\\log R with equality only in the perfectly balanced case nr=N/R∀rn\_{r}=N/R~\\forall r. This implies that, in the limit N→∞N\\to\\infty, any profile whose entropy is noticably below log⁡R\\log R is exponentially supressed in NN, so that the multinomial (entropy) term overwhelmingly favors near-uniform partitions.
