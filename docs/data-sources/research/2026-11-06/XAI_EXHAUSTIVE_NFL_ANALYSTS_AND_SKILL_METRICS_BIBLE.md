## PART II — MATHEMATICAL FORMULAS & COUNTING RULES FOR QB, WR/TE & RB METRICS

### Section A — Quarterback Metrics (`EPA`, `CPOE`, `DAKOTA`, `ANY/A`, `BTT%`, `TWP%`, `P2S%`, `1READ%`, `AYTS`, `PACR`, `AGG%`)

#### `QB-01` — Expected Points Added per Play (EPA/play) & per Dropback (EPA/DB)
- **Mathematical Formula:** `EPA_t = EP(s_{t+1}) - EP(s_t), where EP(s_t) = sum_{k in {TD,+7, FG,+3, Safety,+2, NoScore,0, OppSafety,-2, OppFG,-3, OppTD,-7}} v_k * P_GBDT(NextScore = k | down, ydstogo, yardline_100, half_sec_rem, timeouts, roof, era)`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** Excludes QB kneels, spikes, and two-point conversions. EPA/DB includes pass attempts, sacks, and QB scrambles (excludes designed QB rushes unless computing total QB EPA/play). Fumbles lost assign turnover EP delta; nflfastR optionally supports expected fumble recovery weighting.
- **Garbage-Time & Situational Filter:** Standard Baldwin/nflfastR filter: keep plays where 0.05 <= WP <= 0.95 (or 0.10 <= WP <= 0.90 for strict neutral scripts).

#### `QB-02` — Completion Percentage Over Expected (CPOE — nflfastR & NGS Tracking)
- **Mathematical Formula:** `CPOE = (1 / N_valid) * sum_{i=1}^{N_valid} 100 * (I(Completed_i) - P_hat(Complete_i | x_i))`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** Excludes spikes, throwaways (in NGS), and batted passes at the line of scrimmage. nflfastR xCOMP uses air_yards, pass_location, qb_hit, roof, era; NGS 10Hz xCOMP uses target separation at throw/arrival, nearest defender closing velocity, sideline distance, pass air distance, and pass rush pressure distance.
- **Garbage-Time & Situational Filter:** Evaluated across all valid targeted pass attempts; predictive stability peaks when weighted with EPA/play via DAKOTA.

#### `QB-03` — DAKOTA (Adjusted EPA + CPOE Composite)
- **Mathematical Formula:** `DAKOTA = 0.20 * (EPA_per_play) + 0.009 * (CPOE) + 0.09`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** Fitted by Ben Baldwin to maximize out-of-sample R^2 predicting future QB EPA/play. Combines process accuracy (CPOE) with play value realization (EPA/play).
- **Garbage-Time & Situational Filter:** Computed on 5%-95% Win Probability non-garbage-time dropbacks.

#### `QB-04` — Adjusted Net Yards per Attempt (ANY/A)
- **Mathematical Formula:** `ANY/A = (Pass_Yards + 20 * Pass_TD - 45 * INT - Sack_Yards_Lost) / (Pass_Attempts + Sacks)`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** Denominator is Pass Attempts + Sacks (excludes scrambles). Rewards TDs (+20 yds), penalizes INTs (-45 yds) and sack yardage.
- **Garbage-Time & Situational Filter:** Traditional box-score metric; GSE residualizes ANY/A against opponent pass defense DVOA and garbage-time share.

#### `QB-05` — PFF Big-Time Throw Rate (BTT%) & Turnover-Worthy Play Rate (TWP%)
- **Mathematical Formula:** `BTT% = Big_Time_Throws / Pass_Attempts; TWP% = Turnover_Worthy_Plays / (Pass_Attempts + Sacks + Scrambles)`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** CRITICAL DENOMINATOR ASYMMETRY: BTT% denominator is Pass Attempts (since a QB cannot make a Big-Time Throw on a sack), whereas TWP% denominator is Total Dropbacks (Pass Attempts + Sacks + Designed/Scramble Runs with fumble risk) because strip-sacks in the pocket count as Turnover-Worthy Plays.
- **Garbage-Time & Situational Filter:** Film-charted on every snap; strips hail-mary end-of-half heaves from TWP%.

#### `QB-06` — Pressure-to-Sack Rate (P2S%) & @PattonAnalytics Pocket Survival Curve
- **Mathematical Formula:** `P2S% = Sacks / Pressured_Dropbacks; S_pocket(t) = prod_{t_i <= t} (1 - d_i / Y_i), PrOE = P2S%_observed - E[P2S% | TTT, OL_PBWR, Blitz_Rate]`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** Pressured dropbacks = Sacks + Hits + Hurries. P2S% is a stable quarterback trait (year-over-year r ~ 0.55) measuring pocket presence and sack avoidance, whereas raw Pressure Rate mixes OL blocking + QB holding the ball.
- **Garbage-Time & Situational Filter:** Evaluated on True Pass Sets (excluding screens, RPOs, and <1.5s quick-game throws).

#### `QB-07` — First-Read Target Share (1READ% — @FantasyPtsData) & Progression EPA
- **Mathematical Formula:** `1READ% = First_Read_Targets / Total_Aimed_Targets`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** Charted from All-22 QB head/shoulder/footwork alignment at the top of the drop. Separates QBs who lock onto primary reads from QBs who work to 2nd/3rd progressions vs post-snap shell rotation.
- **Garbage-Time & Situational Filter:** Split by pre-snap static vs post-snap rotated coverage shells.

#### `QB-08` — Air Yards to the Sticks (AYTS), PACR & NGS Aggressiveness (AGG%)
- **Mathematical Formula:** `AYTS = aDOT - ydstogo; PACR = Passing_Yards / Total_Air_Yards; AGG% = Tight_Window_Attempts(sep <= 1.0 yd) / Total_Pass_Attempts`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** aDOT includes incomplete and complete passes (excludes throwaways and spikes in SIS/FTN). AGG% uses 10Hz Zebra RFID receiver-to-nearest-defender Euclidean distance <= 1.0 yard at pass arrival.
- **Garbage-Time & Situational Filter:** Evaluated on 1st/2nd/3rd down normal script dropbacks.

### Section B — Wide Receiver & Tight End Metrics (`YPRR`, `TPRR`, `WOPR`, `RACR`, `ASS`, `SEP SCORE`, `ESPN RTM`, `Reception Perception`, `NGS`)

#### `WR-01` — Yards Per Route Run (YPRR) & Targets Per Route Run (TPRR)
- **Mathematical Formula:** `YPRR = Receiving_Yards / Routes_Run; TPRR = Targets / Routes_Run; 1D/RR = Receiving_First_Downs / Routes_Run`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** Routes Run = pass plays where the player is on the field and runs a receiving pattern (excludes run plays, pass-block snaps, spikes, and kneels). YPRR >= 2.20 indicates elite alpha efficiency; TPRR >= 0.24 indicates elite target-earning ability. Split into YPRR vs Man, YPRR vs Zone, YPRR vs Single-High (Cover-1/3), and YPRR vs Two-High (Cover-2/4/6).

#### `WR-02` — Weighted Opportunity Rating (WOPR) & Receiver Air Conversion Ratio (RACR)
- **Mathematical Formula:** `WOPR = 1.5 * Target_Share + 0.7 * Air_Yards_Share; RACR = Receiving_Yards / Total_Air_Yards`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** Created by Josh Hermsmeyer. Target Share = Player Targets / Team Aimed Pass Attempts; Air Yards Share = Player Air Yards / Team Total Air Yards. WOPR >= 0.65 denotes alpha WR1 opportunity volume.

#### `WR-03` — @FantasyPtsData Average Separation Score (ASS) & SEP SCORE
- **Mathematical Formula:** `ASS = (1 / N_routes) * sum_{r=1}^{N_routes} s_r, where s_r in [-2.0, +2.0] charted at route break / QB top-of-drop`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** Unlike NGS Target Separation (which only measures targeted passes and is biased by zone soft-spots and blown coverages), ASS and SEP SCORE evaluate EVERY route run regardless of whether the QB threw the ball to that receiver, adjusted for route concept and Man vs Zone coverage.

#### `WR-04` — ESPN Receiver Tracking Metrics (RTM: Open Score, Catch Score, YAC Score)
- **Mathematical Formula:** `Open_Score = Percentile_0_99(sum_{routes} (Separation_vs_Expected | Route_Type, Coverage_Shell, Depth)); Catch_Score = Percentile_0_99(CROE); YAC_Score = Percentile_0_99(YACOE)`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** Built by Seth Walder & Brian Burke on 10Hz NGS tracking across all routes run. Overall RTM weights Open Score (~50%), Catch Score (~30%), and YAC Score (~20%).

#### `WR-05` — Matt Harmon Reception Perception (9-Route Success Rate vs Man/Zone/Press/Double)
- **Mathematical Formula:** `SR_RP(c) = Routes_Won_in_Coverage(c) / Total_Charted_Routes_in_Coverage(c), for c in {Man, Zone, Press, Double}`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** 8-game All-22 charting sample grading every route across the 9-route tree (Flat, Slant, Comeback, Curl, Out, Dig/In, Corner, Post, Go/Nine) independent of QB accuracy or target delivery.

#### `WR-06` — NGS Cushion, Target Separation, Expected YAC (xYAC) & YAC Over Expected (YACOE)
- **Mathematical Formula:** `Cushion = ||x_WR(t_snap) - x_CB(t_snap)||_2; Separation = ||x_WR(t_arrival) - x_Def_min(t_arrival)||_2; YACOE = Actual_YAC - E[YAC | Catch_Coords, 22_Player_Tracking_at_Catch]`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** Computed at 10Hz from Zebra RFID shoulder-pad transponders. High Cushion + High YACOE identifies screen/schemed-touch merchants vs true downfield separators.

#### `WR-07` — Contested Catch Rate, On-Target Drop Rate & Primary CB FP/rt Allowed
- **Mathematical Formula:** `Contested_Catch_% = Contested_Catches / Contested_Targets; On_Target_Drop_% = Drops / Catchable_Targets; CB_FP_per_rt = Fantasy_Pts_Allowed_in_Coverage / Coverage_Routes_Tracked`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** Cross-referenced in Stage 2 of GSE against opponent Man/Zone % and primary cornerback shadow rate.

### Section C — Running Back & Rushing Metrics (`NGS RYOE 1D-CNN`, `ROE%`, `YBC/Att`, `YACo/Att`, `MTF/Att`, `Elusive Rating`, `ALY`, `Box Counts`, `xFP`)

#### `RB-01` — NFL Next Gen Stats Rushing Yards Over Expected (RYOE) & Rushes Over Expected % (ROE%)
- **Mathematical Formula:** `xRY_i = sum_{y=-10}^{99} y * P_1DCNN(Y = y | T_handoff_i in R^{10 x 11 x 10}); RYOE_i = Actual_Yards_i - xRY_i; ROE% = (1 / N_att) * sum I(Actual_Yards_i > xRY_i)`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** Derived from the 2020 Big Data Bowl winning 'The Zoo' 1D-CNN spatial architecture. Evaluates the 10Hz tracking snapshot at the exact frame of handoff (relative positions, velocities, and closing angles of all 10 offensive blockers and 11 defenders relative to the RB). Isolates RB vision/burst/tackle-breaking from OL run blocking.

#### `RB-02` — Yards Before Contact per Attempt (YBC/Att) vs Yards After Contact per Attempt (YACo/Att)
- **Mathematical Formula:** `Total_Rush_Yards = YBC + YACo; YBC/Att = YBC / Rush_Att; YACo/Att = YACo / Rush_Att`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** YBC/Att primarily reflects offensive line displacement, scheme space, and light-box advantage; YACo/Att reflects RB contact balance, leg drive, and tackle evasion.

#### `RB-03` — Missed Tackles Forced per Attempt (MTF/Att) & PFF Elusive Rating
- **Mathematical Formula:** `MTF/Att = Missed_Tackles_Forced_Rush / Designed_Rush_Att; Elusive_Rating = ((MTF_Rush + MTF_Rec) / (Designed_Rush_Att + Receptions)) * YACo_per_Att * 100`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** Combines missed-tackle frequency across rushing and receiving touches with yards after contact per carry.

#### `RB-04` — FTN Adjusted Line Yards (ALY), Stuff Rate, Power Success & Open-Field Yards
- **Mathematical Formula:** `Line_Yards_Raw = 1.20 * min(y, 0) + 1.00 * clip(y, 0, 4) + 0.50 * clip(y - 4, 0, 6) + 0.00 * max(y - 10, 0); Stuff_Rate = I(y <= 0) / N_att`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** Attributes 120% of negative runs, 100% of first 4 yards, 50% of yards 5-10, and 0% of yards 11+ to the offensive line, adjusted for down, distance, situation, and opponent defensive front.

#### `RB-05` — Defenders in the Box (8+ Stacked Box % vs <=6 Light Box %) & Run Concept Splits
- **Mathematical Formula:** `Box_Advantage_Delta = EPA_per_Rush(Light_Box_<=6) - EPA_per_Rush(Heavy_Box_>=8)`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** Split across Gap/Man concepts (Duo, Power, Counter GT/GY, Trap, Pin-and-Pull) vs Zone concepts (Inside Zone, Outside/Wide Zone) and cross-matched against defensive front structure (Odd/Tite 3-4 vs Even 4-2-5 Nickel).

#### `RB-06` — Expected Fantasy Points per Game (XFP/G — @KoalatyStats / @HaydenWinks) & High-Value Touches
- **Mathematical Formula:** `xFP = sum_{carries c} E[FP | yardline_100_c, down_c] + sum_{targets t} E[FP | air_yards_t, yardline_100_t, aDOT_t]; Diff = FPPG - XFPPG`
- **Denominator & Counting Rules:** An Inside-5 (Green-Zone) carry is worth ~3.2x a between-the-20s carry (~1.45 xFP vs ~0.45 xFP), and an RB target is worth ~2.6x a between-the-20s carry (~1.18 xFP). Tracks Snap %, Route Participation %, 2-Minute Drill %, LDD %, and Inside-5 Carry %.

## PART III — DEEP SECOND- & THIRD-ORDER DYNAMIC MECHANICS, CAUSAL CONFOUNDER DECONSTRUCTION & HYSTERESIS EQUATIONS

### `DYN-01` — Frisch-Waugh-Lovell (FWL) & Double-ML Orthogonalization of Confounded Surface Metrics (Causal Confounder Deconstruction (Why Surface Metrics Lie))
- **Why the Surface Metric Lies (Confounder Mechanism):** Raw Time-to-Throw (TTT) conflates RPO/screen/bootleg design with QB processing speed; raw CPOE conflates scheme-manufactured play-action layups (separation >= 4.0 yds) with tight-window placement; raw YPRR vs Man conflates slot/bunch/motion free releases with isolated X-receiver boundary press-man against shadow CB1s; raw NGS RYOE conflates QB read-option mesh-point backside DE freeze time with RB talent.
- **True Structural / Orthogonalized Equation:** `Y_tilde = Y - E[Y | Z_scheme, Z_motion, Z_release, Z_box, Z_mesh_freeze]; theta_hat_skill = (X_tilde^T X_tilde)^{-1} X_tilde^T Y_tilde`
- **Engine Feature Variables:** `true_progression_latency_ms (TTT stripped of RPO, screen, and designed rollout snaps)`, `tight_window_cpoe_under_2yd_sep (CPOE restricted to arrival separation <= 2.0 yards)`, `isolated_boundary_press_man_yprr (YPRR on non-motion, non-bunch boundary press alignments vs CB1)`, `mesh_adjusted_ryoe (RYOE residualized against backside edge defender read-option freeze duration ms)`

### `DYN-02` — Non-Stationary Hawkes Post-Hit Trauma Hysteresis Kernel (H_t) (QB Cognitive Processing & Within-Game Trauma Hysteresis)
- **Why the Surface Metric Lies (Confounder Mechanism):** Season-long clean-pocket EPA assumes a quarterback's clean-pocket performance is stationary across all four quarters regardless of how many interior hits he absorbed earlier in the game.
- **True Structural / Orthogonalized Equation:** `H_t = sum_{k < t} (1.65 * I(InteriorHit_k) + 0.85 * I(EdgeSack_k)) * exp(-lambda_decay * (t - t_k)); TTT_clean(t) = TTT_clean_base - gamma_panic * H_t; P(PhantomBailout_t | CleanPocket_t) = sigmoid(beta_0 + beta_1 * H_t)`
- **Engine Feature Variables:** `accumulated_interior_hit_trauma_h_t (exponentially weighted within-game A/B-gap hit & sack load)`, `clean_pocket_ttt_compression_sec (drop in clean-pocket TTT after taking >= 3 early interior hits)`, `phantom_pressure_bailout_rate (rate of breaking a clean pocket at t < 2.2s due to internal clock panic)`, `post_hit_checkdown_reflex_delta (spike in RB/TE checkdown rate below sticks on clean dropbacks)`

### `DYN-03` — Safety Shell KL Divergence x QB First-Read Lock-On Collapse (Post-Snap Safety Spin Disguise & Saccade Progression Hazard)
- **Why the Surface Metric Lies (Confounder Mechanism):** High overall EPA/play and high 1READ% in favorable play-action scripts disguise a quarterback's inability to reset his eyes when a defense rotates post-snap from Two-High (MOFO) to Single-High Robber (MOFC) or drops an edge rusher into a passing lane (Creeper / Simulated Pressure).
- **True Structural / Orthogonalized Equation:** `TWP_hazard(t) = TWP_base * exp(eta_1 * D_KL(P_post || P_pre) * LockOnIndex_QB + eta_2 * I(SimPressure_t))`
- **Engine Feature Variables:** `shell_disguise_kl_divergence_nats (KL divergence between t = -1.0s pre-snap and t = +1.5s post-snap safety shell)`, `post_rotation_progression_epa_drop (EPA delta when forced off 1st read by late safety spin)`, `creeper_simulated_pressure_sack_rate (sack & TWP rate vs 4-man rush from 6-man pre-snap presentation)`

### `DYN-04` — Stem Pacing Variance, Center-of-Mass Hip-Sink Braking (BRK+) & Blind-Spot Entry (WR/TE Micro-Route Biomechanics & Coverage-Leverage Manipulation)
- **Why the Surface Metric Lies (Confounder Mechanism):** 40-yard dash top speed and average NGS target separation fail to capture how receivers actually create separation at the break point: deceleration impulse (hip sink) and attacking the defensive back's blind spot.
- **True Structural / Orthogonalized Equation:** `BRK_plus = 100 + 4.5 * ((v_entry - v_exit) / Delta_t_break - 6.0); P(RouteWin) = sigmoid(alpha_0 + alpha_1 * BRK_plus + alpha_2 * cos(theta_DB_gaze - theta_WR_stem) + alpha_3 * PressReleaseWin_0_5yd)`
- **Engine Feature Variables:** `braking_efficiency_brk_plus (10Hz deceleration impulse over the 300ms window prior to route break)`, `press_release_win_rate_800ms (win rate clearing press jam within 0.8s / 5 yards of snap)`, `blind_spot_leverage_entry_rate (rate of entering zone/trail DB's >110-deg visual blind spot before head nod)`, `hot_route_sight_adjust_sync_rate (completion rate on sight-adjusted hot throws vs Cover-0 / slot blitz)`

### `DYN-05` — Pass-Protection Blitz Pick-Up Veto Gate & Collision Angle YACo Decomposition (RB Vision, Pace-to-Hole, Contact Angle Physics & Pass-Pro Veto Gate)
- **Why the Surface Metric Lies (Confounder Mechanism):** Projected RB volume based solely on rushing YPC or RYOE collapses whenever a back blows a blitz pickup (triggering an immediate coaching benching on 3rd downs and 2-minute drills), and raw YACo/Att treats a glancing 60-degree arm tackle the same as a 0-degree squared-up linebacker collision.
- **True Structural / Orthogonalized Equation:** `SnapShare_PassingDowns = SnapShare_base * I(PassBlockFailRate <= tau_coach_veto); TrueContactBalance = sum_i (YACo_i * cos(theta_impact_i) * (m_Def_i * v_Def_i) / (m_RB * v_RB_i)) / N_contacts`
- **Engine Feature Variables:** `rb_pass_pro_blitz_pickup_fail_rate (blown blitz assignment + sack/hit allowed rate per pass-block snap)`, `pre_hole_patience_velocity_ratio (ratio of 0-2yd mesh speed to 3-6yd burst speed on Duo/Inside Zone)`, `collision_angle_adjusted_yaco (yards after contact weighted by cosine of defender impact angle and momentum)`

### Next Gen Stats (@NextGenStats and official NFL sources)
NGS uses shoulder-pad sensors for precise tracking. Metrics derive from raw XY/speed/acceleration data plus models (e.g., completion probability, expected yards).[[1]](https://nextgenstats.nfl.com/glossary)[[6]](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/nflreadr/vignettes/dictionary_nextgen_stats.html)

**Key passing metrics and rules**:
- Time to Throw (TT or avg_time_to_throw): Average time from snap to throw on pass attempts (sacks excluded). Denominator: pass attempts.
- Completed Air Yards (CAY) / Intended Air Yards (IAY): Vertical yards from LOS to catch point (or target point) at arrival. CAY averages on completions; IAY on all attempts. Air Yards Differential (AYD) = CAY – IAY.
- Aggressiveness (AGG%): % of attempts into tight coverage (defender ≤1 yard from receiver at catch/incompletion point). Denominator: pass attempts.
- Completion Probability / Expected Completion % (xCOMP) / CPOE (+/-): ML model estimating completion likelihood based on depth, separation, defender proximity, location, speed, and related metrics (updated versions add occlusion-aware features). CPOE = actual comp% – xCOMP.
- Other: Air Yards to the Sticks (AYTS), Longest Completed Air Distance (LCAD), Yards After Catch (YAC), xYAC, YAC Above Expectation.

**Rushing metrics**:
- Efficiency (EFF): Total distance traveled by ball-carrier (per NGS tracking) per yard gained. Lower = more north-south. Denominator: rushing attempts/yards gained.
- 8+ Defenders in the Box (8+D%): % of rushes vs. 8+ defenders stacked in the box at snap (NGS calculates box count via positions). Denominator: rushing attempts.
- Avg Time Behind Line of Scrimmage (TLOS); Expected Rushing Yards (xRY), Rush Yards Over Expected (RYOE), First Down/Touchdown Probability models.

**Receiving**:
- Average Cushion (CUSH), Average Separation (SEP), Targeted Air Yards (TAY), % Share of Team’s Air Yards (TAY%), YAC/xYAC.

**Scheme/motion/coverage charting**:
- Run scheme classification (newer models): 16 labels (e.g., inside zone, duo, power, counter) via transformer on spatial/temporal player relationships; tags like man/zone/gap, read option, split zone, pitch; identifies intended vs. actual gap.[[7]](https://www.nfl.com/news/next-gen-stats-new-advanced-metrics-you-need-to-know-for-the-2026-nfl-season)
- Offensive Shift and Motion Classification: Pre-snap motion, motion at snap, shifts; expanded to 19 advanced types (jet, orbit, fly, glide, exit).[[8]](https://www.nfl.com/news-migrated-v2/next-gen-stats-new-advanced-metrics-you-need-to-know-for-the-2025-nfl-season)
- Coverage Responsibility: Closest defender to receiver at pass arrival (with updates for occlusion/contesting).
- Other models: Chip blocks, run blocking assignments/types (pull, down, crack), tackle probability, Passing Score (composite of multiple models), Big Play Score.

**Situational filters**: Down/distance, score/time, personnel, formation, field position, game situation. Box counts and motion are snap-timed.

### PFF (@PFF, @PFF_Moo and related)
PFF uses extensive human charting of every play for scheme, personnel, and grades. They track 100s of fields including formations, motions/shifts (with start/end points), run concepts, POA (point of attack), protections, coverages, pressures, stunts, and defender depths/alignments.[[9]](https://media.pff.com/2018/08/PFF-Play-Data-Fields.pdf)[[2]](https://www.pff.com/news/pro-pff-signature-statistics-a-glossary)

**Charting definitions**:
- Play-action: Faking a run play before passing to open lanes. Tracked separately; stats often split PA vs. non-PA (e.g., comp% boost on PA).[[2]](https://www.pff.com/news/pro-pff-signature-statistics-a-glossary)
- Screen: Specific pass type (often with motion); tracked distinctly; high motion association (e.g., >75% of screens preceded by pre-snap movement in recent seasons).[[10]](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-pre-snap-motion-usage-reaches-new-highs-across-the-nfl)
- Motion at snap / pre-snap motion/shifts: Charted with timing, type (jet, orbit, and related metrics), and usage rates. Pre-snap motion usage has risen league-wide; often used for info-gathering or creating mismatches.
- Blitz: Extra rushers (typically 5+ or DB rushing the passer). Tracked as % of dropbacks; separate from 4-man pressure.[[11]](https://www.pff.com/news/qbs-in-focus-tendencies)
- Stunt: Pass rush stunts/twists/slants (e.g., loops, enters). Charted under pass rush details.
- Light/heavy box: Players in the box (within ~8 yards of LOS at snap for relevant positions like safeties); heavy often implies loaded fronts (e.g., 8+). Run-stop % split by box alignment.[[2]](https://www.pff.com/news/pro-pff-signature-statistics-a-glossary)
- Coverages: Charted schemes including Cover-0/1/2/3/4/6 (one-high vs. two-high; man vs. zone elements; specifics like robber, buzz). Graded vs. each (e.g., QB grades by coverage). Middle of field open/closed noted.[[12]](https://x.com/PFF/status/2080746299503333805)

**Other metrics/grades**: Signature stats split by PA, blitz, screen, depth, and related metrics Time in pocket (quick vs. longer drops). Run concepts, intended/actual POA, double teams, route details/combinations, double moves/chips. Grades isolate player responsibility (e.g., QB: read/decision/throw; not just result).

**Denominators/filters**: Per dropback, per target, per snap, per route; situational (down/distance, personnel like 11/12/21, formation, red zone, two-minute). Screens often filtered separately due to unique dynamics.

### Wave-1C: Film-Meets-Data Scheme Analysts, Trench Specialists, Cap/Contract & Injury Epidemiologists (`grok-4.7` · `completed` · `163.89s` · `$0.2950`)

**What those terms mean, so the catalog stays precise**

- A scheme tell is a pre-snap cue (alignment, stem, motion response, protection declaration) that raises the probability of a play or coverage.
- A creeper, or replacement pressure, keeps four rushers: one drops, another replaces him.
- A simulated pressure disguises the rush so the quarterback sees a blitz threat while the defense keeps a full coverage count, or stacks late movement on a four-man path.
- Saban Rip/Liz Match-3 is pattern-match Cover 3 (or Cover 6 on a weak rotation). Rip and Liz tell the defense which way the safety is rotating. Vertical stems are matched. Routes that settle underneath are zoned off. The corner owns No. 1 unless that receiver is short or under inside about five yards, in which case a smash call passes him to the flat defender and the corner takes No. 2 vertical.
- Quarters is split-field Cover 4 match, with paired readers on No. 2. Palms (Saban’s Clamp is a close cousin) brackets No. 2, often with the corner taking No. 2 out early. A poach is a safety leaving his side to rob or double a vertical from the other side.
- Pass-set depth is how far an offensive lineman gets off the line on a vertical, 45-degree, or jump set before contact.
- Return-to-play performance decay is the drop in snaps, speed, or production relative to that player’s own baseline after he is cleared.

### Wave-1D: Causal Confounder Deconstruction — Why Surface NFL Metrics Lie & How Sharp Quants Residualize Them (`grok-4.20-0309-reasoning` · `completed` · `53.96s` · `$0.0910`)

**Public NFL metrics are surface statistics saturated with scheme, situation, pre-snap alignment, motion, and play-type confounders.** They systematically credit (or penalize) players for offensive design rather than isolating individual skill or talent. True causal identification of quarterback accuracy, processing latency, receiver separation creation, or runner vision requires rigorous residualization—orthogonalizing the outcome against the high-dimensional scheme and situational features that manufacture easy opportunities.[[1]](https://cheddarheadanalytics.com/learn/cpoe-explained)[[2]](https://www.sharpfootballanalysis.com/analysis/nfl-tight-windows-mahomes-mcvay/)

This is not minor noise. Shanahan/McVay/Ben Johnson-style systems (heavy motion, play-action boots/crossers, RPOs, bunch/stack alignments, crack schemes) generate wide-open layups and free releases at far higher rates than traditional pro-style or isolated-press schemes. Metrics that ignore this inflate QBs and receivers in those systems while undervaluing talent operating in tighter, slower-developing structures. Residualization via Frisch-Waugh-Lovell (FWL) or Double/Debiased Machine Learning (DML) is required to strip these confounders.[[3]](https://laxmandahal.github.io/double_machine_learning/orthogonal_DML.html)[[4]](https://towardsdatascience.com/double-debiased-machine-learning-part-2-bf990720a0b2/)

### 1. Time to Throw (TTT) is heavily confounded by RPO rate, screen rate, play-action bootlegs, and 1-read quick game versus true full-field progression latency
Raw TTT (snap to release) mixes apples and oranges. RPOs often require only a simple post-snap read (or pre-snap box count), producing releases around 1.17–1.5 seconds (e.g., high-volume RPO QBs like Mahomes). Screens and quick-game hitches/slants are 1-read, immediate-throw concepts by design. Play-action boots add a fake handoff plus roll-out but frequently pair with quick, high-low reads or check-downs rather than full-field progression. League-average TTT has risen (reaching ~2.86s in recent seasons), partly from declining RPO usage in some systems and improved defensive disguise, but this masks massive scheme heterogeneity.[[5]](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-analyzing-the-effectiveness-of-rpos-over-the-last-five-nfl-seasons)[[6]](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-quarterbacks-are-holding-the-ball-longer-than-ever-the-trends-behind-time-to-throw)

Mechanistically: In true 4-read, full-field dropback from under center against disguised coverage, the QB must process multiple layers, landmarks, and post-snap rotations—adding 0.7–1.2+ seconds of latency. RPO/quick-game eliminates downstream reads; the mesh point or bubble decision is binary and often pre-determined. Play-action freezes second-level defenders but the boot itself can shorten effective decision time by altering launch angle and receiver stems. Bootlegs also correlate with specific route packages (e.g., flood or swing routes) that develop faster than deep crossers or layered concepts.

A raw TTT leader in a quick-game/RPO-heavy offense is not necessarily faster-processing than a longer-TTT QB in a slow-developing, under-center, play-action system. The metric lies without conditioning on play type (RPO vs. dropback), drop depth, play-action usage, route concept depth, and number of reads.

### 2. CPOE and EPA/play are confounded by scheme-manufactured wide-open layups versus true tight-window ball placement
CPOE = actual completion % − expected completion % (modeled on air yards, separation at catch point, pressure, location, scramble). It attempts to credit difficulty but still credits the QB for *how the separation was created*. Shanahan-tree schemes (motion at 50–68% rates for many teams, high play-action on early downs/earned first downs, crossers, bunch/stack alignments, crack blocks) routinely manufacture ≥4 yards of separation via coverage conflicts, linebacker freezes, and horizontal stretching. Play-action alone adds ~+0.115 EPA/play versus baseline dropbacks. Motion and “earned” first-down play-action create layups that inflate both completion% and EPA while lowering the expected completion baseline.[[7]](https://theramswire.usatoday.com/story/sports/nfl/rams/2026/07/07/data-shows-why-sean-mcvays-play-action-passing-is-so-elite/90833117007/)[[8]](https://www.fantasypoints.com/nfl/articles/2026/how-to-be-an-elite-playcaller-nfl-efficiency-hacks)

Mechanistically: A crosser or Y-cross concept with pre-snap motion can bust zone or create rub/pick elements against man, producing wide-open intermediate windows. The QB throws to a receiver with 4–7 yards of separation created by scheme, not elite anticipation or placement under duress. Tight-window throws (≤1 yard separation, especially deep or sideline, under pressure) are far harder but rarer in optimized schemes. CPOE partially controls for catch-point separation, yet that separation is partly a receiver skill *and* a strong function of scheme/alignment; the model does not fully disentangle pre-snap decision-making or coverage busts from pure placement talent. EPA/play compounds this by rewarding the easy YAC and explosive plays manufactured by the scheme.[[1]](https://cheddarheadanalytics.com/learn/cpoe-explained)[[9]](https://www.sportsinfosolutions.com/2024/09/05/using-accuracy-and-openness-to-provide-context-for-receiver-play/)

Result: QBs in motion/PA-heavy systems post inflated CPOE/EPA composites that overstate individual accuracy and decision-making relative to peers throwing into structure-less, press-man, or disguised looks without those manufactured advantages. Raw or even Next Gen-adjusted versions lie unless residualized.

### 3. YPRR vs Man is confounded by slot/bunch/stack/motion free releases against off-coverage versus isolated X-receiver boundary press-man alignment against elite CB1 shadow coverage
Yards Per Route Run versus man coverage appears to measure receiver talent against tight coverage but is dominated by alignment and release ease. Slot/bunch/stack formations plus motion create “free” or contested releases against off-coverage or inside leverage; defenders are conflicted by multiple stems, rubs, or assignment switches. Boundary X-receivers aligned wide against press-man from elite CB1s (shadow technique, physical jams at the line) face far higher disruption rates, slower releases, and reduced target volume. Data show massive spreads in YPRR vs. man driven by these factors rather than pure separation skill.[[10]](https://x.com/throwthedamball/status/1991522639857479973)

Mechanistically: Motion shifts defenders’ pre-snap leverage and creates “bang-bang” conflicts at the snap. Bunch/stack compresses routes, allowing inside releases or rubs that defeat man coverage geometry. Isolated boundary press against a long, physical CB1 forces the receiver to win with hand usage, speed, and route stem against mirror or trail technique—fundamentally harder. YPRR vs. man therefore largely reflects usage (how often a receiver is schemed into easy releases) more than talent. Without conditioning on alignment (boundary vs. slot), press rate faced, motion involvement, and CB1 quality, the metric ranks scheme beneficiaries far above isolated talents.

### 4. Next Gen Stats RYOE is confounded by Quarterback read-option mesh-point freeze time on the backside edge defender (Backside Conflict Freeze ms) and wide receiver crack-block angles occurring after handoff
RYOE measures rushing yards minus expected yards given tracking data on blocker/defender positions, speeds, and directions at handoff. It improves on raw YPC by crediting the back for creating yards beyond the blocking, but read-option/RPO dynamics introduce unmeasured or partially measured pre- and post-handoff elements. The QB’s mesh-point hold time (“Backside Conflict Freeze ms”) freezes the backside edge or linebacker; longer freeze times pull that defender further or slower, improving the frontside gap. Crack blocks by WRs or tight ends (often after handoff) alter pursuit angles from the second and third levels. If the RYOE model’s expectation is built primarily at handoff without fully capturing the duration of the QB’s read hold or the precise timing/angle of crack blocks, the expectation understates the scheme’s contribution.[[11]](https://sticktothemodel.com/encyclopedia/rushing-yards-over-expected)[[12]](https://www.sportsrec.com/explainers/pff-run-blocking-metrics-explained-vs-nfls-ai-model/)

Mechanistically: In zone-read or RPO, the QB’s eyes and ball carriage at the mesh create a true conflict; the edge cannot crash without risking the pull. Extended freeze time (hundreds of milliseconds) changes defender momentum and gap responsibility in ways a static handoff-position model may average out rather than isolate. Crack blocks change force angles post-handoff, creating cutback lanes or sealing pursuit. Thus, high RYOE runners in heavy read-option or crack-heavy schemes receive partial credit for QB action and WR blocking that standard tracking at handoff does not fully orthogonalize. The metric improves on raw yards but still lies for scheme-dependent rushers without additional residualization on read-option rate, mesh timing proxies, and crack usage.

### 5. Exact Frisch-Waugh-Lovell (FWL) and Double/Debiased Machine Learning (DML) orthogonalization equations to strip scheme confounders from player skill talent
Let *Y* = raw metric (TTT, CPOE, EPA/play, YPRR vs. man, RYOE/att). Let *Z* = high-dimensional vector of scheme/situation confounders (RPO indicator or rate, screen rate, play-action/boot indicator, motion rate, bunch/stack/slot alignment dummies, drop depth, down/distance, personnel, boundary vs. slot usage, press rate faced, CB1 quality proxy, read-option mesh indicators, crack-block rate, and related metrics). Let *D* = player talent/skill (player fixed effects, or a talent proxy). Goal: estimate partial effect of player talent orthogonal to scheme.

**FWL orthogonalization (exact equivalence to full controlled regression):**  
Regress *Y* on *Z*:  
*Y = Zγ̂ + ε̂_Y* → residuals ε̂_Y = Y − proj_Z(Y) (purged of scheme).  
Regress *D* on *Z*:  
*D = Zδ̂ + ε̂_D* → residuals ε̂_D = D − proj_Z(D).  
Then:  
*ε̂_Y = θ ε̂_D + ν*  
The coefficient θ is the scheme-adjusted player effect. Equivalently, average the ε̂_Y per player after residualizing the metric on a flexible function of all scheme variables (linear, splines, or interacted). This exactly removes the linear projection of scheme from the metric.[[3]](https://laxmandahal.github.io/double_machine_learning/orthogonal_DML.html)

**Double/Debiased Machine Learning (DML) for high-dimensional, nonlinear, or interactive confounders (preferred when |Z| is large or f(Z) is complex):**  
Use sample splitting (K-fold or 50/50 auxiliary/main). On auxiliary sample, use flexible ML (random forests, boosting, neural nets, or ensemble) to estimate:  
ĝ(Z) ≈ E[Y | Z] (outcome model)  
m̂(Z) ≈ E[D | Z] (treatment/propensity model).  

On main sample compute orthogonalized residuals:  
Ỹ = Y − ĝ(Z)  
D̃ = D − m̂(Z).  

Then solve the moment condition or regress:  
Ỹ = θ D̃ + error  
(or use the efficient score / Neyman orthogonal score for robustness). Cross-fit across folds and average. This yields root-N consistent, asymptotically normal estimates of θ even with high-dimensional or nonparametric nuisance functions, provided the ML rates satisfy mild conditions (Neyman orthogonality from the double residualization). In practice, include player indicators in *D* or estimate player-specific intercepts after orthogonalization.[[4]](https://towardsdatascience.com/double-debiased-machine-learning-part-2-bf990720a0b2/)

These steps produce scheme- and situation-adjusted versions of every metric: residual TTT (processing speed net of quick-game/RPO/PA), residual CPOE/EPA (placement and decision value net of manufactured separation), residual YPRR vs. man (creation net of free releases), residual RYOE (creation net of freeze time and crack geometry). Without them, public metrics lie by conflating coaching scheme with player talent. Rigorous deconstruction demands exactly this orthogonalization; anything less is storytelling.

### Wave-2A: Quarterback Efficiency, Value & Composite Metrics (EPA, CPOE, DAKOTA, ANY/A, QBR) (`grok-4-latest` · `completed` · `239.74s` · `$0.0565`)

**EPA/play and EPA/dropback** derive from expected points (EP) models in packages like nflfastR (open-source) and proprietary variants (e.g., rbsdm.com). EPA for a play equals EP_after minus EP_before, where EP_before/after are modeled probabilities of scoring outcomes (touchdown, field goal, safety, no score) from the current situation.[[1]](https://github.com/sportsdataverse/cfbfastR/blob/main/vignettes/college-football-expected-points-model-fundamentals-part-iv.Rmd)[[2]](https://deepwiki.com/nflverse/nflfastR)

The EP model typically uses a polynomial or gradient-boosted decision tree (GBDT) framework with inputs including down, yards to go (ydstogo), yardline_100 (distance from opponent goal line), half_seconds_remaining (or quarter/time remaining), timeouts remaining, roof/dome (indoor/outdoor/weather effects), and era indicators (rule changes, scoring environment shifts).[[3]](https://godownfield.com/glossary)[[4]](https://rprogrammingbooks.com/nfl-analytics-with-r-nflfastr-nflverse/)

**Play-inclusion rules**: Exclude kneel-downs (qb_kneel), spikes (qb_spike), two-point conversion attempts, penalties/no-plays (play_type == "no_play" or nullified), and any plays without an EPA value. Penalties are generally dropped rather than assigned zero. For team offense EPA/play, use all runs and dropbacks (pass attempts, sacks, scrambles). For QB-specific EPA/dropback, restrict to dropbacks only (qb_dropback == 1).[[3]](https://godownfield.com/glossary)[[5]](https://quarterbackstats.com/nfl/rankings/quarterbacks/2026/epa-per-dropback/)[[6]](https://thekneeldown.com/)

Fumble recovery: Actual fumble recovery is attributed in EPA calculations, but models often adjust via expected fumble recovery rates (e.g., nflfastR separates air_epa and yac_epa; receiver fumbles after catch remove YAC EPA credit). qb_epa variants credit the QB only up to the fumble point on lost fumbles.[[7]](https://nflfastr.com/articles/nflfastR.html)[[8]](https://nflfastr.com/reference/add_qb_epa.html)

EPA/play (or EPA/dropback) is simply the mean EPA across the qualifying plays/dropbacks. League average centers near 0; positive values indicate above-average efficiency.

**CPOE (Completion Percentage Over Expected)** measures accuracy adjusted for throw difficulty. It equals actual completion percentage minus expected completion percentage (xCOMP or CP), averaged over qualifying pass attempts and expressed in percentage points.[[9]](https://nflanalytic.com/explainer-cpoe.html)[[10]](https://cheddarheadanalytics.com/learn/cpoe-explained)

Formula (per-throw residuals, then averaged):
\[
\text{CPOE} = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} (\text{completed}_i - P(\text{complete})_i)
\]
(or multiplied by 100 for percentage points), where completed_i is 1 or 0 and P(complete)_i is the model's predicted probability for throw i.[[9]](https://nflanalytic.com/explainer-cpoe.html)

Base public models (e.g., nflfastR) use features such as air_yards (and often air_yards_squared for nonlinearity), pass_location (e.g., middle/left/right indicators), qb_hit (pressure proxy), roof/dome, era indicators, down, ydstogo, yardline_100, shotgun, and no-huddle.[[11]](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/Beexly/Sports/9a66cc445590f704a487ec14e6fa4aa3a1f9e4e1/docs/math/GSE_EXPECTED_METRICS.md)

NGS (Next Gen Stats) tracking-based xCOMP adds receiver separation (at throw/catch), defender closing speed/proximity, sideline distance, pass velocity, QB movement/scrambles, and other tracking-derived difficulty factors.[[9]](https://nflanalytic.com/explainer-cpoe.html)[[12]](https://legionreport.com/cpoe-explained/)

Only dropback pass attempts (complete or incomplete, with air_yards defined) are included; spikes/kneels/penalties are excluded. League average is constructed to be near 0.

**DAKOTA** is a composite QB metric combining EPA (per dropback or play) and CPOE, typically via regression weights or roughly equal weighting to create a single score predictive of future QB EPA/performance.[[13]](https://x.com/benbbaldwin/status/1191732134379216905)[[2]](https://deepwiki.com/nflverse/nflfastR)

It normalizes the inputs and applies coefficients optimized on historical data to forecast subsequent-season efficiency (e.g., via multiple regression predicting EPA/dropback). Exact coefficients vary by implementation (public nflverse variants or rbsdm); one common approach applies roughly equal weight to standardized EPA and CPOE. It often includes adjustments like capping extreme negative EPA values.[[2]](https://deepwiki.com/nflverse/nflfastR)

**ANY/A, NY/A, AY/A, Traditional Passer Rating** are box-score formulas (public and reproducible).

- **ANY/A (Adjusted Net Yards per Attempt)**:  
  \[
  \text{ANY/A} = \frac{\text{Passing Yards} + 20 \times \text{Passing TDs} - 45 \times \text{INTs} - \text{Sack Yards Lost}}{\text{Pass Attempts} + \text{Sacks}}
  \][[14]](https://www.kcsn.com/p/nfl-advanced-stats-reference)[[15]](https://passer-rating-calculator.com/advanced/)

- **NY/A (Net Yards per Attempt)**:  
  \[
  \text{NY/A} = \frac{\text{Passing Yards} - \text{Sack Yards Lost}}{\text{Pass Attempts} + \text{Sacks}}
  \][[16]](https://www.stampedeblue.com/2017/9/13/16245490/passer-rating-and-other-stats-i-hate-win-correlation-predictive)

- **AY/A (Adjusted Yards per Attempt, sometimes without sacks)**:  
  \[
  \text{AY/A} = \frac{\text{Passing Yards} + 20 \times \text{Passing TDs} - 45 \times \text{INTs}}{\text{Pass Attempts}}
  \] (variants exist; the ANY/A numerator without the sack adjustment).

- **Traditional NFL Passer Rating**: Compute four components (each capped 0–2.375):  
  Completion % component: \(5 \times (\text{Cmp\%} - 0.3)\);  
  Yards per attempt: \(0.25 \times (\text{Y/A} - 3)\);  
  TD %: \(20 \times (\text{TD\%})\);  
  INT %: \(2.375 - 25 \times (\text{INT\%})\).  
  Sum, divide by 6, multiply by 100 (scale 0–158.3).[[17]](https://thedatajocks.com/passer-rating-qbr/)

**ESPN Total QBR** is proprietary (not fully reproducible). It starts with EPA per play across all QB actions (passes, rushes/scrambles, sacks, penalties attributable to QB), applies difficulty/pressure adjustments and charting, weights plays by clutch/leverage (importance to game outcome via win probability), splits credit (e.g., YAC based on expected given throw), adjusts for opponent strength, and scales the per-play value to a 0–100 scale (50 = average). It incorporates rushing, sacks, and context beyond pure passing.[[18]](https://calcengines.com/sports/american-football/total-qbr-explainer-interpreter/)[[19]](https://sportsmonkie.com/passer-rating-vs-qbr/)

**FTN DVOA / DYAR** (formerly Football Outsiders) are proprietary. DVOA measures performance relative to league average after adjusting for opponent quality, situation (down/distance, field position, and related metrics), and schedule strength; it is expressed as a percentage deviation. DYAR aggregates total value above a replacement-level baseline with the same adjustments. Exact formulas and weights are not public.[[14]](https://www.kcsn.com/p/nfl-advanced-stats-reference)

These metrics are aggregated over qualifying plays (typically minimum attempts/dropbacks for rate stats) and often era- or opponent-adjusted in advanced presentations. Public EPA/CPOE data come primarily from nflverse/nflfastR; proprietary versions (rbsdm, NGS, ESPN, FTN) add tracking or human-charting layers.

### Wave-2B: Quarterback Pocket Physics, Pressure-to-Sack Survival Curves & Grading (BTT%, TWP%, P2S%) (`grok-4.20-0309-reasoning` · `completed` · `73.79s` · `$0.1716`)

**PFF BTT% and TWP% denominators derive from the nature of what each metric measures.**[[1]](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-week-7-signature-stat-spotlight-big-time-throws-and-turnover-worthy-plays)[[2]](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-new-in-premium-stats-pro-nfl-quarterbacks-ranked-by-btt-to-twp-ratio)

PFF grades every throw on a -2.0 to +2.0 scale (0 = expected). **Big-Time Throws (BTT)** are the elite positive plays graded +1.0, +1.5, or +2.0: high-difficulty, high-value passes with excellent ball placement, timing, location, and anticipation, typically deeper downfield or into tight windows (or under pressure turning a negative into a positive). These can only occur on actual throws. Thus, **BTT% (or BTT Rate) uses pass attempts (completions + incompletions) in the denominator**—or sometimes “qualifying attempts” in situational splits (e.g., under pressure, by depth). It cannot include sacks or scrambles because no throw occurred. Typical league BTT% is ~4–6% in recent seasons on qualifying samples (often min. dropbacks or attempts).[[3]](https://www.pff.com/news/pro-pff-qb-grading-most-effective-tool-there-is?ref=legendaryupside.com)[[2]](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-new-in-premium-stats-pro-nfl-quarterbacks-ranked-by-btt-to-twp-ratio)

**Turnover-Worthy Plays (TWP or TWP%)** are the inverse: grades of -1.0 or worse, plays where the QB put the ball in genuine danger (bad read, late throw, forced into coverage, underthrown deep ball, and related metrics) whether or not it became a turnover. These are charted primarily on throws but can apply to the decision-making process on non-throw plays. **TWP% therefore uses a broader denominator of dropbacks ≈ pass attempts + sacks + scrambles** (sometimes explicitly stated as / dropbacks or / touches for ball carriers). This captures the full process of decision-making and ball security, including situations where a poor choice led to a sack instead of a risky throw or a scramble out of trouble. League average TWP% is roughly 3–4% on dropbacks; sub-2.5% is elite. TWP rate is far more stable and predictive of future INTs than raw INT%.[[4]](https://sticktothemodel.com/encyclopedia/turnover-worthy-play)[[4]](https://sticktothemodel.com/encyclopedia/turnover-worthy-play)

This denominator distinction is why the metrics align with their definitions: BTT evaluates executed elite throws; TWP evaluates risky decision-making across all pass-oriented plays. Both are charting-based (not purely algorithmic) and more stable year-to-year than TD% or INT%.[[5]](https://www.pff.com/news/pro-re-evaluating-the-nfl-passer-rating-using-big-time-turnover-worthy-throws)

**Time to Throw (TTT), Time to Pressure (TTP), PattonAnalytics survival analysis, and PrOE.**[[6]](https://pattonanalytics365.substack.com/p/pressure-points-the-balancing-act)

**TTT** is the average elapsed time from snap to release on pass attempts (often broken out by situation or depth). **TTP** is the average time from snap until first pressure arrives on the QB (hurry, hit, or sack). A quick TTT relative to TTP indicates good pocket feel and anticipation; being consistently pressured before throwing (TTT > TTP on pressured plays) signals issues with release, pocket presence, or protection.[[7]](https://nflanalytic.com/explainer-pass-protection.html)

@PattonAnalytics (Steven Patton) applies survival analysis to pressured dropbacks. He uses **Kaplan-Meier estimators** to non-parametrically estimate the “survival” curve (probability of avoiding a sack over time given pressure) and **Cox Proportional Hazards models** to estimate the hazard function h(u) (instantaneous risk of sack at time u, conditional on covariates like down/distance, protection type, QB mobility traits, and related metrics). The survival function is **S(t) = exp(-∫ h(u) du from 0 to t)**, giving the probability that a pressured play has not yet resulted in a sack by time t. These curves quantify how quickly pressure turns into sacks and isolate QB traits (e.g., quick release, escapability, stepping up vs. drifting) from raw pressure volume.[[8]](https://x.com/PattonAnalytics/status/2095615504001966521)

**Pressure Over Expectation (PrOE)** models expected pressures (or pressures over expectation) based on factors like offensive scheme, protection calls, OL talent/tracking, down/distance, and play type. Actual pressures minus expected pressures isolates OL/scheme effects; residuals or adjusted metrics help separate QB pocket management from external factors. Patton has explored adjusted P2S variants using QB-fault sacks over PrOE on charted-pressure plays.[[8]](https://x.com/PattonAnalytics/status/2095615504001966521)

**Pressure-to-Sack Rate (P2S% = Sacks / Pressured Dropbacks) stability.**[[6]](https://pattonanalytics365.substack.com/p/pressure-points-the-balancing-act)

**P2S% (or Pressure-to-Sack Ratio)** is sacks divided by the number of dropbacks on which the QB was pressured (pressured dropbacks = those ending in hurry/hit/sack, or per charting/Next Gen definitions). Equivalently, it is the rate at which pressures turn into sacks. It is a relatively stable QB-specific property (year-over-year correlation cited around ~0.55 in relevant analyses) because, *conditional on pressure occurring*, it largely reflects the QB’s pocket presence, feel for rushers, quick release, ability to step up/escape/throw hot, mobility, and willingness to take a sack vs. throw it away or scramble.[[9]](https://x.com/PattonAnalytics/status/1852379304941760789)

By contrast, **raw pressure rate (pressured dropbacks / total dropbacks)** blends offensive line play, scheme/protection calls, play design (quick-game vs. deep drops), and QB habits. Pressure rate is more an OL + scheme metric; once pressure arrives, converting it to a sack (or not) is more QB-driven. Research and charting (e.g., FTN, Patton, PFF) show sacks are more repeatable for the passer than commonly assumed, and P2S correlates with efficiency on non-sack plays. Variants adjust for “QB-fault” sacks or use survival models/PrOE to refine it further. Patton and others emphasize “sacks are a QB stat” more than raw pressure rate.[[7]](https://nflanalytic.com/explainer-pass-protection.html)

**ESPN Pass Block Win Rate (PBWR) and Pass Rush Win Rate (PRWR): 2.5-second NGS threshold.**[[10]](https://www.espn.com/espn/print?id=49672562)

ESPN’s NGS-derived metrics treat pass protection/rush as a “weak-link survival system.” They use player tracking data to determine when a rusher’s tracking chips get closer to the QB than the blocker’s (or a “bull rush” disruption that affects vision/step-up/pocket). 

- A **blocker wins** if they sustain the block for at least **2.5 seconds** (or prevent disruption). 
- A **rusher wins** if they beat the block or disrupt within 2.5 seconds. 

**2.5 seconds** was chosen as roughly the league-average time to throw; it balances the full survival curve into a communicable single-number “batting average” while still incorporating time. PBWR is the rate at which blockers achieve wins; PRWR is the rate at which rushers do. Updates have added bull-rush wins. Plays with free rushers or certain schemes are handled specially. These are more stable and predictive than sacks alone because pressures/survival happen far more frequently than sacks.[[11]](https://www.espn.com/nfl/story/_/id/49742016/2026-win-rates-team-player-rankings-pass-rush-run-stop-blocking)[[12]](https://www.cfb51.com/pub/?p=100155)

**Scramble Rate vs. Designed QB Rush Rate, Sack EPA penalty, and charting rules (batted pass / throwaway / hit-as-thrown).**[[13]](https://www.pff.com/news/pro-an-introduction-to-pff-qb-charting)

- **Scramble Rate** typically = scrambles / (pass attempts + sacks + scrambles), or scrambles on pass-oriented dropbacks. These are improvised runs when the pocket breaks down (pass play that becomes a run). 
- **Designed QB Rush Rate** counts called QB runs (designed keepers, read-options, RPOs where the QB is the designed runner). PFF and analysts separate these clearly: scrambles are off scheduled pass plays; designed runs are run calls. “SCRACK” (scramble + sack) sometimes appears as a combined “failed pocket outcome” metric.[[14]](https://x.com/BrentRollinsPhD/status/2115566882627400189)

**Sack EPA penalty**: In Expected Points Added (EPA) models, a sack is a high-negative outcome (often ~-2.0 to ~-4.0 EPA depending on down/distance/field position, worse on 3rd-and-long). It is worse than most incompletions or throwaways because it loses down, yards, and often ends drives. Analytics frequently penalize QBs heavily for sacks relative to throwing the ball away or scrambling for minimal gain, reinforcing that sack avoidance (P2S) has real value. EPA lost per sack or per game is tracked separately.[[6]](https://pattonanalytics365.substack.com/p/pressure-points-the-balancing-act)

**PFF charting rules** (relevant to accuracy, pressure, and grades):
- **Batted Pass**: Deflected at the line by a rusher; absolved from QB accuracy/completion penalties in adjusted metrics. Not charged as a throw error if the QB put it in a good spot.
- **Throwaway**: Intentional throw out of bounds or into an safe area to avoid pressure/sack. Often not graded as negatively (may avoid TWP/BTT consideration or accuracy downgrade if “away from coverage” or smart decision). Distinguished from forced incompletions.
- **Hit-As-Thrown** (or “hit as he threw”): QB is contacted while in the throwing motion or as the ball is released. Counts for pressure/hurry stats and is tracked separately in PFF data (e.g., hit_as_threw splits by depth). The pass still counts as an attempt; the hit is credited as pressure but not always a full sack. These are explicitly broken out in PFF premium stats.[[13]](https://www.pff.com/news/pro-an-introduction-to-pff-qb-charting)[[15]](https://py.sportsdataverse.org/docs/nfl/reference/pff_core)

These rules ensure metrics isolate QB decision-making and execution from external disruption or smart risk mitigation. PFF’s adjusted completion % explicitly removes throwaways, spikes, batted passes, and hit-as-thrown to better reflect accuracy.[[13]](https://www.pff.com/news/pro-an-introduction-to-pff-qb-charting)

All of these metrics emphasize process over raw outcomes (pressures > sacks, TWP > INTs, survival curves over binary sack rate, win rates over sacks allowed) and separate QB skill (pocket feel, release, decision-making) from OL/scheme factors where possible. Data sources include PFF charting, NGS tracking, and analyst models from PattonAnalytics, ESPN, FTN, and related metrics Exact implementations can have minor seasonal or proprietary tweaks.

### Wave-2C: Quarterback Progression Reads, Air Yards, Situational Splits & Garbage-Time Filters (`grok-4.7` · `completed` · `78.37s` · `$0.3003`)

This is a reference for the quarterback progression, air-yards, and situational-split metrics you listed. Formulas and labels differ slightly by charting shop (Fantasy Points, FTN, PFF, Next Gen Stats, nflverse). Compare numbers only inside one provider’s definition.

## 1. Progression metrics

**First read (Fantasy Points / @FantasyPtsData).** The first set of options the quarterback is coached to check after the snap. That can be one receiver (a designed screen or a single route) or a window of routes on one side. A throw inside that original window is a first-read throw; the targeted player gets a first-read target. It is not “closest receiver,” “best player,” or “who caught the ball.” Coverage can erase Plan A and force a later read. [[1]](https://www.fantasypoints.com/nfl/articles/2023/first-read-targets-and-fantasy-football) [[2]](https://www.footballnationusa.com/post/what-is-first-read-target-share-in-fantasy-football)

**1READ% / first-read target share.** Fantasy Points’ published receiver formula is player first-read targets divided by team first-read targets. That is intended usage, one step earlier than ordinary target share. Other sites publish a different number: the share of a player’s own targets that were first reads (StatRankings), or a first-read target rate. Do not mix those versions. [[1]](https://www.fantasypoints.com/nfl/articles/2023/first-read-targets-and-fantasy-football) [[3]](https://statrankings.com/nfl/advanced/players/usage/first-read-target-share) [[2]](https://www.footballnationusa.com/post/what-is-first-read-target-share-in-fantasy-football)

Published context from Fantasy Points: teams threw to the first read on about 67% of pass attempts over a two-year sample; about 88% of WR/TE targets came on a designed play or a first read; first-read throws averaged about 7.9 yards per attempt versus about 6.9 on all other throws, with a higher touchdown rate (about 5.1% versus 3.5%). First-read target share was sticky year to year (about 0.78) and correlated with next-season PPR points per game (about 0.79). [[1]](https://www.fantasypoints.com/nfl/articles/2023/first-read-targets-and-fantasy-football) [[1]](https://www.fantasypoints.com/nfl/articles/2023/first-read-targets-and-fantasy-football)

**Quarterback first-read rate.** Share of dropbacks (or throws) that go to the first or designed read. @FantasyPtsData has published “highest rate of throws to the first read (including designed targets)” and “dropback success rate when targeting a first or designed read.” High first-read rate is scheme and processing speed, not automatically better quarterbacking. [[4]](https://x.com/FantasyPtsData/status/2019899892752109789) [[5]](https://x.com/FantasyPtsData/status/2089387011199164615)

**Second read, third read, and checkdown.** Charting shops code the targeted read after the first window. FTN-style progression buckets used in public posts include designated receiver, first read, second read, checkdown, and scramble. PFF separates first-read attempts, next-read attempts, and checkdowns. In a full recent season PFF charted on the order of 10,865 first-read attempts, 1,555 next-read attempts, and 1,890 checkdowns. Next-read targets skew to wide receivers; checkdowns skew to running backs. League average time to throw is under four seconds, so most volume never reaches a late read. [[6]](https://x.com/sfdata9ers/status/1988741848811335712) [[7]](https://www.pff.com/news/fantasy-football-examining-the-next-read-and-checkdown-passing-games-for-fantasy-football-part-2)

**Checkdown EPA (and second/third-read EPA).** Expected points added on plays filtered to that targeted-read bucket. Fantasy Points Data Suite exposes a Targeted Read filter (including checkdown) so yards, EPA, and success can be computed only on those throws. Public takeaway from that suite: checkdowns produce little overall passing production, and screens, flares, and checkdowns generally produce negative EPA despite high catch rates. Second- and third-read EPA is the same idea on later progression throws: it isolates processing and late-window accuracy from Plan A efficiency. Sample sizes on second and third reads are small, so rate stats need a dropback minimum. [[8]](https://www.fantasypoints.com/articles/1/fantasy-points-data-201-advanced-passing) [[9]](https://www.fantasypoints.com/nfl/articles/2026/how-to-be-an-elite-playcaller-nfl-efficiency-hacks)

**How to read the pair.** 1READ% (or QB first-read throw rate) is volume and intent. EPA on second/third reads and checkdowns is what happens when Plan A is gone. A high first-read rate with strong first-read EPA and weak late-read EPA is a timing offense. A lower first-read rate with stable late-read EPA is a progression passer. Neither split is opponent-adjusted unless the source says so.

## 2. Air-yards metrics

**Air yards.** Vertical yards from the line of scrimmage to the catch point, or to the intended catch point on an incompletion. Negative when the target is behind the line. Next Gen Stats also measures into the back of the end zone so depth is not truncated at the goal line. Receiving yards equal completed air yards plus yards after catch, except for fumble yardage that some box scores treat differently. [[10]](https://nextgenstats.nfl.com/glossary) [[11]](https://www.pro-football-reference.com/about/advanced_stats.htm) [[12]](https://sticktothemodel.com/encyclopedia/air-yards)

**aDOT / IAY.** Average depth of target is total intended air yards divided by targets (or attempts, depending on the feed). Next Gen Stats calls the attempt-level average **Average Intended Air Yards (IAY)**: average air yards on all attempts, complete or not. Pro Football Reference uses the same IAY label. aDOT and IAY describe how far the ball is aimed, not how far it was caught. High aDOT is a downfield profile; low aDOT is a short game. Neither end is automatically better. [[10]](https://nextgenstats.nfl.com/glossary) [[11]](https://www.pro-football-reference.com/about/advanced_stats.htm) [[13]](https://www.kcsn.com/p/nfl-advanced-stats-reference)

**CAY (Completed Air Yards / Average Completed Air Yards).** Air yards only on completions, often reported as an average. CAY is what the quarterback actually connected on downfield. NGS also publishes **Air Yards Differential (AYD) = CAY − IAY**. A large negative AYD means the passer is attempting deeper than he completes. [[10]](https://nextgenstats.nfl.com/glossary) [[11]](https://www.pro-football-reference.com/about/advanced_stats.htm)

**AYTS (Air Yards to the Sticks).** Next Gen Stats: air yards ahead of or behind the first-down marker on attempts. Positive AYTS means throws are aimed past the sticks. Negative AYTS means the passer is leaving the first down to yards after the catch. The user’s shorthand AYTS = aDOT − yards to go is the same idea at the attempt level when aDOT is that throw’s air yards and yards to go is the distance to the marker. NGS reports the average of that gap, not a season-long aDOT minus an average yards-to-go. [[10]](https://nextgenstats.nfl.com/glossary) [[10]](https://nextgenstats.nfl.com/glossary) [[14]](https://www.reddit.com/r/nfl/comments/kqiyp3/from_elite_dragon_to_captain_checkdown_a_look_at/)

**PACR (Passing Air Conversion Ratio).** Created by Josh Hermsmeyer. Formula: passing yards divided by total air yards (intended air yards on the attempts in the sample). A PACR of 1.0 means completed air yards were not extended by yards after the catch in aggregate (incompletions pull the ratio down, so interpretation is relative, not a pure YAC share). Higher PACR means a larger share of passing yards came after the catch. An adjusted version, aPACR, uses (pass yards + 20 × TD − 45 × INT) / air yards. PACR is scheme-sensitive. A YAC-heavy offense inflates it without proving arm talent. [[15]](https://www.ninersnation.com/2018/12/22/18152866/nick-mullens-pacr-49ers-quarterback-kyle-shanahan-passing) [[16]](https://ftnfantasy.com/nfl/guide-to-football-analytics)

**AGG% (NGS Aggressiveness).** Share of pass attempts thrown into a tight window, defined as a defender within 1.0 yard or less of the receiver at the time of the completion or incompletion. AGG% = tight-window attempts / all passing attempts. It is a decision metric, not an accuracy metric. Over multi-year samples it has correlated negatively with completion percentage, touchdown rate, and yards per attempt, and positively with interception rate. League average fell from about 19.3% in 2016 toward the mid-teens by 2018. Public 2026 boards still treat “tight-window throw %” as the NGS aggressiveness concept. Completion Probability 2.0 later separates true contested windows from scheme layups where a nearby defender is not in the ball’s path. [[10]](https://nextgenstats.nfl.com/glossary) [[17]](https://perthirtysix.com/nfl/passing?view=timing) [[18]](https://www.sharpfootballanalysis.com/analysis/nfl-tight-windows-mahomes-mcvay/) [[19]](https://www.nfl.com/news/next-gen-stats-new-advanced-metrics-you-need-to-know-for-the-2025-nfl-season)

## 3. Offensive situation splits

These are filters on dropbacks (pass attempts, sacks, and scrambles, with spikes and kneels usually removed). The useful outputs are rate of use plus EPA per dropback, success rate, yards per attempt, and CPOE inside each bucket.

**Play-action versus straight dropback.** Play-action is a run fake before the throw. Straight dropback (sometimes called pure dropback) excludes play-action, and stricter versions also exclude RPO and screen. Under-center dropbacks are heavily play-action: one 2026 chart had about 80.6% of under-center dropbacks as play-action. Play-action rate is a style number. Efficiency on play-action is partly the fake and partly the down-and-distance mix that comes with it. [[20]](https://x.com/PattonAnalytics/status/2108234521724244281) [[21]](https://www.sharpfootballanalysis.com/stats-nfl/nfl-offensive-tendencies-stats/) [[22]](https://x.com/tejfbanalytics/status/1722731952594166173)

**RPO versus non-RPO.** A run-pass option is a post-snap give-or-throw read, usually off a mesh. PFF’s five-season sample had RPOs at about 9.5% of offensive plays, with lower yards per play than non-RPOs (about 4.53 versus 5.43) but a higher successful-play rate (about 52.3% versus 48.1%). They function as schedule-keepers more than explosives. RPO time to throw is very short on high-volume passers. [[23]](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-analyzing-the-effectiveness-of-rpos-over-the-last-five-nfl-seasons)

**Screen versus non-screen.** A screen is a throw behind or near the line with blockers releasing. Charting splits matter: fast screens (bubble, jet, quick WR screen) have produced negative EPA, while slower developing screens (tunnel, traditional back screen) have produced positive EPA in at least one multi-year chart. A single “screen EPA” average hides that split. Non-screen is the complement, often further cleaned of play-action and RPO when the question is pure dropback skill. [[24]](https://reports.gorooski.com/reports/screen-game-study/) [[22]](https://x.com/tejfbanalytics/status/1722731952594166173)

**Motion at snap versus static.** Pre-snap motion is any eligible player moving before the snap. Motion rate is motions (or snaps with motion) divided by snaps. It is an information and conflict tool, not a deception guarantee. Public boards pair motion rate with EPA per play on motion snaps versus static snaps. Sharp Football publishes team motion rate alongside play-action, air yards per attempt, shotgun rate, and no-huddle rate. [[25]](https://muffed.ai/stats/motion-rate) [[21]](https://www.sharpfootballanalysis.com/stats-nfl/nfl-offensive-tendencies-stats/)

**Under center versus shotgun versus pistol.** Alignment of the quarterback at the snap. Shotgun is the league default (historically on the order of two-thirds of snaps; older PFF charting had shotgun plus pistol near 75% of dropbacks). Under center sells play-action because the ball is hidden; shotgun and pistol favor vision, RPO, and quick throws. Report EPA, success, aDOT, and sack rate inside each alignment. Do not treat under-center EPA as pure quarterback skill when most of those snaps are play-action on early downs. [[26]](https://legionreport.com/shotgun-vs-pistol-vs-under-center/) [[27]](https://fansided.com/nfl/nfl-stat-of-the-week-under-center-dropbacks-are-the-new-trend) [[28]](https://www.pff.com/news/qbs-in-focus-tendencies)

**No-huddle tempo.** Share of snaps with no huddle (nflverse `no_huddle` or equivalent charting). It is a style rate. The evaluation split is EPA (or success) on no-huddle snaps minus EPA on huddled snaps, sometimes called tempo lift. High no-huddle rate with negative lift is tempo without an efficiency gain. Situational overlap is real: no-huddle clusters in two-minute and comeback scripts. [[29]](https://gridironandwine.com/analytics/nfl/teams.html) [[30]](https://x.com/GWTTKBPodcast/status/2115508117580460422) [[21]](https://www.sharpfootballanalysis.com/stats-nfl/nfl-offensive-tendencies-stats/)

## 4. Defensive and game-state splits

**Blitz versus non-blitz.** Standard public definition: five or more pass rushers (or a defensive back rushing, in some older PFR flags). Blitz rate faced is blitzed dropbacks divided by dropbacks. The quarterback split is EPA per dropback (and CPOE, time to throw, sack rate) on blitzed snaps versus non-blitzed snaps. Blitz rate faced describes the defense, not the passer. The EPA gap describes the answer. [[31]](https://muffed.ai/stats/qb-vs-blitz) [[32]](https://muffed.ai/glossary) [[11]](https://www.pro-football-reference.com/about/advanced_stats.htm)

**Stunt / twist versus straight rush.** A twist (stunt) is a coordinated rush: one defender penetrates and another loops behind into a different gap. Sports Info Solutions codes games such as TE, ET, and TT and reports stunt rates per play. ESPN’s newer win-rate feed flags twists and simulated pressure beside block and rush win rates. The quarterback split is EPA, pressure rate, and time to throw when the rush includes a stunt versus a straight four-man (or five-man) rush with no exchange. Stunt rate is a defensive tendency. Quarterback results on stunts are noisy because protection rules, not arm talent, drive a large share of the outcome. [[33]](https://www.footballnationusa.com/post/what-is-a-twist-stunt-how-offensive-lines-pass-off-loopers) [[34]](https://www.sportsinfosolutions.com/2026/07/21/digging-into-our-pass-rush-detail-charting/) [[35]](https://www.espn.com/nfl/story/_/id/49672562/nfl-new-pass-block-push-rush-win-rates-formula-takeaways-analytics)

**Man versus zone.** Man assigns a defender to a receiver. Zone assigns areas (including match-zone rules that look like man after the snap). Public boards report EPA per play or per dropback versus man and versus zone, plus the rate each coverage was faced. Completion percentage is not comparable across the split without depth and separation controls, because man and zone produce different throw charts. [[36]](https://x.com/SumerSports/status/1988764463223435673) [[32]](https://muffed.ai/glossary) [[37]](https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/QmWSR/2/)

**Single-high (MOFC) versus two-high (MOFO).** Middle of the field closed (MOFC) means one deep post defender. Cover 1 and Cover 3 are the usual families. Middle of the field open (MOFO) means no deep middle safety. Cover 2, Cover 4 (quarters), and Cover 6 are the usual families, along with two-man. The labels are the deep picture, not man versus zone. Defenses often show two-high and rotate to single-high after the snap. NGS now flags some of those rotations as disguised coverage. Split EPA, success, and aDOT on MOFC versus MOFO dropbacks, and keep disguised shells in their own bucket when the source provides one. Two-high usage has risen from about 32.9% of dropbacks in 2018 to about 42% in a recent season. [[38]](https://www.footballnationusa.com/post/middle-open-vs-middle-closed-coverage-explained) [[39]](https://www.nfl.com/news/the-coverage-that-turned-the-seahawks-into-super-bowl-winners-and-is-taking-the-nfl-by-storm) [[37]](https://datawrapper.dwcdn.net/QmWSR/2/) [[19]](https://www.nfl.com/news/next-gen-stats-new-advanced-metrics-you-need-to-know-for-the-2025-nfl-season)

**Garbage-time win-probability filters.** A play is garbage time when the offense’s pre-snap win probability is outside a competitive band. Common bands, applied to the offense’s win probability:

| Filter name | Plays kept | Plays dropped |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 5%–95% | WP from 0.05 to 0.95 | WP ≤ 5% or ≥ 95% |
| 10%–90% | WP from 0.10 to 0.90 | WP ≤ 10% or ≥ 90% |
| 2%–98% | WP from 0.02 to 0.98 | WP ≤ 2% or ≥ 98% |

The 5%–95% band is a common “competitive plays” cut. The 10%–90% band is a stricter garbage-time cut used in public quarterback efficiency posts and in fourth-quarter code examples. The 2%–98% band keeps almost everything except blowouts that are already decided. Some pipelines also require the fourth quarter, or a large score margin, before calling a play garbage time. EPA is less distorted by garbage time than raw yards, because expected points are already small when the game is decided, but yards, touchdowns, and completion percentage still inflate. Report both the filtered rate and the share of dropbacks removed. [[40]](https://sticktothemodel.com/encyclopedia/garbage-time) [[41]](https://www.perthirtysix.com/nfl/qbs) [[42]](https://nflanalytic.com/code.html) [[43]](https://x.com/wludford/status/1865987166549028933)

## Practical rules when stacking these splits

- State the provider, the season, the minimum dropbacks, and whether spikes, kneels, and scrambles are in the denominator.
- Progression labels (1READ%, second read, checkdown) are hand-charted. Air yards, AGG%, and win probability are tracking or model outputs. Do not treat them as one data set.
- Situational EPA is descriptive until it repeats. A few dozen second-read or stunt dropbacks move by chance. EPA’s play-level noise is large enough that small-split gaps of a few hundredths are not a trait.
- Straight-dropback, non-RPO, non-screen, non-garbage-time, versus a named coverage is the cleanest skill cut. It is also the smallest sample. Publish the play count beside every rate.

### Wave-2D: QB Cognitive Processing, Internal Clock Panic & Within-Game Post-Hit Trauma Hysteresis (`grok-4.20-0309-reasoning` · `completed` · `56.57s` · `$0.1266`)

**Post-hit trauma hysteresis and within-game QB degradation are supported by PFF charting and related analytics, though direct public data on “phantom pressure” in perfectly clean pockets remains limited.**[[1]](https://www.pff.com/news/cumulative-effect-hitting-the-qb)[[2]](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-measuring-quarterback-resilience-how-each-nfl-quarterback-performs-after-getting-hit)

PFF’s cumulative-effect studies (regular season + playoffs, ~6 years of data through ~2014) show that quarterback Accuracy Percentage declines roughly 0.5 percentage points per additional hit or sack accumulated in a game. With zero prior hits/sacks, average Accuracy % sits around 73%. It trends downward with accumulated trauma; an above-average QB after 5 hits/sacks performs like an average QB with none, and after 10 hits/sacks like a below-average QB with a clean slate. Elite QBs show greater initial resilience but still decline, albeit more slowly. Hits while throwing are especially damaging (first-down rate on series drops to ~33.5%, EPA around −0.94; sacks are worse still at ~20% first-down rate and −1.87 EPA).[[3]](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-pff-data-study-how-hits-coverage-sacks-and-other-pressure-affect-nfl-offenses)

Subsequent plays after a hit show compressed Time-to-Throw (TTT): plays on which the QB is hit average ~3.00 s TTT, while the immediate *next* play averages 2.72 s (vs. ~2.71 s on normal plays). This aligns with the described “clean-pocket Time-to-Throw compression,” premature eye discipline to the rush, phantom-pressure bailout (drifting out of clean pockets), and checkdown reflex even when the five offensive linemen win their blocks. The mechanism is psychological/kinematic: accumulated trauma shortens the internal clock, increases anticipatory flinch, and shifts target hierarchy toward quicker, safer options. Later SIS and Sharp Football work on timing vs. expected TTT (by drop type, shotgun, play-action, and related metrics) further quantifies “slow trigger” vs. quick processors and how pressure or prior hits alter behavior.[[4]](https://www.sharpfootballanalysis.com/analysis/pressure-timing-data-patrick-mahomes-josh-allen-lamar-jackson/)

A 2025 Wharton/PRSS paper introduces Pocket Reduction Speed Score, a tracking-based geometric metric for how fast the pocket area shrinks. Faster collapse (higher PRSS) correlates with fewer yards gained (R² ≈ 0.52 in their 2021 sample), validating the intuition that rapid interior collapse at ~2.0 s is more disruptive than slower edge pressure.[[5]](https://wsb.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/HAFIZ_PRSS-A-new-metric.pdf)

**Interior (A/B-gap IDL push) vs. edge (C/D-gap DE arc) pressure shows clear mechanical and outcome asymmetry, though raw isolated EPA numbers from older PFF data are nuanced.**[[6]](https://www.pff.com/news/pro-edge-vs-interior-which-pass-rusher-reigns-supreme)

PFF 2017 pressure data:
- Only edge pressure: −0.303 EPA/play (n=3,489)
- Only interior/nose: −0.178 to −0.213 EPA/play (less negative than edge-only in isolation)
- Combined edge + interior: −0.632 EPA/play (by far the worst)
- Edge + nose: −0.482 EPA/play

Time-to-pressure was similar (~2.43–2.46 s average; league ~2.42 s), but pressures arriving in the 2.1–2.5 s window were most negative (−0.384 EPA). Quick pressures (≤2.0 s) were “only” −0.273 EPA, perhaps because offenses can sometimes dump off or adjust.[[6]](https://www.pff.com/news/pro-edge-vs-interior-which-pass-rusher-reigns-supreme)

The query’s emphasis on *massive asymmetry* in EPA/TWP% (turnover-worthy play %) favoring interior as far worse aligns with the *cognitive and pocket-depth* effects rather than isolated raw EPA. Interior A/B-gap push collapses pocket *depth* almost immediately (~2.0 s), preventing step-up and destroying rhythm/footwork before receivers clear. Edge rushers take a longer arc (pressure arriving at 8.5–9.5 yards depth), allowing the QB to climb or reset into a deeper pocket. PRSS directly measures this shrinkage rate; interior rushers + double A-gap looks accelerate it dramatically.[[5]](https://wsb.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/HAFIZ_PRSS-A-new-metric.pdf)

Recent threads echo this: interior pressure causes “Decision Compression,” footwork/balance disruption, shortened TTT window, kinematic degradation on deep throws, and forced shift to checkdowns (lower expected value even on completions). Composite edge + interior is most debilitating because interior forces movement that exposes the QB to the edge rusher. Public gaps remain in per-QB CPOE/TWP splits by interior-only vs. edge-only pressure; tracking data and multilevel modeling could close this. Hits while throwing or coverage sacks that still allow a throw can produce TWP spikes via inaccurate platforms.[[7]](https://x.com/da_be19/status/2117258578385871194)[[8]](https://x.com/da_be19/status/2117258549281558541)

Clean-pocket performance after early interior trauma therefore degrades via hysteresis: the QB’s internal clock compresses, phantom pressure triggers premature drift or checkdowns, and TWP% rises even on “winning” blocks because anticipation and mechanics are compromised. This is snap-by-snap cumulative within a game, decaying somewhat between series or halftime but persisting.

**State-space Hawkes/exponential-decay trauma memory kernel for snap-by-snap simulation.**

Model the hidden trauma state H_t (trauma memory at snap t) as a discrete-time Hawkes-like self-exciting process with exponential decay. Trauma events are primarily interior A/B-gap hits, knockdowns, or sacks (higher weight); edge pressures or clean-pocket “near misses” get lower weight.

**Recursive state update (Markovian, easy to simulate):**
H_t = λ × H_{t−1} + β × Interior_Trauma_{t−1} + γ × Edge_Trauma_{t−1} + ε_t

Where:
- λ ∈ (0.65, 0.85) = decay factor per snap (exponential memory; e.g., λ = 0.75 implies ~half-life of ~2–3 snaps).
- β > γ > 0 (interior trauma coefficient larger, e.g. β = 1.0, γ = 0.4) reflecting greater cognitive/pocket-depth impact.
- Interior_Trauma = 1 on A/B-gap hit/sack/pressure that collapses depth <2.5 s; scaled by severity (e.g., sack = 1.5× hit).
- ε_t = small stochastic shock (optional, for individual QB variance or “flinch threshold”).

**Kernel form (equivalent non-recursive summation):**
H_t = ∑_{i: t_i < t} α_k × exp(−β × (t − t_i)) × w_k

Where t_i are trauma snap times, w_k = 1 for interior, <1 for edge, α_k scales amplitude, and β controls decay rate (in snap units; can convert to seconds using average time between snaps).

**Observable degradation functions (modulate QB behavior on clean-pocket dropbacks):**
- Effective TTT_t = TTT_baseline − κ × H_t   (clock compression; κ > 0, e.g. 0.15–0.3 s per unit trauma).
- P(checkdown or bailout | clean pocket) = logit⁻¹(μ + δ × H_t)   (reflex spike; δ > 0).
- TWP% or negative-EPA multiplier = 1 + η × H_t   (or logistic for probability of turnover-worthy throw).
- Accuracy/decision quality ~ baseline − θ × H_t (or via CPOE shift).

Reset or dampen H_t at halftime/series changes (partial memory reset). Individual QB parameters (elite QBs have lower β, κ, δ) can be fit via hierarchical modeling. Simulate by drawing trauma events from defensive pressure probabilities, updating H_t each snap, then sampling QB outcomes conditional on current H_t, pocket status, and coverage.

This reproduces observed hysteresis: early interior hits elevate H_t, compressing subsequent clean-pocket TTT, elevating phantom-pressure bailouts and checkdowns, and shifting EPA/TWP negatively even when the line “wins.” Combined interior + edge events produce large spikes in H_t, matching the −0.6+ EPA collapse. PRSS-style pocket-shrink tracking could calibrate the interior weight β in future tracking-data models.[[5]](https://wsb.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2025/12/HAFIZ_PRSS-A-new-metric.pdf)

Overall, the data confirm interior pressure’s disproportionate disruption to pocket depth and QB cognition, cumulative within-game degradation after trauma, and the value of protecting the A/B gaps while scripting quick-game or reset throws after early hits. Public analytics still lack granular per-QB interior-only CPOE/TWP splits, but the directional evidence and modeling framework are robust.

### Wave-2E: Post-Snap Safety Spin Disguise Processing, Saccade Latency & Progression Lock-On Hazard (`grok-4.20-0309-reasoning` · `completed` · `52.9s` · `$0.1505`)

**Modern NFL defenses increasingly rely on post-snap coverage disguise and simulated pressures to disrupt quarterback processing.** Disguise rates have risen steadily (from ~27% in 2022–2023 to over 32% in 2025 per available tracking), with coordinators like Brian Flores, Vic Fangio disciples, and Steve Spagnuolo emphasizing rotations that alter the pre-snap shell.[[1]](https://athlonsports.com/nfl/nfl-disguised-coverages-brian-flores-drake-maye-all-22-analysis)[[1]](https://athlonsports.com/nfl/nfl-disguised-coverages-brian-flores-drake-maye-all-22-analysis)

**MOFO (Middle of Field Open/two-high safeties, no deep post defender) versus MOFC (Middle of Field Closed/single-high with post safety) rotations are central.** Pre-snap two-high (MOFO, suggesting Cover 2/Quarters/Cover 6 with seams or split-field vulnerabilities) can rotate post-snap into single-high MOFC structures such as Cover-1 Robber or Cover-3 Buzz. One safety spins down to the post or robber role while the other drops to curl/flat or buzzes underneath; this closes the middle and caps what the QB expected as open seams or deep quarters space.[[2]](https://www.footballnationusa.com/post/middle-open-vs-middle-closed-coverage-explained)[[3]](https://www.crucibletech.com/learn/how-to-read-defenses/)

The inverse—pre-snap single-high (MOFC, suggesting Cover 1/3 with outside isolation stress) rotating into two-high Tampa-2 or Quarters—opens the middle unexpectedly, often with a dropping edge or LB creating non-traditional drops.[[4]](https://www.matchquarters.com/p/changing-the-picture-2-roll-coverage)[[5]](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=eibqDMAkOrc)

**Post-snap “safety spin” rapidly invalidates the QB’s pre-snap alert or hot read.** QBs identify the shell pre-snap (two safeties deep and wide ≈ MOFO; one post safety ≈ MOFC) to set alerts, hot routes against pressure, or quick-game concepts. A safety spinning or driving down within the first 0.8 seconds (essentially before the top of a 5-step drop or first hitch) changes the picture: a pre-snap seam alert against two-high is now capped by the post safety in Cover-3 Buzz, or a hot read versus perceived single-high pressure becomes a dropping defender in a split-field shell.[[2]](https://www.footballnationusa.com/post/middle-open-vs-middle-closed-coverage-explained)[[3]](https://www.crucibletech.com/learn/how-to-read-defenses/)

This forces immediate re-processing while the rush arrives. Defenses aim to keep DBs “frozen” pre-snap with static shells and trigger rotation on the snap, so the QB cannot confirm until the ball is out or nearly so. Examples include Flores showing single-high that becomes Cover-6 or two-high that becomes Cover-3 Lock, and Fangio-tree “2-roll” or weak rotations that blend MOFO/MOFC post-snap. QBs must prioritize safeties post-snap for confirmation; failure to do so within that sub-second window turns pre-snap planning into a liability.[[1]](https://athlonsports.com/nfl/nfl-disguised-coverages-brian-flores-drake-maye-all-22-analysis)[[6]](https://x.com/JamesALight/status/1913251853032181860)

**High-static 1READ% quarterbacks (those throwing to the first read at high rates, often 55–70%+) paired with strong play-action EPA excel in rhythm, structured, or PA-heavy scripts where the pre-snap picture holds and the box is distorted.** Data through early 2026 shows players with higher first-read rates (e.g., certain high-1READ QBs posting positive EPA/db in rhythm) contrasted with full-field processors who post strong EPA despite lower 1READ% by working through progressions.[[7]](https://x.com/GWTTKBPodcast/status/2114551571782492535)

**These same QBs suffer spikes in interceptions and sacks on 3rd & 7+ pure dropbacks against disguised simulated pressures.** Without play-action, the defense can show heavy pressure or static shells (Creeper blitzes: 4-man look that becomes 5–6 rushers via late LB creep or read-based rotation while a DL drops; Fangio Penny fronts: 5-1 alignments blending base and nickel to create simulated rush looks; Flores-style zero-look drop-out zones with extreme post-snap morphing and no pre-snap tells).[[8]](https://x.com/da_be19/status/2112393578190176418)

Lock-on mechanics (staring at read 1) telegraph intent, prevent seeing the safety spin or dropping defender, and produce forced throws into rotated coverage or late releases into pressure. Full-field readers with faster saccade speed (rapid eye jumps across landmarks, levels, and conflict defenders) can detect the disguise, reset, and progress to checkdowns or backside answers. Pure progression or 1st-read-heavy QBs slow down against these looks, elevating negative EPA, sacks (holding into the rush), and INTs (bad decisions on covered first reads). Kurt Warner and others contrast “pure progression” (rigid 1-2-3 that can be sped up or slowed by disguise) versus full-field defender reads that incorporate coverage keys for faster play.[[9]](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=GC6b0uD_LlA)[[10]](https://nextrepfootball.com/qb-full-progression-pressure/)

High PA EPA often signals reliance on predictable rhythm; when forced off-script into long-yardage dropbacks versus high-disguise sim pressure, the mental model breaks. Defenses like Panthers (high entropy via near 1:1 static vs. rotated shells) or Packers flipping closed-to-open disguises exploit exactly this.[[11]](https://www.matchquarters.com/p/panthers-defense-disguise-entropy-safety-steals)[[12]](https://x.com/The_Coach_A/status/2114681102509977997)

**Defensive shell disguise can be quantified via Kullback-Leibler divergence D_KL(P_post || P_pre), which measures the information loss or “surprise” when the post-snap coverage probability distribution diverges from the QB’s pre-snap expectation.** High D_KL indicates major rotational change (e.g., two-high to single-high spin or vice versa), directly invalidating the pre-snap mental model. NFL Next Gen Stats has productionized pre- versus post-snap coverage classification from tracking data precisely to flag these disguises.[[13]](https://www.nfl.com/news/next-gen-stats-new-advanced-metrics-you-need-to-know-for-the-2025-nfl-season)

**This interacts with QB head scan velocity (speed of head turns and correlated saccades to acquire new safety/LB landmarks) and Turnover-Worthy Play (TWP/TOW) hazard.** Slow head-scan QBs cannot sample the changed picture quickly enough; the higher the D_KL (greater disguise entropy), the steeper the rise in TWP rate because the first read is often wrong and progression time is compressed by simulated pressure. Fast scanners mitigate this by confirming rotation early and distributing attention across full-field keys.[[14]](https://www.capitalqbs.com/quarterback-development-why-your-head-and-eyes-post-snap-define-the-play-before-it-starts/)

Public data does not publish exact regression coefficients, but conceptual and tracking-derived models show the relationship is nonlinear: modest disguise (low D_KL) is manageable with average scan velocity, while high-divergence looks (Flores zero-look, Creeper/Penny combos) against low-velocity lock-on QBs produce outsized TWP spikes, matching observed INT/sack increases on 3rd-and-long dropbacks. Coverage Execution Index-style metrics that isolate forced TOW% independent of pass rush further support that disguise-driven decision pressure, not just raw pressure, drives turnovers.[[15]](https://www.matchquarters.com/p/introducing-the-coverage-execution-index)

In summary, post-snap safety spin compresses the QB’s decision window to under a second, 1st-read dependent QBs with PA success are structurally vulnerable to disguise in obvious passing situations, and the interaction of distributional surprise (KL), scan speed, and TWP hazard explains why certain profiles crater against modern simulated-pressure schemes. Elite processors counter with rapid post-snap confirmation, full-field eyes, and pre-snap shell discipline rather than rigid alerts. Defenses continue evolving these tools because the data shows they work.

### Wave-3A: WR/TE Route Efficiency, Target Share, Air Yards Share & Opportunity Metrics (YPRR, TPRR, WOPR, RACR) (`grok-4-latest` · `completed` · `226.99s` · `$0.1243`)

**Yards Per Route Run (YPRR)** is calculated as **Receiving Yards / Routes Run**. It measures receiving production per route opportunity (including untargeted routes), distinguishing it from yards per target or per reception.[[1]](https://www.footballnationusa.com/post/yards-per-route-run-yprr-fantasy-football-explained)[[2]](https://sticktothemodel.com/encyclopedia/yards-per-route-run)

Routes run are charted (not available in raw play-by-play) on pass plays where the receiver releases into a pattern. Standard exclusions include pure pass-blocking snaps, run plays, spikes, and QB kneels. Most charting sources (PFF, SIS, Next Gen Stats since 2023) count a route on pass plays including sacks and scrambles if the player released into a pattern.[[2]](https://sticktothemodel.com/encyclopedia/yards-per-route-run)[[2]](https://sticktothemodel.com/encyclopedia/yards-per-route-run)

Provider differences exist in edge cases:
- **Fantasy Points Data** is stricter: It does not count “blocking on screens” or “run-blocking on RPOs” as routes.[[3]](https://x.com/FantasyPtsData/status/1839692873433796881)
- **PFF** is generally more inclusive/liberal with route charting.
- **FTN** charts RPOs explicitly (via an is_rpo flag) and has detailed play-type data, but specific route-vs-blocking distinctions align more closely with stricter approaches in some analyses.[[4]](https://nflreadr.nflverse.com/articles/dictionary_ftn_charting.html)

**Targets Per Route Run (TPRR)** is **Targets / Routes Run** (often expressed as a percentage or rate, e.g., 0.20 or 20%). It shows how frequently a player earns targets on the routes they actually run.[[5]](https://www.footballnationusa.com/post/targets-per-route-run-fantasy-football-tprr)[[6]](https://www.thefantasyfootballers.com/analysis/fantasy-football-advanced-metrics-glossary/)

**First Downs Per Route Run (1D/RR)** is **First Downs / Routes Run**. It serves as an efficiency/volume hybrid metric, sometimes showing stronger predictive correlations to future fantasy points than YPRR alone in certain analyses.[[7]](https://web.archive.org/web/20250710142608id_/https:/www.fantasypoints.com/nfl/articles/season/2024/statistically-significant-first-downs-per-route-run)

**Target Share** is **Targets / Team Pass Attempts** (or team targets/dropbacks, depending on the exact denominator used by the provider; it represents a player’s portion of the team’s passing opportunities).[[8]](https://fantasytools.ai/nfl-target-share-leaders/)

**Air Yards Share** (or Air Yards Market Share) is **Player Air Yards / Team Air Yards**. Air yards measure the distance a pass travels in the air to the target (on both completions and incompletions).[[9]](https://www.4for4.com/2018/preseason/air-yards-explained)[[10]](https://www.nbcsports.com/fantasy/football/news/article-numbers-why-receiver-air-yards-matter)

**Josh Hermsmeyer WOPR (Weighted Opportunity Rating)** is **1.5 × Target Share + 0.7 × Air Yards Share**. It combines target volume and air-yards involvement into a single opportunity metric, weighted toward predictive value for fantasy scoring. Elite WRs often post values around 0.70+.[[10]](https://www.nbcsports.com/fantasy/football/news/article-numbers-why-receiver-air-yards-matter)[[11]](https://www.fantasylife.com/articles/best-ball/what-are-air-yards-and-why-you-should-pay-attention)

**RACR (Receiver Air Conversion Ratio)** is **Receiving Yards / Air Yards** (sometimes specified as completed air yards + YAC divided by total air yards). It measures how efficiently a receiver converts air yards into actual receiving yards, incorporating elements of catch rate and YAC.[[9]](https://www.4for4.com/2018/preseason/air-yards-explained)[[10]](https://www.nbcsports.com/fantasy/football/news/article-numbers-why-receiver-air-yards-matter)

**Alignment rates** (Slot Rate, Wide Alignment Rate, Inline TE Rate) are percentages of a player’s snaps (or routes) from each pre-snap alignment:
- Slot: Inside the widest receiver but outside the inline tight end/tackle.
- Wide: Outside alignments.
- Inline (primarily for TEs): Attached to the line of scrimmage.[[12]](https://www.sportsinfosolutions.com/2026/10/08/tight-end-evolution/)[[13]](https://statrankings.com/nfl/advanced/players/alignment/slot-rate-te-wr-rb)

These are typically derived from charting data tracking pre-snap positioning.

**Pass Block Rate for TEs** is the percentage of pass plays (or relevant snaps/dropbacks) on which the tight end is assigned to pass-block/protect rather than run a route. It is often reported alongside route participation or role breakdowns and has trended downward league-wide as more TEs are used in receiving roles.[[12]](https://www.sportsinfosolutions.com/2026/10/08/tight-end-evolution/)

These metrics rely on charted data from providers like PFF, FTN, Fantasy Points Data, SIS, or Next Gen Stats; public approximations sometimes estimate routes from snaps and team pass volume. Always verify the specific provider’s methodology for the exact sample, as minor differences in route counting or play-type inclusion (e.g., RPOs, scrambles) can affect rates.

### Wave-3B: WR/TE Separation, Tracking & Film Charting Metrics (SEP SCORE, ASS, ESPN RTM, Reception Perception, NGS) (`grok-4.20-0309-reasoning` · `completed` · `63.26s` · `$0.1324`)

**1. @FantasyPtsData Average Separation Score (ASS) and SEP SCORE** is a manually charted metric (not automated tracking data) that evaluates receiver separation on a per-route basis across *every qualified route run*, not just targeted routes.[[1]](https://www.fantasypoints.com/nfl/articles/2024/what-is-ass-average-separation-score)[[2]](https://x.com/FantasyPtsData/status/1839692873433796881)

A team of human charters assigns a numeric grade on each route after accounting for context: route type (e.g., no credit for separation on most screens, jets, checkdowns, or underneath holes in zone where separation is scheme-driven rather than earned by the receiver), coverage shell (man/zone/press), leverage, whether the receiver was knocked off route, defender positioning, and "gray area" situations dependent on the receiver actually creating separation. Non-routes (e.g., blocking on screens or run-blocking on RPOs) are excluded entirely from route totals and separation charting.[[2]](https://x.com/FantasyPtsData/status/1839692873433796881)[[1]](https://www.fantasypoints.com/nfl/articles/2024/what-is-ass-average-separation-score)

The grading scale (described as -2 to +2 in the query but detailed publicly up to +4 for extreme cases) is approximately:[[1]](https://www.fantasypoints.com/nfl/articles/2024/what-is-ass-average-separation-score)

- **-2**: Completely knocked off route, entirely out of the play.
- **-1**: Tightly covered; would require exceptional QB effort/accuracy for a completion.
- **0**: No opportunity to create separation (includes most screens, checkdowns, or scheme-provided holes underneath zone).
- **+1**: Creates roughly one step of separation.
- **+2**: Creates multiple steps of separation.
- **+3**: Extreme separation.
- **+4**: Complete coverage bust; receiver wide open.

**ASS (Average Separation Score)** is the arithmetic mean of these per-route grades across all qualified routes in the sample (higher is better). It isolates the receiver’s route-running/separation creation independent of target volume or scheme-provided “openness.” SEP SCORE appears to refer to the same or a closely related per-route or aggregated separation metric in their charting ecosystem.[[1]](https://www.fantasypoints.com/nfl/articles/2024/what-is-ass-average-separation-score)[[3]](https://www.fantasypoints.com/nfl/articles/2025/statistically-significant-average-separation-score?ref=thedataschool.co.uk)

It correlates with targets per route run and fantasy points per route, with positive grades (especially on first-read throws) worth several times more production than neutral/negative grades. Stability and predictive work has been done (e.g., “Playing-time Adjusted Separation Score” or PASS using route shares). Data is available via paid Fantasy Points Data tools.[[3]](https://www.fantasypoints.com/nfl/articles/2025/statistically-significant-average-separation-score?ref=thedataschool.co.uk)

**2. ESPN Analytics Receiver Tracking Metrics (RTM by Seth Walder & Brian Burke)** use 10 Hz NFL Next Gen Stats player tracking data on *every route run* (targeted or not). They produce three component scores plus an overall on a 0–99 scale (50 ≈ league average). Each component sets a context-specific benchmark (route, coverage shell, leverage, defender positioning/speed/direction, depth, and related metrics) via machine learning models and measures how the receiver exceeds or falls short of it.[[4]](https://www.espn.com/nfl/story/_/id/34649390/espn-receiver-tracking-metrics-how-new-nfl-stats-work-open-catch-yac-scores)[[5]](https://abc7news.com/sports/takeaways-from-espn-receiver-tracking-metrics-who-leads-rankings/12274900/)

- **Open Score (0–99)**: Evaluates how open the receiver gets and the implied catch probability if targeted. Assessed ~0.2 seconds before pass release (to capture pre-throw positioning before defenders react to QB shoulders). Uses tracking-derived features (movement advantage, leverage, separation creation relative to route/coverage expectations). Position-invariant convolutional neural network (CNN) architecture is referenced in related technical talks for predicting catch probability from spatial-temporal player “dots.” Higher score = consistently creates more openness than expected for the play context.[[4]](https://www.espn.com/nfl/story/_/id/34649390/espn-receiver-tracking-metrics-how-new-nfl-stats-work-open-catch-yac-scores)[[6]](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=teg9SfzpMy0)

- **Catch Score (0–99)**: On targeted routes, measures ability to contest and complete the catch relative to expectation at ball arrival. Incorporates predicted catch probability from tracking (receiver/defenders’ positions, velocities) versus actual outcome (residual analysis). Controls for QB skill to isolate receiver. Combines general contextual expectation with play-specific prediction; described as a “better completion percentage from the QB perspective” solved via ML to avoid bias.[[4]](https://www.espn.com/nfl/story/_/id/34649390/espn-receiver-tracking-metrics-how-new-nfl-stats-work-open-catch-yac-scores)[[6]](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=teg9SfzpMy0)

- **YAC Score (0–99)**: At the moment of catch, predicts expected yards after catch from full 22-player tracking data (locations, directions, speeds, accelerations of receiver and all defenders/blockers, open space, number of defenders in path, angles, and related metrics). Measures how actual YAC exceeds or falls short of that dynamic benchmark.[[4]](https://www.espn.com/nfl/story/_/id/34649390/espn-receiver-tracking-metrics-how-new-nfl-stats-work-open-catch-yac-scores)

**Overall RTM Score**: Weighted linear combination of the three components calibrated to best predict real-world production (primarily yards per route run, with some yards per target). Open Score receives the heaviest weight (roughly half), followed by Catch Score (~quarter or slightly less), then YAC Score (remainder). Weights reflect that getting open is foundational; variance is higher in Catch/YAC, so metrics are most stable at the season level. All routes contribute to Open; targeted plays drive Catch and YAC.[[4]](https://www.espn.com/nfl/story/_/id/34649390/espn-receiver-tracking-metrics-how-new-nfl-stats-work-open-catch-yac-scores)[[5]](https://abc7news.com/sports/takeaways-from-espn-receiver-tracking-metrics-who-leads-rankings/12274900/)

RTMs are descriptive (explain *how* production occurs or fails) more than purely predictive, with Open being the most repeatable year-to-year. Updated weekly in-season on platforms like FiveThirtyEight/ESPN.[[7]](https://espnpressroom.com/feature/espn-analytics-debuts-new-receiver-tracking-metric-on-fivethirtyeight/)

**3. Matt Harmon Reception Perception** is a manual film-charting system by Harmon. It uses an eight-game sample per player (typically the four statistically best and four worst games to balance representation while reflecting full-season usage). Only “qualified” routes are counted—those on which the receiver could realistically have been targeted (screens where the receiver is purely blocking downfield are excluded).[[8]](https://www.thebackyardbanter.com/reception-perception-a-video-rundown-of-the-data.html)[[9]](https://receptionperception.com/introduction-to-reception-perception-and-its-new-home/)

It charts against the standard **9-route tree** (Slant, Flat, Comeback, Curl, Out, In/Dig, Corner, Post, Go/Nine), plus an “Other” category for non-tree routes.

**Key outputs**:
- **Route Percentage**: Frequency each route is run (as % of total qualified routes in sample). Shows usage/deployment.
- **Success Rate vs. Coverage (SRVC or “success rate”)**: Primary metric. Binary per-route determination of whether the receiver “gets open” (creates enough separation for a reasonable QB target window). Automatic success on obvious holds/interference (called or not). Success rate = successes / opportunities, expressed as a percentage (higher is better). Broken down vs. Man coverage, Zone coverage, Press coverage, Double coverage, and per route on the 9-route tree. Alignment (slot/outside, on/off LOS) is also tracked because it affects exposure to man/zone/press.[[8]](https://www.thebackyardbanter.com/reception-perception-a-video-rundown-of-the-data.html)[[9]](https://receptionperception.com/introduction-to-reception-perception-and-its-new-home/)

There is no numeric per-route grade like -2 to +2; it is a binary “open/not open” charted judgment that yields percentages and percentiles. Historical data shows success rate vs. man is particularly predictive of future production (roughly 1.5–1.75× more valuable per percentage point than vs. zone for fantasy points/receptions), with thresholds like >70% vs. man or elite combined marks (75%+ man / 80%+ zone/press) indicating high-end talent. Route success rates contextualize usage (e.g., a high % of vertical routes with strong success on them).[[10]](https://receptionperception.com/assessing-the-effect-of-reception-perception-success-rates-and-alignment-on-statistical-production/)

Data is available via subscription at ReceptionPerception.com; it has been used for prospect and NFL player evaluation for over a decade.

**4. NFL Next Gen Stats (NGS) separation and YAC metrics** are derived from official 10 Hz (or higher) player tracking data (chip in every player’s shoulder pads). They focus on measurable quantities at specific timestamps (snap, pass arrival, catch) and model-based expectations.[[11]](https://nextgenstats.nfl.com/glossary)

- **Average Cushion (CUSH)**: On targeted routes, the average distance (yards) between the WR/TE and the nearest aligned defender at the snap. Measures how much “space” the receiver is given off the line.[[11]](https://nextgenstats.nfl.com/glossary)

- **Average Target Separation (or Average Separation / SEP)**: Average distance (yards) between the receiver and the nearest defender at the moment the pass arrives (or at catch/incompletion). Higher values indicate more space created at the catch point. Often reported on targets only.[[11]](https://nextgenstats.nfl.com/glossary)

- **Expected YAC (xYAC)**: Model-predicted yards after catch at the instant of reception. Inputs include receiver speed, direction, acceleration; distance to nearest defender(s); number of defenders and blockers in the projected path/open space; angles; open-space area (accounting for velocities/directions of all relevant players); and other tracking-derived features. The model first estimates “yards gained before close-in” (strong predictor of actual YAC, r² ≈ 0.64 in training), then incorporates open-space concepts to output full xYAC. It differentiates skill by comparing to what an average player would do in identical tracking circumstances.[[12]](https://www.nfl.com/news/next-gen-stats-intro-to-expected-yards-after-catch-0ap3000000983644)

- **YAC Over Expected (YACOE or YAC Above Expectation / YAC +/-)**: Simple residual = Actual YAC − xYAC. Positive values indicate elusiveness, vision, or acceleration beyond what tracking suggests; aggregated as a per-target or seasonal average. NGS also applies empirical Bayes shrinkage in some derived versions for stability.[[11]](https://nextgenstats.nfl.com/glossary)[[13]](https://www.nfeloapp.com/analysis/over-expected-explained-what-are-cpoe-ryoe-and-yacoe/)

These are published on NFL.com/NextGenStats, with leaderboards, per-play data available via APIs or sites like nflreadr, and used in broader models (e.g., completion probability also incorporates separation). They are objective tracking outputs or model residuals rather than subjective charting.[[12]](https://www.nfl.com/news/next-gen-stats-intro-to-expected-yards-after-catch-0ap3000000983644)

Each system isolates receiver skill differently: FantasyPtsData and Reception Perception via comprehensive human charting of all (qualified) routes with contextual judgment; ESPN RTM via ML on full tracking for every route with component decomposition; NGS via direct measurements and expectation models at key timestamps. They complement one another—charted nuance for scheme/route context versus scalable tracking-derived expectations. Full proprietary details (exact ML architectures, exact weighting coefficients, or charter training manuals) are not always public.

### Wave-3D: WR/TE Micro-Route Biomechanics, Stem Pacing, Hip-Sink Deceleration (BRK+) & Blind-Spot Leverage (`grok-4.20-0309-reasoning` · `completed` · `47.55s` · `$0.1182`)

**NFL wide receiver and tight end route running at the micro level relies on 10 Hz player tracking data (sampling location, speed, acceleration, direction, and orientation 10 times per second) combined with All-22 film to reveal biomechanics and spatial leverage.**[[1]](https://operations.nfl.com/operations/game-operations-logistics/technology/performance-tracking-data-next-gen-stats)[[2]](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.17866v3)

Next Gen Stats (NGS) and similar RFID/Zebra-derived datasets quantify these movements frame-by-frame, while film shows the visual deception, hip displacement, center-of-mass (CoM) control, stride modulation, and hand usage that tracking numbers only imply. Elite WRs (such as Jaxon Smith-Njigba, Cooper Kupp, Keenan Allen, Tyreek Hill) and TEs (Travis Kelce, George Kittle) excel by manipulating defender perception and reaction windows within the ~250–350 ms human visual-motor delay.[[3]](https://app.getsupers.com/sites/wr-route-separation-lab-34/)

### 1. Stem Pacing & False Acceleration (0–12 yards)
Elite route runners maintain consistent stride length and visual “game speed” (often cruising near 85% of max velocity) to avoid telegraphing breaks. This lulls bail-technique cornerbacks or pattern-match defenders who read the stem and transition from backpedal to run only after the receiver appears committed vertically.[[4]](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uqW13r-ghaU)

Tracking shows smooth velocity curves without early chopping or deceleration indicators. On film, shoulders and pad level stay level; the “shoulder plane never dips,” and routes look identical until the last moment. JSN is frequently cited for this—fluid hip mobility, no wasted motion, and the ability to cut off the inside foot while staying level, preventing defenders from anticipating direction.[[5]](https://x.com/Buckeye937B/status/2118676540414218460)

False acceleration involves subtle bursts or “false pressure steps” that sell vertical while the receiver has already won inside leverage or prepared the break. This exploits the defender’s inertia in bail or off-coverage. TEs use similar stem pacing against linebackers or safeties, leveraging size to maintain controlled speed before a sudden shift. The result: the defender opens hips or commits vertically too late, creating a 2–4 yard window by the break point.

### 2. Top-of-Route Braking Efficiency (BRK+) & Center-of-Mass Hip Sink
This is the core of separation on breaking routes (Comeback, Dig, Curl, Out). Elite players achieve rapid deceleration (often sub-0.35–0.40 seconds for break + cut time, top-tier per NGS-derived metrics) by violently dropping the hips and CoM rather than slowly sinking or chopping steps early.[[3]](https://app.getsupers.com/sites/wr-route-separation-lab-34/)

**Biomechanically**, a “violent hip drop” or “snap down” (analogized as musical chairs or a string pulling the chin to the knee) lowers the CoM quickly, allowing the feet to brake hard into the ground while preserving balance for re-acceleration. Coaching breakdowns emphasize maintaining full stride and speed into the breakpoint, then using the inside or plant foot to pivot explosively. Slow sinks cause drift, extra steps, and telegraphing; violent drops minimize ground contact time and allow exit velocity preservation.[[4]](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=uqW13r-ghaU)[[6]](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=19NYNg5xyp0)

Quantitatively, WRs cover significant high-intensity deceleration distance per game. NGS-derived “Break Decel & Cut Time” around 0.34 seconds ranks in the top few percent and correlates with elite separation on digs, curls, and outs.[[7]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/373184826_Positional_Movement_Demands_during_NFL_Football_Games_A_3-Year_Review) Against a defender’s 250–350 ms reaction lag, this creates an effective “free” window: the receiver has already changed direction and begun re-accelerating before the defender processes the hip sink and cuts.

On Comebacks and Curls, the sink is paired with a rocker or trigger step for tight redirection. Digs and Outs emphasize hip/shoulder violence to “throw the hip” into the cut while keeping toes forward for push-off. Exit-angle velocity preservation is visible in tracking as minimal speed loss post-cut and high yards after catch (YAC). Kupp and Allen exemplify smooth, violent transitions; taller or less explosive receivers compensate with sharper knee bend and body control.[[8]](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=vP1kSQCrNEQ)

### 3. Blind-Spot Entry & Leverage Stealing
Receivers attack a cornerback’s “high-hip” posture (elevated hips in backpedal or transition) or blind spot, especially in trail-man (defender chasing from behind or inside hip) and pattern-match Quarters. Stem leverage manipulates the defender’s hips and eyes—selling vertical or one direction to force an open hip, then crossing face or breaking into the created blind spot.[[9]](https://www.musiccitymiracles.com/2018/7/31/17627358/malcolm-butler-and-the-trail-technique)

In trail or match quarters, the receiver uses consistent stem pacing to close cushion to 2–3 yards, then exploits the defender’s transition lag. Press-release hand-fighting in the first 5 yards (≤0.8 s at typical release speeds) is critical: active hands, throw-by moves, elbow aiming, and maintaining separation without pass interference. Win rate here determines whether the receiver gains clean inside/outside leverage or stays contested.[[10]](https://x.com/RouteUniverse9/status/2016302593383793090)

Film shows intentional releases (split, switch, or stalk) that force defenders to declare leverage early. JSN and similar technicians use subtle head fakes, shoulder leans, and unorthodox footwork to disguise intent. TEs add physicality—using size to “ride” or shed press while stealing leverage against slower underneath defenders. The physics is spatial: by controlling the defender’s hip angle and vector, the receiver creates throwing windows the defense cannot close in time.[[11]](https://www.footballnationusa.com/post/what-is-switch-release-football)

### 4. Hot-Route Sight-Adjustment Synchronization with the QB
When a nickel or safety triggers a post-snap blitz (creep toward the line, abnormal alignment), elite offenses rely on pre-programmed “hot” rules or sight adjustments. Both QB and receiver must read the same keys—safety depth, linebacker creep, unblocked rusher—without verbal communication.[[12]](https://legionreport.com/hot-route-in-football-essential-tactics-for-effective-play-calling/)

The receiver “replaces the blitzer,” breaking the route off early (often to a slant, hitch, or sit route) using inside leverage, catching first and running second. The QB delivers on a quick 3–5 step timing window. Synchronization comes from shared rules: safeties moving up abnormally or press/bump alignments trigger the hot. Tracking and film show these as rapid route conversions with minimal hesitation, turning potential negative plays into positive EPA throws.[[13]](https://blogs.usafootball.com/blog/4307/follow-these-8-rules-for-quarterback-and-receiver-sight-adjustment)

TEs often serve as reliable hot options due to their alignment and blocking versatility. Success depends on exact conceptual alignment—misreads lead to sacks or interceptions. Players like Kupp or Kelce demonstrate this through consistent timing and quarterback trust.

**Overall**, these micro-elements combine deception (consistent stem, no tells), physics (rapid CoM shifts, stride efficiency, deceleration rates under 0.4 s), and spatial awareness (leverage, blind spots, hot reads). 10 Hz data quantifies the speed, acceleration, and separation; All-22 reveals the violent intent, hand usage, and eye discipline that make the numbers elite. Receivers who master violent hip sinks, non-telegraphed stems, and instantaneous adjustments consistently create windows against the defender’s reaction lag. This is where route running transcends raw speed.

### Wave-4A: Running Back Next Gen Stats RYOE (1D-CNN / Spatial Tracking), ROE% & Box Defender Physics (`grok-4.20-0309-reasoning` · `completed` · `89.33s` · `$0.1659`)

[[1]](https://www.nfl.com/news/next-gen-stats-intro-to-expected-rushing-yards)[[2]](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/nfl-big-data-bowl-2020/writeups/the-zoo-1st-place-solution-the-zoo)[[3]](https://sticktothemodel.com/encyclopedia/rushing-yards-over-expected)[[1]](https://www.nfl.com/news/next-gen-stats-intro-to-expected-rushing-yards)[[4]](https://www.sharpfootballanalysis.com/stats-nfl/nfl-offensive-personnel/)[[5]](https://raycarp.com/nfl/personnel)[[6]](https://cheddarheadanalytics.com/learn/advanced-stats-glossary)**NGS RYOE derives from a spatial/2D convolutional neural network developed by Philipp Singer and Dmitry Gordeev (“The Zoo”), who won the 2020 NFL Big Data Bowl by a wide margin.**[[1]](https://www.nfl.com/news/next-gen-stats-intro-to-expected-rushing-yards)[[2]](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/nfl-big-data-bowl-2020/writeups/the-zoo-1st-place-solution-the-zoo)

The competition tasked participants with predicting the yardage outcome of rushing plays using NGS player-tracking data available at the moment of handoff. The winning entry used only kinematic and spatial features at that instant and produced a full probability distribution over possible outcomes, which NGS adapted and productionized (with later expansions, such as for quarterback runs).[[7]](https://www.geekwire.com/2020/nfl-reveals-new-expected-rush-yards-stat-help-amazon-web-services/)[[8]](https://www.aboutamazon.com/news/aws/from-start-to-finish-aws-powers-this-new-nfl-next-gen-stat)

### Input Tensor and Architecture
NGS tracking data is sampled at approximately 10 Hz. The model ingests the specific frame at (or immediately around) handoff. For all 22 players (11 offensive including the ball carrier + 11 defensive), the core inputs are:

- Coordinates (x, y), conventionally oriented with the line of scrimmage and direction of play.
- Velocities (v_x, v_y), derived from speed (S) and direction (Dir): v_x ≈ S × cos(Dir), v_y ≈ S × sin(Dir), or equivalent projections.
- Relative displacements to the ball carrier (dx, dy for each player relative to the rusher’s position).
- Derived closing features, including relative speeds toward the rusher and closing angles (angle between a defender’s velocity vector and the line connecting the defender to the ball carrier’s position/orientation).

These are engineered into *relative* features centered on the rusher, removing absolute field position and individual player identities/labels where possible. The data is reshaped into a tensor structured around defense-versus-offense pairwise interactions, with multiple channels corresponding to the five key vector features (location and speed components projected on X and Y axes, yielding ~10 numeric channels). Additional minor features were tested but contributed little.[[2]](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/nfl-big-data-bowl-2020/writeups/the-zoo-1st-place-solution-the-zoo)

The architecture is a 2D convolutional neural network (spatial convnet) that treats the defense-by-offense matrix as an “image.” Early convolutional layers (with activation and pooling) learn pairwise blocker-defender geometric relationships relative to the rusher—capturing how likely a defender is to reach the ball carrier unimpeded versus how effectively an offensive player can impede that path. Subsequent layers aggregate these interactions into a higher-level representation of the overall “situation” at handoff. The model assumes limited immediate cooperation among defenders at the exact handoff instant and focuses overwhelmingly on these spatial/velocity relationships; traditional contextual variables (down, distance, formation) added little marginal value in the winning solution.[[9]](https://www.sportperformanceanalysis.com/article/how-the-nfl-developed-expected-rushing-yards-with-the-big-data-bowl)

### Output and Derived Metrics
The network outputs a probability distribution (typically discretized probabilities or a CDF) over yards gained, covering a practical range such as −10 to +99 yards to account for losses, short gains, and breakaway runs. Training optimized Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS), which rewards well-calibrated distributions across the entire outcome space rather than point estimates.[[10]](https://www.kaggle.com/c/nfl-big-data-bowl-2020/overview)

**Expected Rushing Yards (xRY)** is the expectation under this distribution: xRY = ∑ (yards_k × p(yards = k)) or the equivalent integral/mean from the CDF. It represents the yardage an average runner would be expected to gain given the exact alignment, velocities, displacements, and closing geometry at handoff.[[1]](https://www.nfl.com/news/next-gen-stats-intro-to-expected-rushing-yards)

**Rushing Yards Over Expected (RYOE)** for a play = actual yards gained − xRY. Positive values mean the ball carrier created more than the situation provided; negative means the opposite. Aggregates yield:

- **RYOE/Att** = total RYOE ÷ rushing attempts (average value created or lost per carry). Seasonal leaders in RYOE/Att are typically in the +0.5 to +1.0+ range for elite seasons.[[3]](https://sticktothemodel.com/encyclopedia/rushing-yards-over-expected)
- **ROE% (Rushes/Rush Percentage Over Expected)** = percentage of carries on which actual yards > xRY for that specific play.

NGS publishes these metrics weekly and historically; the underlying model has been refined and extended since the original Big Data Bowl entry.[[11]](https://x.com/NextGenStats/status/1303766971045965824)

### 2. Defenders in the Box and Personnel Groupings
“Defenders in the Box” counts defensive players aligned in the box (roughly between the offensive tackles and within a few yards of the line of scrimmage) at the snap. Standard NGS/analytics thresholds are:

- **Light box**: ≤6 defenders (offense-friendly; creates one-on-one or numerical advantages in gaps).
- **Neutral**: 7-man box.
- **Stacked/Heavy box**: 8+ defenders (defense loaded to stop the run, raising the bar for positive RYOE).[[12]](https://www.footballnationusa.com/post/stacked-box-count-run-scheme-fantasy-football)

Offensive **personnel groupings** (first digit = RBs/FBs, second = TEs; remainder WRs, with 5 OL + QB fixed) mechanically influence these counts and resulting efficiency:

- **11 personnel** (1 RB, 1 TE, 3 WR): Dominant passing set. Three WRs pull defenses into nickel/dime packages with lighter boxes (frequently 6 or fewer). This often produces favorable light-box rushing opportunities and higher RYOE/Att when the run is called.
- **12 personnel** (1 RB, 2 TE, 2 WR): Balanced run/pass. The extra TE adds a run-blocking body, typically increasing box count toward neutral (7) or light-heavy. Efficiency depends on whether the TE wins at the point of attack or creates extra gaps.
- **13 personnel** (1 RB, 3 TE, 1 WR): Heavy run/goal-line set. Three TEs bunch the formation and demand extra defenders in the box (often 8+), but supply maximum in-box blockers for power/gap schemes. High-variance efficiency.
- **21 personnel** (2 RB, 1 TE, 2 WR) and **22 personnel** (2 RB, 2 TE, 1 WR): Traditional power-run groupings with lead blockers/fullbacks. These reliably draw heavier boxes (neutral to stacked) as defenses match personnel with linebackers instead of extra DBs. Success requires precise blocking and vision to overcome the numbers.[[4]](https://www.sharpfootballanalysis.com/stats-nfl/nfl-offensive-personnel/)[[5]](https://raycarp.com/nfl/personnel)

The interplay is strategic: heavier personnel (higher TE/RB counts) signals run intent and invites stacked boxes, but supplies additional blockers that can generate positive RYOE if the scheme and execution prevail. Lighter 11 personnel can “manipulate” defenses into light boxes. Analysts therefore bucket rushing efficiency (YPC, EPA/play, RYOE/Att, success rate) by both box count and personnel to isolate scheme, blocking, and individual runner contributions.

### 3. Time Behind Line of Scrimmage (TLOS) and Efficiency
**TLOS (Time to/Behind Line of Scrimmage)** is the average time (seconds) from snap until the ball carrier reaches or crosses the line of scrimmage, measured via tracking data. Lower TLOS reflects decisive, vision-driven runs that hit the hole quickly. Higher TLOS indicates hesitation, blocked lanes requiring cutbacks, or patience while blocks develop. It is best interpreted alongside RYOE/Att—some runners generate positive value even with moderate TLOS by creating yards after contact.[[6]](https://cheddarheadanalytics.com/learn/advanced-stats-glossary)

**Efficiency** = total distance traveled by the ball carrier (path length integrated from 10 Hz tracking, summing all lateral, backward, and forward movement) ÷ north-south yards gained (net forward progress, i.e., the official rushing yards). The ratio is always ≥1. Lower values (roughly 2.8–3.5 for qualifying seasons) indicate direct, north-south runners who minimize wasted movement. Higher values (4.0–5.0+) characterize elusive, jittery runners who use lateral agility and cutbacks, traveling significantly more ground for each yard of forward progress. It complements RYOE by separating power/vision from pure elusiveness and path efficiency.[[6]](https://cheddarheadanalytics.com/learn/advanced-stats-glossary)

Together, these tracking-derived metrics (RYOE/xRY from the spatial convnet at handoff, box counts contextualized by personnel, TLOS, and path Efficiency) give a far richer picture of rushing performance than raw yards per carry, isolating what the runner creates versus what the blocking, alignment, and defensive pursuit provide. ### Wave-4B: Running Back Contact, Elusiveness & Blocking Splits (YBC/Att, YACo/Att, MTF/Att, Elusive Rating, Stuff Rate) (`grok-4-latest` · `completed` · `244.24s` · `$0.1081`)

**Yards Before Contact per Attempt (YBC/Att) and Yards After Contact per Attempt (YACo/Att or YAC/Att)** measure how rushing yards split around the point of first defender contact with the ball carrier. YBC/Att captures pre-contact yardage (often tied more to blocking, scheme, vision, and line play), while YACo/Att captures post-contact yardage (more reflective of the runner’s elusiveness, power, balance, and tackle-breaking).[[1]](https://www.pro-football-reference.com/about/advanced_stats.htm)[[2]](https://www.footballnationusa.com/post/yards-before-contact-vs-after-contact-fantasy-football)

Providers define “first contact” via play charting (not automated tracking alone), typically as the initial physical touch by a defender on the ball carrier. This is distinct from mere proximity, defensive penetration into the backfield without touch, or a grazing arm that does not constitute a tackle attempt. Exact thresholds can vary slightly by provider due to subjective charting judgments (e.g., whether a light arm swipe counts versus a committed tackle attempt or solid contact), but all major sources emphasize the first meaningful defender touch that allows attribution of subsequent yards to the runner.[[2]](https://www.footballnationusa.com/post/yards-before-contact-vs-after-contact-fantasy-football)[[3]](https://www.espn.com/espn/print?id=6712347)

- **PFF**: Uses detailed game charting to mark the exact contact point on each run. YBC is yards gained up to that point; YACo is yards gained afterward. PFF distinguishes runner-created space pre-contact and post-contact production, with high YACo often linked to missed tackles forced.[[4]](https://www.pff.com/news/fantasy-footabll-going-deeper-on-yards-before-and-after-contact)[[5]](https://www.pff.com/news/fantasy-football-using-yards-after-contact-over-expected-evaluate-running-backs)
- **Pro-Football-Reference (PFR)**: YBC/YAC defined as yards gained after first contact from a defender (with broken tackles also tracked separately). Data from 2018 onward via charting.[[1]](https://www.pro-football-reference.com/about/advanced_stats.htm)
- **SIS (Sports Info Solutions)**: YAContact/Yards After Contact is additional yards after first defender contact. Missed tackles are logged when a defender is in position but the carrier eludes (with or without initial contact).[[6]](https://sisdatahub.com/glossary)
- **FTN/Fantasy (and Football Outsiders alignment)**: Similar charting-based split; YBC reflects team blocking/scheme, YACo the runner’s individual contribution post-contact.[[7]](https://ftnfantasy.com/stats/nfl/adjusted-line-yards)

League averages are typically around 1.3–1.6 YBC/Att and 2.6–3.0 YACo/Att in recent seasons, though these fluctuate with scheme (e.g., outside zone).[[5]](https://www.pff.com/news/fantasy-football-using-yards-after-contact-over-expected-evaluate-running-backs)

**Missed Tackles Forced per Attempt (MTF/Att) and per Touch (MTF/Touch)** count instances where a defender is positioned to tackle the ball carrier but fails to bring them down (elusion without contact or failure after initial contact). MTF/Att divides by rushing attempts; MTF/Touch uses total touches (rushes + receptions). These isolate runner elusiveness independent of blocking.[[8]](https://nfldraft.sportsinfosolutions.com/glossary?ref=bandit.football)

**PFF Elusive Rating** combines forced missed tackles (on rushes and receptions) with post-contact production:  
**Elusive Rating = (MTF_total / (Designed_Rush_Att + Receptions)) × YACo_per_Att × 100**  
(Exact PFF variants use total rushes + receptions in the denominator and multiply the rate by YACo/Att scaled by 100; qualification often requires ~100 touches.) This produces a scale roughly 1–100 emphasizing tackle-breaking frequency and yards created afterward.[[9]](https://www.pff.com/news/signature-stats-elusive-rating)[[10]](https://www.pff.com/news/signature-stats-snapshot-elusive-rating)

**Stuff Rate** is the percentage of carries stopped at or behind the line of scrimmage (gain ≤ 0 yards). It measures negative or zero-yard runs and is a key efficiency indicator (lower is better).[[7]](https://ftnfantasy.com/stats/nfl/adjusted-line-yards)

**Line Yards and Open-Field Yards** (Football Outsiders / FTN) use a piecewise weighting on rushing plays to attribute credit:

- Losses: 120% (1.20) value to the line.
- 0–4 yards: 100% (1.00) value.
- 5–10 yards: 50% (0.50) value.
- 11+ yards: 0% value (attributed fully to the runner or open-field play).

These feed into **Adjusted Line Yards** (further adjusted for down/distance, situation, opponent, and formation). **Second-Level Yards** are the portion of average yards gained 5–10 yards past the LOS (per carry; midpoint attribution between line and back). **Open-Field Yards** are the portion gained more than 10 yards past the LOS (per carry; fully attributed to the runner).[[7]](https://ftnfantasy.com/stats/nfl/adjusted-line-yards)[[11]](https://web.archive.org/web/20160114042009/http:/www.footballoutsiders.com/info/methods)

**Explosive Run Rate** (or Gash Rate in some FTN contexts) is the percentage of carries gaining at least 10 yards (sometimes thresholds of 12 or 15 yards are used for “explosive” or “big play” variants).[[12]](https://ftnfantasy.com/nfl/exploring-ftn-data-advanced-rb-metrics)

**Rushing Success Rate** measures consistency: a carry succeeds if it gains ≥40% of yards to go on 1st down, ≥60% on 2nd down, or 100% (conversion) on 3rd/4th down. An alternative or complementary definition uses EPA > 0 (positive expected points added).[[13]](https://www.espn.com/espn/print?id=3079031)

These metrics come from charting-heavy sources (PFF, SIS, FTN/Football Outsiders, PFR) and are not interchangeable across providers without aligning contact definitions. Data availability and exact thresholds can evolve seasonally.

### Wave-4C: Running Back Run Concept Splits (Duo/Power/Counter vs Inside/Outside Zone) & Backfield Workload Share (`grok-4-latest` · `completed` · `254.12s` · `$0.2215`)

**1) Run Concept Taxonomy & Splits**

NFL analysts from the specified accounts (particularly @SumerSports, @PFF, and supporting data from @FantasyPtsData) track a standardized set of run concepts that capture the core blocking schemes and back reads. These include:

- **Inside Zone (IZ)**: Downhill scheme where the offensive line steps laterally in one direction, creating double teams; the back reads the defensive tackles and can cut back. Often the most frequent concept league-wide (historically ~28%+ of rushes in some seasons). Teams like the Commanders, Eagles, and Cowboys have run high rates.[[1]](https://sumersports.com/the-zone/translating-zone-and-gap-run-schemes-from-college-to-pro/)[[2]](https://www.pff.com/news/breaking-down-pffs-run-concepts)

- **Outside Zone / Wide Zone (OZ)**: Lateral stretch scheme (also called stretch) designed to move the defense sideways; the back takes a wide track and reads the edge defender, with cutback options. High-volume teams include the Falcons, 49ers, and Texans. Backside blocks are critical for cutbacks.[[1]](https://sumersports.com/the-zone/translating-zone-and-gap-run-schemes-from-college-to-pro/)[[2]](https://www.pff.com/news/breaking-down-pffs-run-concepts)

- **Duo (often grouped under Man blocking)**: Straight-ahead man/gap scheme with no pulling linemen and frequent double-team opportunities at the point of attack (e.g., two linemen doubling a defensive tackle). Common for teams like the Rams, Bills, and Bengals.[[1]](https://sumersports.com/the-zone/translating-zone-and-gap-run-schemes-from-college-to-pro/)

- **Power**: Gap scheme featuring one pulling offensive lineman from the backside to the frontside, with predetermined point of attack and frontside blocks to clear the first level. Effective against certain fronts; historically strong YPC in data.[[2]](https://www.pff.com/news/breaking-down-pffs-run-concepts)[[2]](https://www.pff.com/news/breaking-down-pffs-run-concepts)

- **Counter (GT/GY)**: Similar to power but with a second backside blocker pulling (often the guard and/or tackle/fullback); the puller often reaches the hole first. Used in smaller volumes but can be explosive.[[2]](https://www.pff.com/news/breaking-down-pffs-run-concepts)

- **Trap/Wham**: Quick-hitting gap concepts with a lineman or tight end trapping/blocking a down lineman from the backside; wham often involves a tight end or fullback. Part of PFF's tracked concepts alongside draws.[[3]](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-highest-graded-running-backs-run-concept)

- **Pin-and-Pull / Toss / Crack Sweep**: Pin-and-pull involves linemen pinning defenders inside while others pull; toss and crack sweeps are perimeter or edge schemes often involving crack blocks from receivers or tight ends. These fall under broader gap or zone-stretch variants in detailed charting.

- **Draw**: Play-action or delayed handoff, often from shotgun/pistol, designed to exploit aggressive pass rushes; tracked by PFF as a distinct concept.[[4]](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=WyvbEC2KrAY)

@SumerSports regularly posts team-level run concept mixes (e.g., through Week 7/8 data) and allows filters by concept alongside box count for context.[[5]](https://x.com/SumerSports/status/2115137702068736341)[[6]](https://sumersports.com/players/running-back/) @PFF provides grades and volume splits by concept (e.g., highest-graded backs on IZ, OZ, power, counter, trap) with minimum attempt thresholds and notes on how concepts perform against specific fronts.[[3]](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-highest-graded-running-backs-run-concept)[[3]](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-highest-graded-running-backs-run-concept)

**OL blocking profiles vs. opposing DL stunt/blitz and gap-fit tendencies**: Zone schemes (IZ/OZ) rely on lateral movement and double teams, making them more adaptable to stunts/blitzes as linemen can adjust on the fly and create cutback lanes; however, they can struggle against well-coordinated gap fits or heavy boxes if the defense flows laterally effectively. Gap schemes (Power, Counter, Duo) feature more predictable pullers and predetermined points of attack, which can exploit over-aggressive stunts or light boxes but are vulnerable to well-timed blitzes or disciplined gap fits that fill the intended hole. PFF analysis on fronts (e.g., Bear fronts, even fronts, over/under) shows directional efficiencies varying by concept—e.g., zone runs can have different success against one- vs. three-technique alignments, while gap concepts like counter often align better against certain over fronts.[[7]](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-how-to-attack-even-fronts-in-the-running-game)[[8]](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-bear-front-effectiveness-zone-runs-2021) @SumerSports and @PFF emphasize contextual filters (box count, front type) because raw volume without scheme/defensive interaction context misleads. Small samples are flagged as unreliable.[[6]](https://sumersports.com/players/running-back/)

### Wave-4D: RB Vision, Pace-to-Hole, Collision Contact Angle Physics & Pass-Protection Blitz Pick-Up Veto Gate (`grok-4.20-0309-reasoning` · `completed` · `170.74s` · `$0.1025`)

**These second- and third-order mechanics separate scheme-fit chess pieces from athletic specialists in the NFL.** Pass protection reliability functions as a hard veto on situational snap share. Scheme-specific pace and landmark manipulation turn running backs into extensions of the offensive line’s leverage game. Collision outcomes only become predictive once decomposed by impact geometry and relative momentum. Analytics (especially PFF), film study, coaching breakdowns, and physics-based modeling all support these layers.[[1]](https://www.pff.com/news/2011-pass-blocking-efficiency-running-backs)[[2]](https://www.theringer.com/2018/08/16/nfl/running-back-pass-protection)

### 1. Pass-Protection Blitz Pick-Up Veto Gate
PFF’s Pass Block Efficiency (PBE) quantifies pressure avoidance: PBE = (1 – ((Sacks + 0.75 × (Hits + Hurries)) / Pass Blocking Snaps)) × 100. It rewards volume without negative events. Backs like Fred Jackson (perfect or near-perfect seasons with zero pressures on high volume) or Maurice Jones-Drew (100.0 PBE on 102 snaps) set historical benchmarks. Recent standouts such as Javonte Williams (high snap volume with 97.9% efficiency) and Travis Etienne Jr. illustrate sustained trust.[[1]](https://www.pff.com/news/2011-pass-blocking-efficiency-running-backs)[[3]](https://x.com/WerderEdNFL/status/1996598493482242326)

The deeper layer is *mental* reliability: pre-snap scan, post-snap eye discipline, and instant identification of pressure packages. A-gap cross-dog (rushers crossing paths to create assignment conflicts between guard, back, and center) versus nickel cat blitz (slot or overhang pressure with simulated or looped rushers) demands distinct “most dangerous man” or hot/read rules. Coaches film the back’s eyes, first step, and assignment accuracy on every dropback. A single miss creates a free runner on the quarterback—catastrophic on third-and-medium, in the two-minute drill, or while trailing (when pass frequency spikes and protection must hold without max help).[[4]](https://www.footballnationusa.com/post/what-is-blitz-pickup-football)

This creates an absolute coaching trust gate. As Rams GM Les Snead noted, in the modern game the “third-down back means most reliable pass protector.” Inability to handle complex protections forces scat protection (back releases immediately into a check-down route), limits the playbook, and caps the offense’s ability to keep the back on the field or protect the quarterback on obvious passing downs. Explosive runners who fail here still earn early-down carries but are substituted or game-planned around in high-leverage situations. Rookies like early Christian McCaffrey posted poor PBE (78.9 on limited snaps) before improving; non-rookies with persistently low marks (sub-90 range) saw usage restrictions. Snap share in pass-heavy or critical scripts is gated by this skill far more than pure rushing explosiveness. Recent high-volume, high-efficiency backs earn multi-down trust precisely because coaches believe they will not compromise the pocket.[[2]](https://www.theringer.com/2018/08/16/nfl/running-back-pass-protection)

### 2. Pace-to-Hole & Linebacker Manipulation on Duo vs Wide Zone
Elite backs treat the hole as a dynamic landmark rather than a fixed aiming point. They calibrate entry velocity and body position to influence second-level defenders before the ball crosses the line.

**Duo** (gap/power without a puller) relies on vertical double teams at the point of attack and a patient read of the Mike linebacker. The back aligns deeper (often 8+ yards) for extra read time. By pressing the frontside double-team at controlled speed—roughly 65% effort—the runner occupies the Mike’s eyes and freezes his scrape or fill. The double team can establish vertical push and displacement; the Mike hesitates between fitting inside the double or pursuing over the top. This creates cutback windows (often B-gap weak or C-gap bounce) or allows the back to redirect explosively once the Mike declares. Data from thousands of Duo carries shows that redirecting or bouncing dramatically increases efficiency—13+ yards per carry in C/D-gap entries versus the “wear-down” reputation. Patience, vision of the Mike, and calibrated pace are non-negotiable; full-speed entry collapses the read and lets the linebacker flow freely.[[5]](https://www.footballfilmroom.com/p/duo)[[6]](https://www.xandolabs.com/the-lab/offense/run-game/tight-zone-run-concepts/the-duo-concept-power-without-a-puller/)

**Wide Zone** (outside zone/stretch) emphasizes horizontal displacement. The back presses the outside hip of the tight end (or equivalent landmark) to stretch the defensive front laterally, forcing edge defenders and linebackers to widen and honor the perimeter. This creates vertical one-cut seams inside once the flow declares. The initial pace is more lateral and patient to let the stretch develop, followed by a sharp vertical burst once the crease appears. The landmark and velocity profile differ markedly from Duo: one scheme freezes a linebacker inside via vertical pressure; the other stretches the entire front horizontally to open interior lanes.[[7]](https://www.canesinsight.com/articles/football-school-inside-zone-or-duo-understanding-the-running-game.8229/?page=1)

These are second- and third-order skills because raw burst or vision is insufficient without scheme-specific modulation. The back manipulates linebacker flow and fit through deliberate pace, landmark discipline, and post-mesh acceleration. Backs who master both concepts (pressing doubles on Duo to freeze the Mike before bouncing; stretching the perimeter on wide zone before cutting up) become every-down weapons in multiple schemes. Film and play-volume studies confirm that elite redirection and patience in Duo produce explosive outcomes far above the concept’s traditional reputation.[[8]](https://www.xandolabs.com/the-lab/offense/run-game/duo-run-concepts/trend-line-3-key-adjustments-turning-duo-run-schemes-into-an-explosive-play-generator/)

### 3. Collision Contact Angle Physics
Raw yards after contact per attempt (YACo/Att) is noisy and poorly isolates skill. It aggregates easy broken arm tackles with dominant wins against squared-up, momentum-matched defenders. Decomposition by tackler approach angle (*theta_impact*) and relative momentum (*p_RB / p_Def*) reveals true physical and technical quality.[[9]](https://mattwaldmanrsp.com/2019/07/11/matt-waldmans-rsp-yards-after-contact-theres-a-better-way/)

- **Glancing arm tackles** (*theta_impact* > 45°): These are indirect, side or rear-quarter hits. Leverage is poor for the defender; torque is low. The ball carrier can shed, spin, or run through with relatively modest power because the force vector has a large tangential component. Many high raw YAC totals are inflated by frequent successes against these attempts.
- **Squared-up head-on collisions** (*theta_impact* < 15°): Direct impacts align the force vectors closely with the runner’s path. The defender can drive through the runner’s center of mass. Winning these consistently requires superior pad level, leg drive, balance, and relative momentum advantage (*p = m × v*, with vector decomposition of the normal component). Physics-based models using conservation of momentum predict tackle-break probability and post-contact yards from combined momentum at impact; forward momentum at contact correlates strongly with both break likelihood and residual yards even on “successful” tackles.[[10]](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/17479541261489994)

A runner’s mass, incoming velocity, and post-contact footwork determine whether he can “fall forward,” maintain balance, or generate extra yards against a given defender’s momentum. Low center of mass, quick directional changes that steal angles, and deliberate final push all matter. Context from film—classifying reaches versus hit-and-wraps, direct versus indirect angles, and the specific defender’s leverage—turns noisy aggregate YACo/Att into a diagnostic tool. High raw totals can reflect avoidance of tough contact or exploitation of arm tackles; consistent positive outcomes on low-*theta*, high-relative-momentum collisions identify the backs who truly win at the point of impact.[[11]](https://www.columbiamissourian.com/sports/tiger_kickoff/choosing-the-vector-how-ahmad-hardy-harnesses-physics-to-break-tackles/article_95d8699c-29ee-4d0b-838f-8e927103e8c1.html)

These mechanics illustrate why running back evaluation must integrate PFF efficiency metrics, scheme-specific technique, film context on reads and pace, and physics-derived collision breakdowns. Raw explosiveness or aggregate stats miss the trust gate on passing downs, the calibrated manipulation of linebackers by scheme, and the angle-and-momentum reality of contact. Coaches and analysts who isolate these layers identify the backs who elevate entire offenses rather than merely contribute to them.

### Wave-4E: Third-Order Cross-Unit Interaction Tensors (Pocket Arc Depth x Edge Bend, Motion x LB Run-Fit Lag) (`grok-4.20-0309-reasoning` · `completed` · `53.41s` · `$0.1508`)

**These are advanced, interdependent NFL schematic interactions that go beyond basic blocking, run fits, or play-calling.** They involve geometry/timing across offensive and defensive units, pre-snap manipulation of post-snap responsibilities, and situational defensive tendencies shaped by game scripting and down-and-distance “tensors” (nonlinear spikes in aggressiveness).[[1]](https://www.sbnation.com/2016/11/25/13746680/nfl-pass-blocking-sets-best-offensive-linemen-joe-thomas-tyron-smith)[[2]](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-pre-snap-motion-usage-reaches-new-highs-across-the-nfl)

### 1. Offensive Tackle Pass-Set Angle & QB Drop Depth vs. Edge Rusher Arc Bend
Offensive tackles choose pass sets based on defender alignment, help inside, and expected quarterback depth: a **jump set** (quick engagement, often on play-action or 3-step drops), a **45-degree set** (aggressive angle with contact on the second kick step, used against wider alignments to close rush angles quickly while maintaining balance and inside-out leverage), or a **vertical set** (deeper drop-back to force the rusher wide, preserving square shoulders and inside leverage for longer-developing plays).[[1]](https://www.sbnation.com/2016/11/25/13746680/nfl-pass-blocking-sets-best-offensive-linemen-joe-thomas-tyron-smith)[[3]](https://www.rollbamaroll.com/2018/3/22/17149416/spring-study-hall-three-primary-types-of-pass-protection-alabama-crimson-tide)

The “spot” or intersect landmark for the tackle runs from the rusher’s inside shoulder to the quarterback’s setup point. A standard shotgun drop might place the QB’s back foot at ~6–8 yards; a 9.5-yard deep drop pushes this landmark meaningfully deeper and wider. An OT executing a 45-degree (or “45-degree vertical”) set calibrated for shallower depth will have set his angle and depth assuming an earlier intersection. Even if he wins the initial hand-fight/punch and controls the rusher’s breastplate momentarily, the extra QB depth alters the pocket geometry.[[4]](https://gridironstrategies.com/article/drop-back-pass-protection-centers-around-5-basic-keys)

By approximately t = 2.3 seconds (typical edge speed-to-power rush development window on a 5+ step drop), the edge rusher’s arc bend can flatten or tighten to the now-exposed outside corner. The OT’s initial set, which cut off the expected path, leaves an outside “bubble” or corner that the rusher exploits without needing to disengage early. Deeper drops correlate with sharply higher pressure rates (often double that of quick-game drops), confirming the geometric vulnerability.[[5]](https://www.sharpfootballanalysis.com/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/12634)

This is a true cross-unit interaction: the QB’s drop decision directly degrades the OT’s set angle and landmark, independent of the OT winning the first contact. Offensive lines must coordinate drop depth with protection calls (e.g., deeper sets or max protection on 7-step concepts), while edge rushers are coached to read QB depth and bend accordingly.

### 2. Pre-Snap Jet/Orbit Motion × Linebacker Run-Fit Lag
Pre-snap motion appears on a record ~64% of NFL plays and continues rising because it creates momentum, deception, and leverage. High-velocity jet motion (receiver or back sprinting flat across the formation) or orbit motion (back looping behind the QB) forces defenders to declare intentions. Linebackers must often “bump” a half-gap (shift alignment or responsibility laterally to maintain gap integrity against potential sweep, pitch, or power) or risk being outflanked. Safeties may spin down from two-high or deep alignments to fill the vacated spot.[[2]](https://www.pff.com/news/nfl-pre-snap-motion-usage-reaches-new-highs-across-the-nfl)[[6]](https://www.viqtorysports.com/pre-snap-movement-in-the-offense/)

This adjustment is not instantaneous. It creates a ~0.35-second horizontal leverage “vacuum” or lag in the run-fit structure—the LB is momentarily caught between fits, neither fully filling his original gap nor the new one, while eyes may be pulled by the motion man. The offense exploits this with **counter-direction inside zone** (running the opposite way from the motion, attacking the gap the LB vacated or hesitated in) or **play-action crossers** (routes that flow back against the motion, hitting voids created by displaced LBs or rotated safeties).[[7]](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=VGjDEOd2n30)

Motion snaps produce higher EPA/play, better completion percentages, and improved run outcomes, especially when the ball goes opposite the motion or uses the motion as a decoy for play-action. This is second- and third-order: the pre-snap look manipulates linebacker post-snap run fits and safety rotation, opening brief windows that static alignments rarely create. Defenses must choose between matching motion (risking lag) or staying disciplined (risking numbers disadvantages). Offenses that pair this with unbalanced looks or orbit from wildcat-style sets amplify the confusion.[[8]](https://www.newedgetimes.com/how-one-motion-play-swept-through-the-nfl-in-2023-everybody-is-copying-it/)

### 3. Script-Elasticity & 3rd-and-Medium (3rd & 4-6) Defensive Coordinator Call Tensors
“Script-elasticity” refers to how offenses use the opening scripted plays (classically the first 15) to probe and map defensive responses across personnel, formations, motion, and coverages, then exploit the tendencies revealed. Defenses adjust elastically based on game context, but certain down-and-distance situations produce nonlinear “tensors”—sharp, non-proportional spikes in aggressiveness.[[9]](https://sumersports.com/the-zone/whats-on-the-script-looking-at-how-offenses-approach-the-first-15-plays-of-the-game/)[[10]](https://www.theringer.com/2025/02/06/nfl/andy-reid-game-planner-opening-script-kansas-city-chiefs-super-bowl)

On **3rd & 4-6**, blitz and man-coverage rates spike compared to 3rd & 1-3 (where defenses often play heavier run-oriented fronts or zone to stop the short run) or 3rd & 8+ (deeper drops invite more conservative zone or prevent shells to limit explosives). Medium distances represent an optimal “get-off-the-field” window: long enough that offenses are likely passing, short enough that a negative play or sack ends the drive without conceding a first-down cushion. DCs respond with pressure (linebackers or DBs sent) and man coverage to shrink windows, force contested throws, and generate negative EPA. Leaguewide blitz rates have risen in certain packages, and specific DCs show elevated aggression in these “must-convert-but-not-too-long” spots.[[11]](https://www.pff.com/news/fantasy-football-emerging-blitz-trends-and-scheme-rates)[[12]](https://www.dallascowboys.com/news/science-lab-dear-cowboys-blitz-the-qb)

Offenses exploit these exact tendencies with **slot/RB/TE matchups** that punish man coverage and blitzes: versatile tight ends or receiving backs that create favorable one-on-one vs. linebackers (chips, leaks, or routes that force LBs to cover in space); slot receivers with quick releases, separators, or rub/pick concepts against man; or 11/12 personnel groupings that keep a TE or RB in to chip the blitz while releasing into hot routes or crossers. Bunch sets, orbit/jet motion paired with play-action, or TEs motioned to create mismatches further stress the rotated or blitzing defenders. These personnel choices turn the DC’s aggressiveness against them by creating exploitable individual matchups precisely when man and pressure peak.[[13]](https://outsidezone.io/insights/how-nfl-offenses-beat-coverages/)

These three mechanics illustrate **third-order football**: not just “block your man” or “fit your gap,” but how QB depth warps OL geometry against DL paths, how pre-snap motion creates post-snap LB lag for counter runs or crossers, and how scripted probing reveals DC situational spikes that specific offensive groupings can target. Modern NFL offenses increasingly weaponize these inter-unit dependencies through motion, precise drop timing, and personnel-driven mismatch hunting. Data from tracking, PFF, and film confirm the rising usage and efficiency of these concepts.
