# [0211] Cross-individual generalizability of machine learning models for ball speed prediction in baseball pitching (arXiv:2605.05487v1)

**Citation:** Ryota Takamido, Chiharu Suzuki, Hiroki Nakamoto (2026). *Cross-individual generalizability of machine learning models for ball speed prediction in baseball pitching*. arXiv:2605.05487v1. URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2605.05487
**Ledger completed:** 2026-09-21. **Read:** full text (PDF text extract, 689 lines incl. tables/references).
**Verdict:** ADAPT — the LOSOCV validation discipline (within- vs. cross-individual performance gap) and the signed-residual-as-ability methodology port directly to GSE's NFL player-prop model validation; the baseball biomechanics do not transfer.

## 1. Research question
Do machine-learning models for performance-outcome prediction generalize across individuals, and what factors (expertise level; which body segments and movement phases) drive or break cross-individual generalizability? The task is ball-speed prediction in baseball pitching. The paper's framing point: most sports-ML studies leak by mixing the same athletes into train and test, or never describe the partitioning, so reported accuracy overestimates real-world applicability to unseen individuals.

## 2. Dataset / schema
Baseball pitching motions + ball velocity from 50 pitchers (same dataset as the authors' prior study Takamido et al. 2025): 4 high-school players, 10 collegiate athletes, 20 industrial (corporate) league players, 3 independent league players, 13 professional (NPB) league players. Each athlete threw more than five fastballs under simulated game conditions; the top five fastballs per athlete by velocity were selected (250 pitches total). Motion: 16 optical motion-capture cameras (Raptor-E, Motion Analysis Corporation) at 200–500 Hz; 15 anatomical landmarks extracted (head/parietalis, bilateral acromion/shoulder, lateral humeral epicondyle/elbow, radial styloid/wrist, greater trochanter/hip, lateral femoral condyle/knee, heel and toe); left-handers mirrored to right-hand orientation. Ball velocity = initial speed, recorded by TrackMan Baseball. Preprocessing: 4th-order Butterworth filter (cutoff per Schreven et al. 2015); ball release = moment of maximum throwing-arm wrist velocity; segment from 1.0 s before to 0.2 s after release (weight-shift, release, follow-through); 3D positions temporally normalized to 101 frames → input matrix 15 × 101 × 3 (joints × time frames × xyz) per pitch. Missing TS fill analog: TS feature filled with median from similar players; counting stats forward/backward filled (carried from the prior dataset). Ethical: opt-out consent framework; approved by the Institutional Ethics Committee of the National Institute of Fitness and Sports, Kanoya (approval 25-1-26); Declaration of Helsinki. Access: anonymized dataset public per the paper ("The source code and anonymized dataset used in this study are publicly available at GitHub (https://github.com/takamido/ Ball_speed_pred_cross_ind)" — URL printed with a space in the paper; likely https://github.com/takamido/Ball_speed_pred_cross_ind).

## 3. Method / model
- **Baseline selection:** candidate architectures: Graph Neural Network–Recurrent Unit (GNN-GRU, from Yang et al. 2025) and Transformer (Vaswani et al. 2017). Six hyperparameter configs each (Table 1): GNN-GRU = (GNN layers, hidden units) ∈ {(2,32),(2,64),(2,128),(3,32),(3,64),(3,128)}; Transformer = (attention heads, d_model, d_ff) ∈ {(2,32,64),(2,64,128),(4,64,128),(4,128,256),(8,128,256),(8,256,512)} (three stacked transformer encoders). Architecture details omitted ("this study focuses on the application of these models"); refer to Vaswani et al. 2017 and Yang et al. 2025.
- **Training/evaluation:** leave-one-subject-out cross-validation (LOSOCV): all five pitches of one pitcher as test, remaining 49 pitchers for training; repeated for all 50 pitchers. Adam, lr 0.001, weight decay 1e-4, MSE loss, up to 50 epochs; early stopping when **training** R² exceeded 0.90 ("all training runs were terminated early according to this criterion"); Google Colab / NVIDIA T4. Per-pitcher mean predicted value → R² vs. actual per-pitcher mean.
- **Within-individual comparison:** one of the five pitches per pitcher randomly assigned to test → 200 train / 50 test from the *same* pitchers, same training procedure.
- **Analysis 1 (expertise):** pitchers split into Intermediate (high school + collegiate, n=14) vs. Expert (corporate + independent + NPB, n=36) by choosing among all 15 combinations of the 5 competition levels the split maximizing η (Eq 1). Independent-samples t-tests (α=5%, Cohen's d) on absolute and signed (predicted − true) errors.
- **Analysis 2 (spatiotemporal ablation):** five body regions (throwing arm, leading arm, trunk, pivot leg, leading leg; shoulder/hip overlap permitted) × ten cumulative time windows growing in 10-frame increments from movement start (cumulative design, not sliding window, "to ensure that information from the ongoing kinematic chain was always retained"). 50 region×window datasets × 50 pitchers × 10 repeats = 25,000 model trainings; mean ± SD reported.

## 4. Equations & assumptions
- Grouping criterion (Eq 1): η = d_GS / (σ_I + σ_E), where d_GS = difference between the mean ball velocities of the two groups, σ_I and σ_E = within-group SDs of the Intermediate and Expert groups. The split maximizing η was selected.
- No other equations are stated in the paper (model architectures not formalized; metrics referenced but not written out).
- **Assumptions:** ball velocity is the performance index of interest; top-5 fastballs per pitcher represent stable performance; leave-one-pitcher-out evaluates true generalization; the expertise grouping maximizing η is the right partition; early stopping on *training* R² > 0.90 prevents overfitting (unusual — stopping on train rather than validation R²); 50 pitchers suffice to estimate cross-individual effects.

## 5. Features / target
Features: time series of 3D positions of 15 anatomical landmarks (15 × 101 × 3 = 4,545 inputs per pitch) over the −1.0 s to +0.2 s window around ball release; region ablations restrict to 5 body regions and cumulative time windows. Target: ball velocity (mph, TrackMan initial speed). Horizon: N/A (single-pitch outcome, not a forecast horizon).

## 6. Validation design
LOSOCV over 50 pitchers (test = 1 pitcher's 5 pitches; train = other 49 pitchers) — the paper's core methodological point: no individual appears in both train and test. Within-individual comparison: random 1-of-5 pitches per pitcher to test (same pitchers in train and test). Baselines: GNN-GRU vs. Transformer across 6 hyperparameter configs each (no non-ML baseline, no linear-model control). Metrics: R² of per-pitcher mean predicted vs. actual velocity; absolute and signed prediction errors with t-tests and Cohen's d; ablation performance reported as mean ± SD over 10 repeats.

## 7. Numerical results / baselines
Table 2 (cross-individual R² by config), quoted exactly:
- GNN-GRU: (2,32): 0.14; (2,64): 0.20; (2,128): 0.28; (3,32): 0.21; (3,64): 0.25; (3,128): 0.28.
- Transformer: (8,256,512): 0.17; (8,128,256): 0.18; (4,128,256): 0.31; **(4,64,128): 0.38 (selected baseline)**; (2,64,128): 0.21; (2,32,64): 0.27.
- Headline result: cross-individual R² = 0.38 vs. within-individual R² = 0.91 ("substantial decline in predictive performance... R² decreasing from 0.91 to 0.38"). Within-individual value is "comparable to the value reported in the previous study by Yang et al. (2025)."
Analysis 1: absolute error — no group difference (t(48) = 0.12, p = .89, d = 0.04). Signed error (predicted − true): significant group difference (t(48) = 2.13, p = .03, d = 0.67) — Intermediate pitchers overestimated, Experts showed no clear directional bias. Group means: Intermediate 77.60 ± 4.41 mph (n=14); Expert 84.54 ± 4.39 mph (n=36).
Analysis 2: pivot leg and trunk showed relatively high generalizability; leading arm lowest. Pivot leg maintained R² > 0.25 even during the weight-shift initiation phase (first peak, ~20% time point). Temporal pattern: trunk, leading leg, leading arm generalizability increased toward ball release; pivot leg and throwing arm showed U-shaped patterns.

## 9. Leakage & limitations
- Early stopping on **training** R² > 0.90 is methodologically odd — stopping on train fit rather than a validation metric is not standard leakage-proofing; combined with only 50 pitchers, the within-individual R² = 0.91 may itself be inflated (the paper's headline contrast could partly reflect test-contamination-free LOSOCV vs. a weak within-individual protocol rather than a pure generalizability gap).
- n = 50 pitchers (250 pitches) is small for deep models; authors acknowledge this. The Intermediate group is only n = 14, so the signed-error finding (p = .03, d = 0.67) rests on a small sample.
- No non-ML baseline (e.g., linear regression on a few kinematic features) — can't tell whether the generalizability problem is model-specific or data-inherent.
- The two explanations for Intermediate overestimation (movement-efficiency differences vs. regression-to-the-center bias, citing Ribeiro & Moniz 2020) are not disentangled; authors suggest comparing within the same ball-speed range if larger samples were available.
- Biomechanics-specific; the pivot-leg/trunk findings don't transfer to NFL. Transfer value is purely methodological (validation discipline + residual analysis).
- Ablation: cumulative windows + region ablations with overlap at shoulder/hip joints; explainability via XAI deferred to future work (Minh et al. 2022).

## 10. GSE overlap
The biomechanical content is irrelevant to GSE, but the **methodological lesson is directly on-point for GSE's prop models**: GSE trains player-level prediction models (props/DFS projections) on data where the same players appear across train and test windows — the exact leakage pattern this paper warns about. The existing-research map covers calibration, state-space, and market microstructure but has **no validation-discipline work on leave-one-player-out (LOPO) evaluation** of player-level models. Additionally, the paper's second-order idea — using signed prediction residuals (predicted − actual) as a measure of player-specific skill/efficiency, citing Teranishi et al. 2023 — is a portable ability-estimation method GSE does not currently use (repo ability work is via EPA/CPOE residuals, not cross-individual ML residuals). **Extension** (new validation discipline + residual-as-ability method), not duplicate.

## 11. GSE implementation spec
1. **LOPO validation protocol for prop models:** any GSE player-level model (receiving yards, rush yards, TD props) gets evaluated leave-one-player-out: train on all players except one, test on the held-out player's full season, rotate. Report LOPO R²/MAE alongside the current pooled-CV numbers — expect a Takamido-style drop; that drop is the honest generalization estimate for new players.
2. **Signed-residual ability metric:** after fitting the prop model, compute per-player mean signed residual (actual − predicted); players systematically outperforming their kinematic/statistical inputs get a positive "efficiency" score — the analogue of the paper's expert-efficiency finding. Use it as a prior in the following week's projections (shrinkage toward zero for small samples).
3. **Segment-ablation analogue:** the paper's region×window ablation maps to feature-group ablation for NFL: which feature groups (route-level, matchup-level, team-context) generalize across players vs. memorize individuals.
4. Data: nflverse (in hand); effort ~1–2 days to bolt LOPO onto the existing prop/DFS pipeline.

## 12. Reproducible test
Take GSE's existing receiving-yards prop model (or the Week 2 DK projection pipeline from `2026-09-19-dk-week2/`): evaluate pooled-CV vs. leave-one-player-out CV on 2023–2024 nflverse regular-season data. Metric: MAE on held-out player-weeks; report the pooled→LOPO degradation. Baseline: the current validation scheme's MAE. Success = LOPO implemented and the degradation quantified; the test is diagnostic, so the gate is about the *method's* stability, not beating a baseline.

## 13. Acceptance / rejection gate
**Adopt** LOPO as a mandatory validation gate for all GSE player-level models if the pooled→LOPO MAE degradation is material (≥ 10% relative) on the 2023–2024 test — that confirms the paper's warning applies to GSE's data. **Adopt** the signed-residual ability metric into weekly projections if players' mean residuals are stable week-to-week (Spearman ≥ 0.5 across consecutive 4-week windows); if residuals are pure noise, **reject** the ability interpretation and keep the LOPO gate only. The baseball model itself is rejected for implementation.

## 14. Improvement experiment
Go beyond the paper's two explanations (efficiency vs. center-bias) with a **disentangled residual decomposition**: fit the prop model, then regress the signed residuals on (a) player identity (efficiency hypothesis) and (b) predicted-value distance from the pooled mean (center-bias hypothesis) in a mixed-effects model. The variance-components ratio tells GSE how much of its prop-model error is fixable skill signal vs. irreducible shrinkage bias — the paper proposes this comparison ("compare players within the same ball-speed range") but never runs it.
