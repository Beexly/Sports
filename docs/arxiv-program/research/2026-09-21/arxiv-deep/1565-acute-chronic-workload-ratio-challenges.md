# [1565] The acute:chronic workload ratio: challenges and prospects for improvement (arXiv:1907.05326)

**Citation:** Chinchin Wang, Jorge Trejo Vargas, Tyrel Stokes, Russell Steele, Ian Shrier (2019). *The acute:chronic workload ratio: challenges and prospects for improvement*. arXiv:1907.05326. URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/1907.05326
**Ledger completed:** 2026-09-21. **Read:** full text (PDF via pdftotext).
**Verdict:** ADAPT

this paper is the field's canonical critique of the ACWR workload-injury paradigm (coupled-ratio ceiling of 4, EWMA initial-value bug, discretization bias, time-matching bias, IV/g-method recommendations); GSE should adopt its *uncoupled + continuous + time-matched* design rules when building NFL workload/injury-risk features, not the IOC's naive ratio.

## 1. Research question
Is the acute:chronic workload ratio (ACRatio), as endorsed by the IOC consensus statement for monitoring injury risk, a valid and well-constructed metric — and what are its mathematical, statistical, and causal limitations? The paper is a methodological critique plus repair manual, not an empirical study.

## 2. Dataset / schema
No new dataset. The paper re-examines published studies behind the IOC model: 3 foundational studies with n=28, n=53, and an unreported n (Hulin et al. 2014 cricket fast bowlers; Carey et al. 2017 Australian football; Hulin et al. 2016 rugby league); a cohort of 59 Australian football players (Murray et al. 2017) used for the EWMA comparison; Nielsen et al.'s review of 35 workload-injury studies (only 11 with n>150). Workload quantified as distance, weight lifted, or session-RPE. Illustrative examples use Menaspà's 3-athlete training profiles. Proprietary/clinical data not involved; all references are published sports-medicine literature.

## 3. Method / model
Analytical critique with mathematical demonstrations and worked numerical examples. Eight limitations diagnosed, each with a prescribed fix: (1) coupled vs uncoupled ratio — the conventional ACRatio includes acute load in the chronic denominator (mathematical coupling); the uncoupled version (acute excluded from chronic) removes the spurious acute–chronic correlation and the theoretical ceiling of 4; (2) unweighted 4-week averages obscure training distribution (Menaspà's 3 athletes, same ratio, different risk) — EWMA weighting proposed by Williams et al. but shown to have an "initial value" bug: with decay constant <0.5, the time-0 (most distant) load gets more weight than subsequent days (with Williams' constants 0.25 acute / 0.068 chronic over 28 days, initial load contributes 1.9× the day-28 load to the chronic weighted average); convergence requires ~50 burn-in days; (3) tapering breaks the ratio (marathon/swim/cycle taper inflates ACRatio at competition while reducing injury risk); (4) discretization before modeling loses information, inflates false discovery rates (citing simulations [27]), and invites sparse-data bias (rule: ≥5 events per level×covariate combination); model continuous, discretize after; (5) the IOC "sweet spot 0.8–1.3 / danger zone >1.5" model rests on sparse discretized data — the apparent risk increase is driven entirely by points at ACRatio=2.0; below 2.0 there is no apparent relationship; (6) low-ACRatio "risk" is an artifact: athletes injured early in a weekly block accumulate less acute load (time-mismatch bias) — worked Tuesday-injury example; fixes: nested case-control matched on exposure time with censoring at matched injury time, case-crossover design, or planned-schedule proxies; (7) unmeasured confounding — recommends instrumental variables (proposed training schedule as instrument for actual activity, estimable by 2SLS) and g-methods for time-varying confounding (aches/pains both caused by load and cause future load changes); (8) subsequent (recurrent) injuries violate independence, deflate the chronic denominator after time-loss, and change load capacity — need recurrent-event models plus g-methods.

## 4. Equations & assumptions
- Coupled ACRatio ceiling proof: if inactive 3 weeks then workload WL1 in week 4, ACRatio = WL1/(WL1/4) = 4 regardless of WL1 magnitude — the metric cannot discriminate spike sizes at the top end.
- EWMA weight anomaly: with decay constant λ<0.5, weight at time 0 exceeds weights at subsequent days (Appendix 2 of paper); with λ_chronic=0.068 over 28 days, initial-load contribution = 1.9 × day-28 contribution (contradicting "recent matters most").
- Sparse-data-bias rule: ≥5 events per level of each covariate combination (Bennette & Vickers); continuous + spline fitting avoids it.
- IV conditions stated: instrument causes exposure, affects outcome only through exposure, and is unconfounded with the outcome given measured covariates; estimator: 2-stage least squares.
- g-methods referenced (Robins) for time-varying confounding where exposure affects a confounder of its own future effect.
- No new estimator derived; the mathematics are diagnostic proofs and worked examples.

## 5. Features / target
Features: acute workload (recent 7-day load), chronic workload (prior 28-day / 4-week load), training schedule (planned vs actual), covariates (sex, age in examples). Target: injury occurrence (binary), subsequent/recurrent injury. Horizon: week or day ahead. Workload units: distance, sRPE, weight lifted — unit-agnostic. This is a methods paper; no fitted model or label definition of its own.

## 6. Validation design
No empirical validation of its own. Evidence is by citation and demonstration: (a) mathematical proofs (ceiling of 4, EWMA weight inversion); (b) the Tuesday-injury worked example showing mechanical low-ratio bias; (c) cited simulation [27] showing discretization increases false discovery rate vs continuous models; (d) cited empirical result: EWMA-ACWR R² rose from 0.21 (unweighted) to 0.87 (weighted) in 59 Australian footballers [19] — presented as motivation for EWMA while simultaneously flagging its bugs; (e) convergence demonstration: two initial-value scenarios converge after ~50 repeated training days. No train/test splits (not applicable). Baselines: the IOC consensus model itself (Figure 1) is the baseline being critiqued.

## 7. Numerical results / baselines
Exact numbers quoted: coupled ACRatio maximum = 4.0 by construction (worked proof). EWMA constants from Williams et al.: 0.25 (acute), 0.068 (chronic); initial-load weight = 1.9× day-28 weight in the chronic calculation. Convergence of initial-value variants after ~50 days of repeated training profile. R² 0.21 → 0.87 (unweighted vs EWMA, n=59 Australian footballers, cited from Murray et al. [19]). IOC model: sweet spot 0.8–1.3, danger zone >1.5, based on n=28, n=53, and one unreported-n study; risk increase driven solely by ACRatio=2.0 endpoint bin; no relationship visible below 2.0. Sparse-data rule: 5 events per covariate-level combination (e.g., 3 bins → ≥15 injuries univariately). Of 35 reviewed workload-injury studies, only 11 had n>150 (Nielsen et al.).

## 8. Code / data availability
None stated. No code repository; no new data. Appendix 1 (uncoupled calculation worked example) and Appendices 2-3 (EWMA weight derivations) are in the paper itself. All empirical claims cite published sports-medicine studies.

## 9. Leakage & limitations
The paper is a critique, not an empirical study — it proposes fixes (IV with planned schedule, g-methods, nested case-control) without demonstrating them on data, so the fixes' practical performance is unvalidated here. The Tuesday-injury bias example assumes weekly calendar blocks; daily monitoring (which NFL teams actually have via GPS) attenuates but doesn't eliminate the mismatch. The IV proposal (planned schedule as instrument) is fragile: coaches adjust plans based on observed soreness, violating the exclusion-adjacent assumption unless reasons for schedule changes are recorded — the authors acknowledge this and recommend recording deviation reasons. Recommending "50 days burn-in" for EWMA discards early-season data precisely when NFL injury risk is highest (preseason→week 1 ramp). The uncoupled ratio has no upper bound, which fixes the ceiling but makes extreme values noisier. External validity to NFL: all cited data are cricket/AFL/rugby/soccer; American-football workload dynamics (collision load vs running load) differ; the paper notes tapering sports break the model but doesn't solve collision sports. No discussion of positional heterogeneity.

## 10. GSE overlap
Directly on GSE's injury/workload gap: the existing-research map lists "causal injury impact" as gap #9 and the 15-area ML brief has causal inference pending — no existing GSE workload-ratio work found in the map. This paper doesn't duplicate anything; it's a design-constraints document for any workload feature GSE builds. Its warnings map exactly onto pitfalls GSE would hit: naive ACWR on NFL snap counts would inherit the coupling ceiling, the weekly-block time-mismatch bias (injured Thursday players show low "acute" snap counts), and the discretization temptation (binning players into "red zone" workload tiers before modeling). Complements paper 1563 (ACTE rest effects): 1563 estimates causal rest effects on performance; this paper constrains how workload features must be constructed for injury risk.

## 11. GSE implementation spec
Build `gse-workload-risk` following the paper's rules, never the IOC's: (1) Workload signals from nflverse: snaps, routes run, high-speed efforts (proxy via NGS when available), plus practice participation (DNP/LP/FP). Use UNCOUPLED ratios only: acute = trailing 7-day load, chronic = prior 28-day load excluding the acute window. (2) Continuous modeling: fit injury probability as a smooth function (GAM/spline) of the continuous uncoupled ratio; discretize into tiers only after fitting, for display. (3) Time-matching: injury labels use nested case-control — each injured player-week matched to non-injured player-weeks censored at the same calendar time (same week, same position group); never compare full-week loads of completers vs partial-week loads of early-week injured. (4) Recurrent injuries: separate model with prior-injury count, days since return, and rehab-completion proxy as covariates; chronic denominator adjusted for time-loss. (5) Confounding: collect planned vs actual practice loads; use planned load as IV for actual load (2SLS) where schedule deviations are recorded; use g-methods (marginal structural models) for the soreness→load→injury feedback loop. (6) Position-specific curves (OL collision load vs WR running load). Effort: 1 week for the feature pipeline + nested case-control label builder; 2 weeks with the recurrent-injury and IV extensions.

## 12. Reproducible test
Dataset: nflverse 2019-2024, weekly player data, injury = DNP-with-injury-designation or IR placement within the following 7 days. Features: uncoupled 7:28-day snap-count ratio (continuous), position group, age. Baseline to beat: the naive coupled weekly-blocked ACWR binned into the IOC tiers (0.8-1.3/1.5+) — the paper predicts the naive version will show the artifactual low-ratio risk bump and no signal below ratio 2.0. Metric: out-of-sample AUC/PR-AUC for next-week injury, leave-one-season-out; plus a calibration plot of predicted vs observed injury rate across the continuous ratio. Window: train 2019-2022, test 2023-2024. Success: continuous uncoupled model beats naive binned coupled model by ≥0.03 AUC and shows monotone risk above the fitted threshold without the low-end artifact.

## 13. Acceptance / rejection gate
ADOPT the workload-risk feature into GSE's injury overlay only if: (a) leave-one-season-out AUC ≥ 0.60 for next-week injury among skill-position players (a low but real bar for rare events); (b) the low-ACWR risk artifact is absent (monotone or flat risk below ratio 1.0 — verify the time-matching fix worked); (c) the risk curve's inflection point is identified from the continuous fit with bootstrap 90% CI width < 0.5 ratio units. Otherwise reject — workload stays a descriptive dashboard input, not a prediction adjustment. Never ship the IOC 0.8-1.3/1.5 bins; if the test fails, the finding is "ACWR has no measurable NFL signal," which is itself valuable.

## 14. Improvement experiment
Go beyond ratios to a structural model: fit a latent "load capacity" state-space model per player where capacity evolves with chronic load and decays with time-loss, and injury probability = P(acute load > capacity). This directly implements the paper's biological premise ("injury occurs when load exceeds tissue capacity") that the ratio only proxies. Compare the state-space model's PR-AUC against the best ratio model; if capacity modeling wins, GSE gets individualized workload ceilings (e.g., "Player X's capacity is 62 snaps this week") instead of population ratio tiers — the natural endpoint of the paper's critique.
