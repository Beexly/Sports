# [1702] Evaluating Weather Forecasts from a Decision Maker's Perspective (arXiv:2512.14779)

**Citation:** Raeth, K. & Ludwig, N. (2025). *Evaluating Weather Forecasts from a Decision Maker's Perspective*. arXiv:2512.14779. URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.14779
**Ledger completed:** 2026-09-22. **Read:** full text (ar5iv HTML, Sections 1–5 + Appendices A–C + references, ~39k chars).
**Verdict**: ADAPT
ADAPT — one sentence: the decision-calibration framework (cost gap between forecast-anticipated and observed decision costs) is directly adaptable to how GSE selects and evaluates weather inputs — judge a weather feed by the betting/fantasy decision costs it produces, not by CRPS — but the paper's cost functions are agricultural/energy tasks that must be rebuilt as staking and lineup cost functions.

## 1. Research question
Does forecast-level skill (CRPS, PIT histograms, spread-skill ratios) reliably identify the best weather model for a downstream decision task? The authors apply the decision-calibration framework to compare ECMWF's IFS ENS (classical NWP) against ArchesweatherGen (diffusion-based ML weather model) on three weather-dependent decision tasks — frost crop protection, civil heat protection, wind-power dispatch — and find model rankings change between forecast-level and decision-level evaluation and across tasks.

## 2. Dataset / schema
- 50-member ensemble forecasts from IFS ENS and ArchesweatherGen for all of 2021; variables: 2 m temperature, 10 m wind speed; lead times 1–15 days; 1.5° horizontal resolution over Europe.
- Ground truth: IFS HRES analysis (for IFS ENS) and ERA5 (for Arches); Arches forecasts generated by running the public trained model; remainder from the WeatherBench dataset.
- Decision-task grids: European mid-latitudes (frost, 00 UTC), Europe excluding Scandinavia (heat, 12 UTC), EMODnet offshore wind-farm grid points (wind power, 00 UTC).

## 3. Method / model
- Decision-calibration framework (Zhao et al.): Bayes decision rule δ_c(F_x) = argmin_{a∈A} E_{Y~F_x}[c(a,Y)] (Eq. 1); expected cost under forecast C_exp = E_X E_{Ŷ~F_X}[c(δ_c(F_X),Ŷ)] (Eq. 2, Monte-Carlo over ensemble members per Eq. 6); observed cost C_obs = E_{X,Y}[c(δ_c(F_X),Y)] (Eq. 3); cost gap C_gap = |C_exp − C_obs| (Eq. 4), computed per observation then averaged over space/time.
- Three hand-built cost functions: (a) frost protection — binary protect/not, threshold θ (plant death below θ, safe above θ+3, linear interpolation between), asymmetric cost ratio c∈(0,1) weighting missed protection (c·10) vs unnecessary protection ((1−c)·10); (b) heat protection — same structure with θ as max-cost temperature; (c) wind dispatch — 11 actions (promised power in 0.1 bins + turbine-off standby), linear under-delivery penalty (slope u-pen), curtailment for surplus, wind→power via power law (α=0.1, hub 120 m) and standard power curve (cut-in 3, rated 13, cut-off 23 m/s).
- Compared against standard diagnostics: CRPS, PIT histograms, spread-skill ratio (Appendix B).

## 4. Equations & assumptions
- δ_c(F_x) ≔ argmin_a E_{Y~F_x}[c(a,Y)] (Eq. 1); C_exp (Eq. 2); C_obs (Eq. 3); C_gap = |C_exp − C_obs| (Eq. 4); Monte-Carlo E[c(a,Ŷ)] ≈ (1/M)Σ_i c(a,Ŷ^(i)) (Eq. 6).
- Key structural insight: cost functions encode *where* calibration matters — only where c(a_0,·) and c(a_1,·) cross does miscalibration change decisions; elsewhere calibration is irrelevant.
- Assumptions: decision-maker follows the Bayes rule exactly; cost functions are stylized but realistic; per-observation cost gaps (not pre-averaged) to avoid masking (per Zhao et al.); ensemble members treated as i.i.d. draws from F_x.

## 5. Features / target
Features: ensemble forecast distributions (50 members) of 2 m temperature / 10 m wind at grid points and lead times. Targets: (a) per-observation cost gap C_gap and observed cost C_obs per decision task; (b) standard calibration diagnostics for comparison.

## 6. Validation design
No train/test in the ML sense — both forecast models are fixed; evaluation is a model-comparison study over the 2021 forecast archive. For each task × (θ, c / u-pen) configuration × lead time, the authors compute C_gap and C_obs for both models and report relative improvements (Arches over IFS), plus per-grid-point maps. Standard metrics (CRPS/PIT/SSR) computed on the same forecasts for the forecast-level comparison.

## 7. Numerical results / baselines
- Frost: CRPS/PIT/SSR show similar performance (Arches slightly worse at 7/10-day leads; stronger underdispersion). Decision level: Arches has better C_gap for low θ, but the ranking weakens/reverses as θ rises; same pattern across cost ratio c. Task details invisible to global metrics change conclusions.
- Heat: standard metrics similar; decision level: Arches improves over IFS at high θ (tail events); per-grid-point maps show Arches better in the Mediterranean than central Europe — geographic ranking variation lost in spatial aggregation.
- Wind power: small C_gap differences; at the decision-relevant 1-day lead, Arches is slightly *worse* on CRPS/SSR but *better* on decision calibration under stronger penalties and yields lower observed costs — "from an economic perspective, Arches is the better model."
- Structural finding: when cost functions punish errors uniformly across the output space (wind task), decision-calibration rankings deviate less from CRPS rankings; binary threshold tasks (frost/heat) deviate most.

## 8. Code / data availability
WeatherBench data public; ArchesweatherGen model public (forecasts regenerated by authors). No analysis code repo stated.

## 9. Leakage & limitations
- Cost functions are author-constructed, not elicited from real decision-makers — the "economic perspective" claim rests on stylized costs.
- Single year (2021), Europe only; model ranking reversals could be year-specific.
- Per-observation cost-gap averaging is an author choice that amplifies tail sensitivity; no uncertainty quantification on the relative-improvement figures.
- One limitation the authors state: decision calibration requires well-defined cost functions, which "may not be readily available in practice."
- IFS ENS evaluated against IFS analysis (its own product) vs Arches against ERA5 — asymmetric ground truth.

## 10. GSE overlap
New capability, not duplicate. GSE's calibration lane works at the *probability* level (ECE, CQR, PIT-style checks); this paper operates one level up — at the *decision* level — and proves the two can disagree: a weather feed can win on CRPS and lose on the decision that matters. Nothing in the corpus evaluates weather inputs by downstream betting cost. Directly relevant to GSE's weather lane: which weather API / which forecast fields to buy and trust should be decided by C_gap on GSE's own decision tasks (stake/no-stake, over/under lean), not by the vendor's advertised skill scores. Also complements ledger 1708 (decision-oriented forecast benchmarking, same theme from a different angle).

## 11. GSE implementation spec
- Module `weather/decision_calibration.py`: implement Eqs. 1–4, 6 generically. Define GSE cost functions: (a) binary stake/no-stake on a spread pick — cost of staking a loser vs skipping a winner, parameterized by edge threshold and Kelly fraction (the direct analogue of the frost task's (θ, c)); (b) totals over/under lean with asymmetric over/under payoff; (c) DFS lineup swap under late weather news (multi-action analogue of wind dispatch).
- For each candidate weather input (vendor A vs B, raw vs postprocessed ensemble), compute per-game C_gap and C_obs over 2+ seasons of games where weather was a plausible factor (outdoor, wind ≥ 10 mph or temp ≤ 35°F or precip).
- Decision rule: select the weather feed minimizing C_obs subject to small C_gap (well-anticipated costs); report per-stadium maps (the paper's geographic-variation finding → GSE's stadium-specific feed selection).
- Effort: ~3 days (framework is ~100 lines; cost-function design + backtest harness is the work).

## 12. Reproducible test
Dataset: 2022–2025 outdoor NFL games with game-time weather from two sources (e.g., a station-observation feed vs a forecast API) + GSE engine's historical spread/total picks and closing lines. Baseline: pick the weather source by CRPS on temperature/wind. Test: pick by decision calibration (C_gap/C_obs on the stake/no-stake task). Metric: realized ROI of weather-adjusted picks under each source on 2024–2025 holdout. Expectation per the paper: the CRPS winner and the decision winner differ on at least one task configuration.

## 13. Acceptance / rejection gate
ADOPT decision-calibrated weather-source selection if the decision-chosen feed beats the CRPS-chosen feed by ≥ 1% ROI on weather-relevant games in the 2024–2025 holdout AND the cost gap C_gap is smaller (costs are actually anticipated, not just lower). REJECT if rankings agree everywhere (then CRPS suffices and the extra machinery buys nothing) or if neither feed beats a no-weather baseline — weather may not move GSE's decisions at all.

## 14. Improvement experiment
The paper's stated future work — "optimize MLWP models for specific downstream decision tasks by utilizing cost functions as loss function during training" — ported to GSE: train (or fine-tune) the weather-to-adjustment mapping directly on the decision loss. Build a small model mapping (forecast ensemble → projection adjustment) and train it to minimize realized staking cost (Eq. 3) rather than CRPS on weather variables. Hypothesis: a decision-trained adjustment beats a CRPS-trained one specifically near the stake/no-stake boundary (where the paper shows calibration matters most — the cost-function crossing region). Test: compare both adjustments' ROI on games where the engine's edge was within ±2% of the staking threshold; success = decision-trained wins by ≥ 2% ROI there with no degradation elsewhere. This turns weather from a feature into a decision-optimized input.

**Verdict:** ADAPT — the decision-calibration framework (cost gap at the decision level, calibration-only-where-costs-cross) is the correct way for GSE to select and train weather inputs, but the agricultural/energy cost functions must be replaced with staking and lineup cost functions.
