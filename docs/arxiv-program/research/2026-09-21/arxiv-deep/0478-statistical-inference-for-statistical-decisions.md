## 1. Research question
Can inference-based SDFs — mappings of the form [data → inference → decision] — serve as practical, tractable substitutes for fully general Wald SDFs (all mappings [data → decision])? The paper motivates them two ways: (i) tractability — Wald's three nested operations (sample integration → state-space extremum → SDF extremum) are usually infeasible, while evaluating a fixed inference-based rule needs only the inner and middle operations; (ii) necessity — institutional separation between research and decision making means planners often see only reported inferences, not raw data, so they can only perform [inference → decision]. Illustrations: binary choice (tests), as-if optimization, as-if maximin/MMR with set estimates.

## 2. Dataset / schema
No new dataset. Worked examples: (i) hypothetical cancer treatment choice — status quo mean lifespan 1 year; innovation either 1/3 year or 5 years; conventional trial test with Type I error prob 0.05, Type II 0.20; (ii) Manski & Tetenov (2016) numerical comparison of the empirical success (ES) rule vs a one-sided two-sample z-test: balanced trial, N=145 per arm, binary outcome, state space [0,1]² discretized by grid; (iii) Dominitz & Manski (2017) missing-data prediction setup; (iv) Manski (2007b) missing-data treatment allocation with known observable features.

## 3. Method / model
Wald statistical decision theory (frequentist): SDF c(ψ): Ψ→C evaluated ex ante by state-dependent expected welfare (E_s{w[c(ψ),s]}, s∈S), then ranked by maximin, MMR, or subjective-average-welfare criteria. Inference-based SDFs: point-estimate as-if optimization c[s(ψ)] = argmax w[c,s(ψ)] (eq. 9); the empirical distribution as estimate of the state (Goldberger's analogy principle); the empirical success (ES) rule (pick treatment with highest sample mean); set-estimate as-if problems (1^N)–(3^N) that act as if the state space is the estimated set S(ψ) (confidence sets, estimated identification regions); the wald_mse STATA algorithm (Manski & Tabord-Meehan 2017) for numerical max regret of midpoint predictors.

## 4. Equations & assumptions
Criteria (1)–(3) without data (maximize subjective average welfare; maximin; MMR), statistical versions (4)–(6) over feasible SDFs Γ. Binary choice: regret = R_{c(ψ)s}·|w(a,s)−w(b,s)|, eqs. (7)–(8); Neyman-Pearson convention: Q_s[c(ψ)=b]≤α for s∈S_a, α=0.05 typical. As-if optimization: c[s(ψ)]∈argmax w[c,s(ψ)] (9). ES-rule max-regret bounds (Manski & Tetenov 2016, Props. 1–2), K arms, outcome range M, n/arm balanced: (10) (2e)^{−½}M(K−1)n^{−½}; (11) M(ln K)^{½}n^{−½}. MMR fractional allocation with missing data (Manski 2007b, eq. 12): z*(b)=1 if (e_a−y_{1a})p_a+(y_{0b}−e_b)p_b+(y_{1a}−y_{0b})<0; =0 if symmetric condition; else [e_bp_b+y_1(1−p_b)−e_ap_a−y_0(1−p_a)]/[(y_1−y_0)(2−p)] (eq. 13); sample analog (14)/(16) is finite-sample MMR when p is known (Prop. 2, uses E[z_N(b)]=z*(b)). Assumptions: outcomes bounded [y_{0t},y_{1t}]; individualistic treatment response; random selection may be a model rather than reality (§4.4: S+ orderings preserved, S− reversed, S= tied); confidence set S(ψ): Q_s[ψ: s∈S(ψ)]≥α ∀s∈S.

## 5. Features / target
Features: sample data (trial outcomes, possibly with missingness/misspecification). Target: a decision (treatment allocation, point prediction), reached via an inference step. GSE mapping (as in 0477): features = engine probabilities + odds; decision = bet/stake; the draft's distinctive addition is the [inference → decision] separation — GSE model builders and the betting desk are separated exactly this way (the desk sees reported edges, not training data).

## 6. Validation design
Numerical: regret computed state-by-state via Monte Carlo integration of error probabilities (5,000 draws per state implied; user-controlled), maximized over discretized state-space grids (Bernoulli parameters for binary outcomes; Beta shape parameters for continuous). Analytical: large-deviations bounds for the ES rule; closed-form MMR allocations under partial identification. No real-data validation.

## 7. Numerical results / baselines
Cancer example: conventional test (α=0.05, β=0.20) → Type I regret 1/30 yr, Type II regret 4/5 yr, max regret 4/5 yr; reversed test (α=0.20, β=0.05) → regrets 2/15 and 1/5 yr, max regret 1/5 yr — the unconventional test has 4× smaller max regret. Manski & Tetenov (2016), N=145/arm: ES rule max regret 0.01 vs one-sided z-test 0.05; ES regret peaks at effect size ≈±0.03 (error prob ≈0.35); z-test regret peaks at effect size ≈0.08 (error prob ≈0.6). ES-rule bounds: (10) tighter for K=2,3 treatments; (11) tighter for K≥4. Midpoint predictor max regret ¼[P(δ=1)²/N + P(δ=0)²]. Curious finite-sample result (Prop. 2): under its assumptions there is no MMR advantage to large sample size — one observation per treatment is as good as the full sample. All numbers are the paper's claims.

## 9. Leakage & limitations
Adversarial notes: (a) superseded draft — several results are refined/replaced in the v4 paper (e.g., the ES vs z-test comparison is re-presented with different framing in v4 §2.3.3; the treatment analysis is redone with the AMMR rule); (b) the "no advantage to large N" finding rests on the artificial setting where p is known and E[z_N]=z* — it says nothing about real trials; (c) the cancer example is a stylized two-point state space chosen to make the conventional test look bad; the 4× improvement is an artifact of the extreme 1/3-vs-5-year asymmetry; (d) maximin dismissed as "ultrapessimistic" on Savage's verbal argument plus one example, not on generality; (e) the set-estimate program (1^N)–(3^N) is only motivated asymptotically (consistency of S_N(ψ)→H(s*)) — finite-sample properties are open questions, and §5.4 on confidence sets is explicitly "a topic for future research"; (f) evaluating inference-based SDFs still requires the middle operation (max over S), which the paper admits "may be burdensome" — the tractability gain is partial.
