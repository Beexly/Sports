# [0399] The Anatomy of Corner 3s in the NBA: What makes them efficient, how are they generated and how can defenses respond? (arXiv:2105.12785v1)

**Citation:** Konstantinos Pelechrinis, Kirk Goldsberry (2021). *The Anatomy of Corner 3s in the NBA: What makes them efficient, how are they generated and how can defenses respond?* arXiv:2105.12785v1. URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.12785v1
**Ledger completed:** 2026-09-21. **Read:** full text (PDF text extract, 626 lines).
**Verdict:** ADAPT — basketball domain with no direct NFL transfer, but its tracking-data trajectory-clustering pipeline and game-theoretic defender-commitment model are directly portable to NFL NGS work (pass-rush arcs, route clusters, contain-vs-help decisions); adopt the methods, discard the basketball conclusions.

## 1. Research question
What makes NBA corner three-point shots (C3) more efficient than above-the-break threes (ATB3), how do offenses generate them, and how should defenses respond? The paper answers three questions: (i) why C3s are ~12 points/100 shots better than ATB3s — testing the media claim that it is the shorter distance to the basket; (ii) how offenses generate C3s, via shooter–defender movement choreography in the 4 seconds before the shot; and (iii) what the optimal defensive response is, derived from a simplified zero-sum game between offense and defense in drive-and-kick situations.

## 2. Dataset / schema
Two datasets. (a) Player tracking data from **750 games of the 2016–17 NBA season**, captured via six cameras mounted on arena rafters, with player and ball locations sampled at **25 Hz**. No URL given; proprietary Second Spectrum-era NBA optical tracking (not stated as public — access not described, treated as proprietary/non-public). (b) Shot data from **324 games of the 2016–17 season of FIBA's Basketball Champions League** (via the FIBA API, acknowledged in the paper). Used as a natural experiment because the FIBA three-point line geometry differs from the NBA (the C3-vs-ATB3 distance-to-basket difference is only ~28% of the NBA court's), allowing a test of whether the efficiency gap survives when the distance mechanism is largely removed.

## 3. Method / model
Three analyses. (1) **Efficiency decomposition**: a logistic regression for shot-make probability using only shooter–basket distance, comparing the predicted FG% gap to the observed gap; contest measured by distance between shooter and closest defender at shot time; assist rates compared between C3 and ATB3; FIBA natural experiment for the causal argument. (2) **Generation analysis**: trajectories of the shooter and his defender over the 4 seconds before the shot, clustered with **k-means** into **10 clusters**, number of clusters selected via the **gap statistic** (Tibshirani et al. 2001); cluster coherence quantified by the **radius of gyration** of each cluster; pass-origin analysis by court zone (Table 1 in the paper). (3) **Defensive game**: a simplified **zero-sum game** — the corner defender chooses a distance *d* ∈ {1, 2, …, 21} feet from the shooter (discretized in 1-foot bins); the offense chooses drive-to-basket or kick-out pass; the payoff matrix is built from Second Spectrum's **qSQ** (Chang et al. 2014, league-average points per shot as a function of closest-defender distance) plus a parametrized double-team model in which a second defender at distance *d* from the corner reduces the drive's expected points per shot by the factor (1 − 1/α^(22−d)) for α ∈ [1, 2]. Solved for Nash equilibrium mixed strategies at multiple α values.

## 4. Equations & assumptions
- Radius of gyration for cluster C (equation 1 in the paper): r_C = √( (1/‖C‖) · Σ_{x_i ∈ C} (x_i − x_C)² ), where x_i is a data point in cluster C and x_C is the centroid of C. Low r_C = spatially coherent cluster.
- Defensive strategy space: Σ_def = {1, 2, 3, …, 21} (feet of separation from the corner shooter).
- Double-team impact factor: (1 − 1/α^(22−d)), where *d* is the defender's distance from the corner (hence 22−d from the penetrating player) and α ∈ [1, 2] parametrizes double-team effectiveness.
- Pass-success adjustment for the payoff matrix (example): π_{d,pass}^{new} = 0.8 · π_{d,pass}, where π_{d,pass} captures expected points from the C3 assuming all passes succeed.
- Stated assumptions: (i) the game is **zero-sum** — offense payoff equals defense loss; (ii) payoff matrix built from **league-average** shooting models (not player-specific, though the paper notes the matrix can be adjusted for shooter/driver/defender ability); (iii) the model does not account for the ability to complete the pass to the corner when the penetration is double-teamed (authors note this can be integrated via the π adjustment above); (iv) Nash equilibrium is solved on the discretized 21-strategy space; (v) the future-work hypothesis posits a **unit disk** mental model of defensive influence (radius ρ, uniform over the disk) as a possible cognitive-bias explanation for defenders lingering between corner and basket.

## 5. Features / target
- Efficiency analysis: input = shot type (C3 vs ATB3), shooter–basket distance (average 23.0 ft for C3, 25.1 ft for ATB3), distance of closest defender at shot time, assist flag. Targets = shot made (FG%), points per 100 shots (expected point value: 22-ft corner three ≈ 1.14 expected points vs 20-ft two-point jumper ≈ 0.9 in the paper's illustration).
- Generation analysis: input = (x, y) positions of shooter and his defender sampled at 25 Hz over the 4 seconds before the shot; pass origin court zone. Target = cluster membership (10 clusters, e.g., "Stationed-RC3", "Stationed-LC3").
- Game model: strategies Σ_def = {1..21} ft, Σ_off = {drive, kick-out pass}; target = expected points per possession (offense payoff).

## 6. Validation design
No train/validation/test splits (not a predictive ML paper — it is a descriptive/econometric/game-theoretic study). Validation is argument-based: (a) the distance-only logistic model is compared against the observed FG% gap to quantify how much distance can explain; (b) the **FIBA natural experiment** — if distance were the main driver, the C3–ATB3 efficiency gap should shrink on FIBA courts where the line-geometry distance difference is only 28% of the NBA's; the gap persists (16 pts/100 shots); (c) the Nash equilibrium's expected defender distance (d̂ ≈ 13 ft) is compared against the empirically observed average distance (12.3 ft) — the means match but the shapes differ (bimodal mixed strategy vs. empirical normal-like distribution), which the paper presents as evidence defenses deviate from equilibrium.

## 7. Numerical results / baselines
Key numbers quoted exactly as in the paper (these are the paper's claims, not my interpretation):
- C3 FG% = **38.8%**, ATB3 FG% = **34.7%**; observed gap = **4.1 percentage points**.
- Distance-only logistic regression predicts C3 should be only **~1.8 percentage points** higher than ATB3 — so distance explains less than half the observed gap.
- Average shot distances used in the model: C3 = **23 feet**, ATB3 = **25.1 feet**.
- **>90%** of C3 shots are assisted; ATB3s assisted at just above **70%**.
- Closest defender at time of C3 shot: average **6.5 feet** away; for ATB3 "a bit lower than 6 feet".
- NBA efficiency gap: C3s generate about **12 points per 100 shots more** than ATB3s.
- FIBA: C3s provide **16 points/100 shots more** than ATB3 (paper notes the gap is higher than the NBA's, possibly due to the much smaller FIBA sample); FIBA three-point-line geometry difference = **28%** of the NBA court's; FIBA C3 assist rate ≈ **45% higher** than FIBA ATB3 assist rate.
- ~**half** of all C3s fall in the two "Stationed" clusters (shooter anchored at the corner ≥ 4 seconds before the shot, defender lingering between corner and basket awaiting drive-and-kick).
- Table 1 pass origins: **~one third** of kick-out passes come from near the basket — Basket Area 20.6% (left corner) / 22.1% (right corner); Deep Paint 5.8% / 6.5%.
- Nash equilibrium: defender should **commit** to either the corner shooter or the drive; expected equilibrium distance d̂ ≈ **13 feet** vs. observed average **12.3 feet**, but the empirical distance distribution is approximately normal around the mean rather than bimodal.
- α tested over [1, 2]; equilibrium strategies shown for α = 1.3 and α = 1.9 — the fraction of time on each option shifts with α (i.e., with double-team effectiveness).

## 9. Leakage & limitations
- **Causality**: the authors are explicit that the assist-rate analysis "cannot be treated as causal" — assists and shot openness are jointly produced by good ball movement; the FIBA natural experiment strengthens the argument but is not randomized (sample far smaller: 324 games vs 750).
- **No defender-quality or game-state controls** in the efficiency decomposition; closeout speed and contest quality are proxied crudely by static closest-defender distance at shot time, ignoring defender trajectory and closeout angle.
- **Game-model simplifications**: league-average payoff matrix (no player-specific abilities), no pass-completion-when-doubled modeling (left as a multiplicative patch), discretized strategy space, no dynamics of the other 8 players on court.
- **Sample concerns**: 2016–17 season only — the 3-point revolution has continued since; stationed-cluster shares and assist rates may have shifted.
- **External validity to NFL**: none of the basketball conclusions transfer (shot geometry, assist mechanics are basketball-specific). Transferable elements are the *methods*: 25 Hz trajectory clustering for pattern discovery, natural-experiment validation via geometry/rule differences, and the commit-vs-linger game-theoretic framing of help defense.
- **No predictive validation**: descriptive study; no out-of-sample test, no baselines beaten.
- **Overfitting risk**: k-means with 10 clusters chosen via gap statistic on one season; cluster stability across seasons not checked; radius of gyration reported per cluster but no cross-season replication.

## 11. GSE implementation spec
Port the two transferable methods to NFL NGS tracking data:
1. **Trajectory-clustering pipeline**: replicate §3.2 on NGS 10 Hz player tracking — cluster pass-rush arcs (first 1.5 s after snap) and route stems (first 2 s of routes) with k-means + gap statistic + radius-of-gyration coherence. Discover empirical archetypes (e.g., "anchored edge contain" vs. "stunt inside"). Data: NGS tracking via nflverse (or NFL's official Big Data Bowl releases for 2018+). Effort: ~1 week for a first pass with scikit-learn.
2. **Commit-vs-help game model**: replicate §3.3 for NFL help decisions — e.g., safety choosing commit-to-man vs. robber/help on a run-pass conflict, or edge defender choosing contain vs. crash. Build the payoff matrix from nflverse EPA data (expected points per play as a function of the defender's commitment level, discretized), add a parametrized help-impact factor analogous to (1 − 1/α^(22−d)), solve the Nash equilibrium, and compare to observed defender behavior in NGS data to detect systematically suboptimal "in-between" positioning. Effort: ~2 weeks including payoff-matrix calibration.
3. **Natural-experiment validation**: where the paper uses FIBA court geometry, GSE can use rule/season discontinuities (e.g., pre/post-2018 helmet rule, kickoff rule changes 2024) as quasi-experiments for causal checks on metric claims.

## 12. Reproducible test
Replicate the generation analysis on public NFL data: take the NFL Big Data Bowl tracking release (e.g., 2018–2024 weekly data), extract edge-rusher (x, y) trajectories over the first 1.5 seconds post-snap for ≥10,000 pass-rush snaps, run k-means with k selected by the gap statistic (testing k = 2..15), compute radius of gyration per cluster, and check whether a small number of coherent archetypes emerge (criterion: top-2 clusters contain ≥40% of snaps with radius of gyration below the dataset median). Baseline: uniform random clustering assignment. Time window: one full season of tracking data (e.g., 2023). Runnable entirely with public data + scikit-learn; no proprietary feeds needed.

## 13. Acceptance / rejection gate
**Adopt** the trajectory-clustering pipeline into GSE's NGS lane if the reproducible test yields ≥2 clusters each containing ≥20% of pass-rush snaps with radius of gyration below the median of all clusters AND the cluster archetypes are interpretable as distinct rush styles on manual review of ≥50 sampled snaps. **Reject** (keep as method reference only) if the gap statistic selects k = 1 or the clusters collapse into noise (top cluster <20% share or no cluster with below-median coherence), since that would indicate the pipeline finds nothing stable in NFL tracking data. Gate evaluated on the 2023 Big Data Bowl tracking season before any production wiring.

## 14. Improvement experiment
Go beyond the paper by replacing k-means with **time-warped clustering**: use dynamic time warping (DTW) distance on the trajectories (k-medoids or hierarchical clustering) instead of Euclidean k-means on fixed windows, which handles the variable timing of NFL plays (different snap-to-pressure durations) that fixed-window k-means cannot. Then test whether DTW-discovered rush archetypes predict pressure/sack outcomes better than the paper-style Euclidean clusters in a logistic regression on 2024 holdout data (metric: out-of-sample AUC for pressure within 2.5 s). If DTW clusters win by ≥0.02 AUC, the Euclidean step in the pipeline is upgraded permanently. Second improvement: replace the league-average payoff matrix with **player-specific** matrices (per-paper's own suggested extension, unattempted) — build per-rusher commit/payoff matrices and solve individual Nash equilibria, testing whether player-specific equilibria explain observed behavior better than the league-average one.
