# [0320] Data-driven modelling of low-dimensional dynamical structures underlying complex full-body human movement (arXiv:2602.11492v1)

**Citation:** Ryota Takamido, Chiharu Suzuki, Hiroki Nakamoto (2026). *Data-driven modelling of low-dimensional dynamical structures underlying complex full-body human movement*. arXiv:2602.11492v1. URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.11492v1
**Ledger completed:** 2026-09-21. **Read:** full text (PDF text extract, 604 lines).
**Verdict:** ADAPT — the baseball-pitching mocap domain does not transfer to the NFL, but the method (transformer-encoded latent space + neural-ODE dynamical flow predicting full movement from ~8% initial conditions) is a directly portable pattern for NFL tracking-data player-trajectory forecasting (NGS lane); adapt by swapping 45-D mocap joints for NGS tracking frames and adding play-context conditioning.

## 1. Research question
Can the dynamical systems approach (DSA) — which explains complex movement as the time evolution of a low-dimensional dynamical system — be extended from cyclic/simplified movements to complex, non-cyclic, ecologically valid full-body movements? The authors use baseball pitching as the test case and ask whether Neural Ordinary Differential Equations (NODEs) can learn governing dynamics directly from data (without predefined order parameters, periodicity, or stationarity assumptions) and predict the full motion from only its initial segment.

## 2. Dataset / schema
Lab motion-capture baseball pitching dataset: 8 collegiate-league pitchers (174.1 ± 4.1 cm, 74.91 ± 3.8 kg, 19.87 ± 1.0 years; all with >10 years experience). 16 synchronized optical motion-capture cameras (Raptor-E and Kestrel 2200, Motion Analysis Corp.) at 200 Hz. 15 joints extracted: head (parietal), bilateral shoulders (acromions), elbows (lateral epicondyles), wrists (radial styloid), hips (greater trochanters), knees (lateral femoral condyles), heels and toes — giving 45-dimensional features (15 joints × 3D positions) per frame. Per-pitcher datasets (Table 1): sub01: 105 pitches / 450 frames; sub02: 100 / 396; sub03: 81 / 488; sub04: 137 / 513; sub05: 140 / 447; sub06: 115 / 503; sub07: 140 / 359; sub08: 150 / 391 (968 pitches total). Analysis window: onset = earliest frame where lead-knee vertical upward velocity dropped below 5% of its maximum, tracing backward from maximum lead-knee height; end = ball release (maximum right-wrist velocity), temporally aligned using the latest-release trial as reference. Crucially, no temporal normalization (real physical time preserved); some trials include follow-through beyond ball release, others end exactly at release — deliberately increasing late-sequence inter-trial variability. Anonymized dataset on GitHub (see §8).

## 3. Method / model
Three components, trained end-to-end:
(a) **Transformer encoder** (3 layers, 8 attention heads per layer, model dimension 256; causal mask to prevent future access) maps the full motion sequence x_{0:T−1} ∈ R^{T×45} to K=12 latent points z_{0:K−1} ∈ R^{K×3} — i.e., the 45-D pitching motion is embedded as 12 trajectory points in a 3-D latent space. The initial state z_0 encodes approximately the first 8% of the motion and serves as the ODE's initial condition.
(b) **Neural ODE**: from z_0, the latent trajectory is generated by integrating dẑ(t)/dt = f_θ(z(t), t), where f_θ is a 3-layer MLP (two hidden layers × 128 units, tanh activations) defining a vector field on the latent space; deterministic flow, trajectory length T matched to the original sequence.
(c) **MLP decoder** (3 fully connected layers, two hidden layers × 256 units ReLU, linear output) reconstructs the 45-D joint positions from the predicted latent trajectory.
Loss (VAE-style): L = λ_recon L_recon + λ_KL L_KL, with λ_recon = 1.0, λ_KL = 1×10⁻³ (KL regularizes latent posterior toward standard normal, encouraging a smooth latent space for a well-behaved flow). Training: Adam, learning rate 1×10⁻⁴, batch size 32, 1500 epochs, NVIDIA T4 on Google Colab. Features standardized with train-set mean/std. **10-fold cross-validation per pitcher** (9 subsets train, 1 test); at test time latent variables are deterministic (mean of encoded distribution).

## 4. Equations & assumptions
Paper's stated equations, copied faithfully:

(1) dẑ(t)/dt = f_θ(z(t), t) — latent state evolution as a deterministic neural ODE flow, f_θ an MLP vector field.

(2) L = λ_recon L_recon + λ_KL L_KL — reconstruction loss plus KL-divergence regularization toward a standard normal prior.

(3) R²(t) = 1 − SS_res(t)/SS_ori(t) — time-resolved coefficient of determination, where SS_ori(t) is total sum of squares of original data at frame t and SS_res(t) the residual sum of squares between original and predicted motion.

Stated assumptions: (a) full-body motion admits a low-dimensional (d=3) latent dynamical structure; (b) the deterministic ODE flow captures the high reproducibility/stereotypy of expert movement; (c) the initial ~8% of motion suffices as the initial condition determining subsequent evolution (sensitivity to initial conditions); (d) real physical time (no temporal normalization) is the correct clock; (e) KL-regularized smooth latent space permits a well-behaved vector field; (f) causal masking prevents the encoder from leaking future information into z_0. Assumption (b) is explicitly a modeling choice — stochastic fluctuations are acknowledged as missing (see §9).

## 5. Features / target
Input features: 45-D per frame (15 joint 3-D positions, standardized). Encoder output: K=12 latent states in d=3 dimensions. Target: the full 45-D joint-position time series (reconstruction of all frames), with prediction scored per frame and per joint. Prediction task framing: forecast the remaining ~92% of motion using only the initial ~8% (via z_0 → ODE integration → decode).

## 6. Validation design
10-fold cross-validation within each of the 8 pitcher datasets (9 subsets train, 1 test). Two metrics: (a) per-frame RMSE aggregated per joint across pitchers and folds, compared against a **training-mean baseline** (each frame predicted as the training-data mean); (b) time-resolved R²(t) via Eq. (3), summarized as mean R² over the entire series and over the latter 50% (the higher-variability phase). Deterministic test-time latents (posterior mean). No cross-pitcher generalization test (models trained per pitcher — a real gap); no comparison against other motion-prediction architectures (e.g., RNN/seq2seq or diffusion baselines, despite citing them in related work).

## 7. Numerical results / baselines
All numbers quoted from the paper (paper's claims):
- Mean RMSE across all pitchers and the entire prediction interval: **66.1 ± 15.7 mm**.
- Mean R² over the entire motion: **0.46 ± 0.20**; mean R² over the second half: **0.49 ± 0.20** (abstract: "R² > 0.45").
- Baseline (training-data mean): RMSE shows a pronounced increase in the late phase; NODE prediction mitigates this late-phase error growth (Figure 2).
- Key claim: "approximately 50% of the variance in the latter half of the pitching motion was explained using only the initial ~8% of the temporal sequence."
- Qualitative: generated motions capture trial-to-trial variability in later evolution from subtle preparatory-phase differences (sensitivity to initial latent state); reconstructed motions appear more stereotyped than ground truth (Figure 4).

## 8. Code / data availability
Source code, anonymized dataset, and supporting materials (including overlay videos of all pitching motions and reconstructed-motion videos) publicly available at https://github.com/takamido/NODE_baseball_pitching. Previously collected data; opt-out informed consent framework per university ethics guidelines.

## 9. Leakage & limitations
- **No information leakage**: causal mask in encoder; z_0 derived only from initial 8%; proper within-pitcher 10-fold CV; no temporal normalization is actually a strength (avoids phase-rescaling lookahead).
- **Variability reduction (movement stereotypy)**: generated motions are visibly more stereotyped than ground truth — the deterministic model drops ~50% of variance, attributed to fluctuations around the dominant flow that the model doesn't represent (functional variability, Stergiou & Decker 2011, is task-relevant, not noise). Deterministic latent ODE is a deliberate simplification; stochastic extensions (latent SDEs, ref [36]) are future work.
- **Expert-only sample**: 8 highly experienced pitchers with stereotyped, stable patterns (deliberate-practice effect). Paper itself notes prediction would likely degrade on novices; this is also a generalizability concern for any population beyond elite repeaters.
- **No cross-pitcher evaluation**: each model is pitcher-specific; no test of whether latent dynamics transfer across individuals — the "individual-specific dynamical structure" claim is untested across subjects.
- **Weak baselines**: only the training-mean predictor; no RNN/transformer seq2seq or diffusion motion-prediction baseline despite citing strong prior NODE work (refs [22, 23]).
- **Not canonical DSA**: no order/control parameters, no demonstrated phase transitions or metastability; the latent variables are uninterpreted (no mathematical analysis of the learned flow, ref [37] deferred).
- **n=8 subjects, lab mocap**: external validity to in-game NFL tracking data (10 Hz, 22 players, occlusion, no marker set) is a large domain jump; baseball pitching is a closed, rehearsed skill — football movement is open and reactive.
- **No uncertainty quantification**: point predictions only; no confidence intervals on R² or RMSE beyond fold-wise std.

## 10. GSE overlap
Existing map coverage: **diffusion trajectory modeling (2503.18589)** already read — Garrett's corpus has a trajectory-generation method (diffusion) for sports movement; the 15-area ML brief includes "representation learning on play-by-play" and NGS tracking is inventoried (27 metric families). None of the existing work uses **neural ODE latent dynamics conditioned on initial frames** for movement prediction — diffusion is the generative paradigm in-house; NODE is a complementary/deterministic alternative. Baseball pitching biomechanics is a new domain not otherwise covered. **Verdict: extension** — the method pattern extends the trajectory-modeling lane (diffusion) with a latent-dynamics alternative, directly applicable to NGS tracking data (initial frames of a play → predicted player trajectory), which the NGS-replacement spec would value.

## 11. GSE implementation spec
Build an NFL player-trajectory predictor in the NGS/tracking lane:
- **Data**: NGS tracking data (10 Hz player positions) — replace 45-D mocap joints with per-player (x, y, speed, orientation) sequences; play clips from nflverse + Big Data Bowl tracking releases. Frame the task as: given the first ~0.5 s of a play (analog of the initial 8%), predict the ball-carrier's and nearest defenders' full trajectories via a learned latent ODE.
- **Architecture**: transformer encoder → low-dim latent (d=3–8) → neural ODE (MLP vector field) → MLP decoder, mirroring the paper; add play-context conditioning (down, distance, formation embedding, personnel) into z_0 and f_θ — the paper's missing "control parameters."
- **Training**: per-position models (RB, WR, QB) rather than per-pitcher; torchdiffeq for the ODE solve; loss = reconstruction MSE + KL (paper's Eq. 2).
- **Product use**: (a) real-time "route continuation" prediction for broadcast-style visualizations; (b) expected-YAC modeling conditioned on predicted defender trajectories; (c) anomaly detection (plays where realized trajectory deviates from latent-flow prediction → broken tackles, missed assignments).
- **Effort**: 1 engineer-week for a Big-Data-Bowl-data prototype (public tracking sample), 2–3 weeks to productionize on the NGS replacement pipeline. Compare head-to-head vs the repo's diffusion trajectory approach (2503.18589) on identical data.

## 12. Reproducible test
Dataset: publicly released NFL Big Data Bowl tracking data (or the paper's own GitHub mocap dataset as a sanity check). Metric: mean per-frame displacement RMSE of predicted ball-carrier trajectory over the full play, vs a constant-velocity baseline and vs the training-mean baseline from the paper. Window: one full season of BDB tracking (or all 8 pitchers' data for the sanity check). Baseline to beat: constant-velocity extrapolation from the initial 0.5 s (a stronger, more honest baseline than train-mean). Success = NODE latent-ODE beats constant velocity by ≥ 15% RMSE on held-out games, reproducing the paper's late-phase error-mitigation pattern.

## 13. Acceptance / rejection gate
**Adopt** (as a tracking-lane model family) if, on held-out NFL tracking plays, the latent-ODE model reduces ball-carrier trajectory RMSE by ≥ 15% vs constant-velocity extrapolation AND produces calibrated uncertainty is not required (deterministic allowed) but cross-player generalization holds: a model trained on RBs must retain ≥ 70% of its within-RB improvement when applied to WRs (guards against the paper's per-subject overfitting). **Reject** if it only matches diffusion-trajectory performance (2503.18589) at higher implementation cost, or if latent dimension > 8 is needed to match baseline — the paper's whole point is d=3 sufficiency.

## 14. Improvement experiment
Beyond the paper: make the latent ODE **stochastic and context-conditioned** — (a) replace the deterministic flow with a latent SDE (neural drift + learned diffusion, per the paper's ref [36]) to model functional variability explicitly instead of averaging it away; (b) condition the vector field on play context (down/distance/formation/defensive shell) as explicit DSA "control parameters," then test for the canonical DSA phenomena the paper skipped: sweep a control parameter (e.g., defender distance at handoff) and look for bifurcations/phase transitions in predicted ball-carrier trajectories. This would be the first rigorous test of whether football tracking data exhibits true dynamical-systems phase structure — and a miss-assignment detector (trajectory crosses a predicted phase boundary) is a genuinely new defensive metric.
