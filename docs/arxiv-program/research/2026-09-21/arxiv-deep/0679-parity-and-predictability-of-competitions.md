## 1. Research question
"What is the most competitive sports league?" — answered via an extensive statistical survey of game results in five major professional sports leagues (English FA soccer, MLB, NHL, NBA, NFL). The paper relates parity among teams (variance in season-end winning fractions) to game predictability (frequency of upsets) using a novel single-parameter theoretical model in which the underdog wins with a fixed upset probability q, and shows q can be estimated from easily-accessible standings data.

## 2. Dataset / schema
More than 300,000 regular-season games spanning over a century, from two public sources (http://www.shrpsports.com/, http://www.theenglishfootballarchive.com/). Per-league summary (Table I):

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| FA | 1888–2005 | 43,350 | 39.7 | 0.102 | 0.452 | 0.459 |
| MLB | 1901–2005 | 163,720 | 155.5 | 0.084 | 0.441 | 0.413 |
| NHL | 1917–2004 | 39,563 | 70.8 | 0.120 | 0.414 | 0.383 |

NFL data includes the short-lived AFL; incomplete seasons (e.g. the quickly abandoned 1939 FA season) and nineteenth-century National League baseball results were excluded. Implied schema: per-game records (date, teams, score/outcome) plus season-end standings (team, wins, games). All-time team records (Table II, teams with ≥10 seasons, defunct franchises ignored): FA 37 teams (⟨games⟩=2,154), MLB 26 (13,100), NHL 26 (2,850), NBA 27 (3,060), NFL 31 (720).

## 3. Method / model
Parity is quantified by σ = √(⟨x²⟩ − ⟨x⟩²), the standard deviation of the season-end win fraction across teams and years (Fort 1995 definition). Predictability is quantified by the upset frequency q, measured directly from chronological game results: reconstruct league standings on each game date, and count the fraction of games in which the team with the worse record won. Games between teams with no record (season start) or equal records were disregarded; game location and margin of victory were ignored; ties (fractions 0.246 in soccer, 0.144 in hockey, 0.016 in football) were counted as 1/2 victory for each side, with a sensitivity check that ignoring ties changes q by at most 0.02.

An artificial-league simulation grounds the theory: N teams paired at random play a fixed number of games; the team with the better record is the favorite, the worse the underdog; the underdog wins with probability q < 1/2, the favorite with 1 − q, and equal-record games are decided randomly. The value qmodel is the q giving the best match between the simulated win-fraction distribution F(x) and the empirical one (Fig. 1). A kinetic-theory analysis (competition rate 1/2; t = average games per team playing the role of time; gk(t) = fraction of teams with k wins) yields nonlinear master equations, solved in the large-t continuum limit to give the closed-form F(x) and the σ–q relation.

## 4. Equations & assumptions
- Win-fraction cumulative distribution, infinite-game limit (Eq. 1): F(x) = 0 for 0 < x < q; F(x) = (x − q)/(1 − 2q) for q < x < 1 − q; F(x) = 1 for 1 − q < x. Hence the density f(x) is uniform on [q, 1 − q], zero elsewhere.
- Parity–predictability relation (Eq. 2): σ = (1/2 − q)/√3 — the standard deviation is a linear function of the upset probability. The paper states "the bias 1/2 − q and the variance σ are equivalent measures of the competitiveness."
- Master equation for the win-number distribution (Eq. A1): dgk/dt = (1 − q)(g_{k−1}G_{k−1} − g_k G_k) + q(g_{k−1}H_{k−1} − g_k H_k) + (g²_{k−1} − g²_k)/2, where Gk = Σ_{j=0}^{k−1} gj and Hk = Σ_{j=k+1}^{∞} gj are cumulative distributions of teams with fewer/more than k wins; boundary condition g_{−1}(t) = 0; initial condition gk(0) = δ_{k,0} (all teams equal at season start). First pair of terms: stronger team wins; second pair: weaker team wins; last pair: equal-team games (prefactor 1/2 because there are half as many ways to choose equal teams).
- Cumulative master equation (Eq. A2): dGk/dt = q(G_{k−1} − Gk) + (1/2 − q)(G²_{k−1} − G²_k), with G_0 = 0, G_∞ = 1, Gk(0) = 1 for k > 0. Summing gives mean wins ⟨k⟩ = t/2.
- Continuum PDE (Eq. A3): ∂G/∂t + [q + (1 − 2q)G] ∂G/∂k = 0, from replacing differences with derivatives.
- Scaling ansatz (Eq. A4): Gk(t) → F(k/t) in the long-time limit; with x = k/t the win fraction.
- Scaled ODE (Eq. A5): [(x − q) − (1 − 2q)F(x)] dF/dx = 0, whose admissible solutions (constant, or linear F(x) = (x − q)/(1 − 2q)) with boundary/continuity conditions F(0) = 0, F(1) = 1 yield Eq. (1).

Stated assumptions: all teams equal at season start — "our idealized model does not include the notion of innate team strength"; disparity emerges by chance; fixed-game schedule (each team plays all others equally often); no ties in the model (paper notes a tie-probability parameter is a straightforward generalization); underdog win probability q is fixed and independent of the strength differential; game location ignored; equal-strength games are coin flips; large-t limit so fluctuations in games played vanish.

## 5. Features / target
Features: season-end standings (win fractions), chronological game results, team records on each game date. Target: league competitiveness index q (upset frequency), and σ (win-fraction dispersion) as its standings-based proxy. Horizon: per-season and cumulative-over-time estimates; plus all-time career-record analysis.

## 6. Validation design
No train/test split — this is descriptive empirical work, validated four ways: (a) qmodel (simulation best-fit) vs directly measured q across the five leagues (Table I); (b) the all-time team-record analysis (Table II, Fig. 3) as an independent long-t check of the same q estimates; (c) temporal consistency: cumulative σ and q tracked year by year (Fig. 2a/2b), and all three trend conclusions drawn from both measures agreeing; (d) finite-season simulations vs the infinite-game theory (Fig. 4, q = 1/4, t = 100 and t = 500), confirming convergence to the uniform limit. Robustness checks: ignoring games between teams separated by <0.05 in win percentage changes q by <0.005 on average; ignoring the first half of each season changes q by <0.007; ignoring ties changes q by at most 0.02. No baselines beyond the random-game limit (σ = 1/(2√n) for a pure-random season) and Eq. (2).

## 7. Numerical results / baselines
- Headline (measured q): soccer (FA) q = 0.452 and baseball (MLB) q = 0.441 are the most competitive; basketball (NBA) q = 0.365 and football (NFL) q = 0.364 the least — "nearly identical" (consistent with Stern 1997, 1998). NHL q = 0.414.
- Model-fit qmodel: FA 0.459, MLB 0.413, NHL 0.383, NBA 0.316, NFL 0.309. Systematic bias: q − qmodel grows as games become less competitive (MLB 0.028, NHL 0.031, NBA 0.049, NFL 0.055); FA is the exception (−0.007). The paper notes σ "mirrors the bias, 1/2 − q."
- Finite-season variance exceeds the infinite-game limit: measured seasonal σ (0.084–0.210) is far above Eq.-(2) values (e.g. NFL: (0.5−0.364)/√3 = 0.0785 vs measured 0.210), because season length dominates — "the small number of games is partially responsible for the large variability observed in the NFL."
- All-time records (Table II): FA σall = 0.035 → qall = 0.439, xmax = 0.582 (Liverpool), xmin = 0.406 (Crystal Palace); MLB σall = 0.024 → qall = 0.458, 0.567 (NYY), 0.459 (SDP); NHL σall = 0.044 → qall = 0.424, 0.589 (Montreal Canadiens), 0.403 (Tampa Bay Lightning); NBA σall = 0.057 → qall = 0.401, 0.616 (LAL), 0.358 (LAC); NFL σall = 0.057 → qall = 0.401, 0.600 (Miami Dolphins), 0.358 (Tampa Bay Buccaneers). qall = 1/2 − √3·σall inverts Eq. (2) and agrees with season-level q, since all-time records are long-t.
- Temporal trends (from both q and σ): (1) MLB games "steadily becoming more competitive"; (2) "NFL has dramatically improved the competitiveness of its games over the past 40 years"; (3) FA shows "an opposite trend with the games becoming less competitive" over the past 60 years — a "rich gets richer" scenario.
- Home-field datapoint (unmodeled): during the 2005 baseball season, 54% of total team wins occurred at home.

## 9. Leakage & limitations
No lookahead in the predictive sense (no model is deployed prospectively), but the following limit external validity to NFL: (i) no home-field advantage in the model, while e.g. 54% of 2005 MLB wins were at home; (ii) unbalanced schedules and divisional subdivisions assumed away — the authors tested this and found "small influence"; (iii) fixed q: the paper admits "it is plausible that the upset probability q depends on the relative strengths of the two competing teams," and this generalization is "especially relevant for tournament competitions"; (iv) the favorite/underdog label comes from the current record, a noisy strength proxy — their sensitivity analyses bound this at Δq < 0.007, but it still conflates luck with strength early in seasons; (v) the model fits worst where GSE cares most: the NFL q − qmodel gap (0.055) is the largest of the five leagues, so the closed-form theory is weakest for football; (vi) data ends 2004–2005, predating the 2011 CBA rest-rule change, the 17-game season, and modern spread offenses; (vii) Eq. (2) conflates season length with competitiveness, so cross-era NFL comparisons are contaminated by schedule-length changes. My adversarial note: the "all teams equal at season start, disparity by chance" mechanism is a modeling convenience, not a finding — it cannot distinguish structural parity (salary cap, draft) from randomness, which is exactly the distinction a betting model needs.

## 11. GSE implementation spec
Three small builds: (1) q-index pipeline: from nflverse play-by-play/schedules, reconstruct standings at each game date, label underdog = worse record on date, compute q per season and rolling 5-season windows; apply the finite-season correction from the paper's simulations rather than the raw Eq. (2). (2) Parity-regime monitor: track NFL q from 2000 through 2026; flag regime shifts (post-2011 CBA, 17-game season, schedule-matrix changes) — publishes as a content product ("how predictable is the NFL this year") and as a prior for GSE's favorite-win-rate expectations. (3) Season-simulator dispersion gate: given fitted q, the simulated season-end win-fraction σ must match historical σ within tolerance; divergence means the simulator's team-strength dispersion is mis-specified. Effort: ~1 day for (1)+(2); ~2 days including (3) validation.

## 12. Reproducible test
Dataset: nflverse 2002–2025 regular-season games. Compute per-season measured q (worse-record-on-date wins / decisive games) and seasonal σ. Test A: the pipeline must reproduce the paper's methodology stably — year-to-year q std < 0.03 and no implementation artifacts (spot-check 3 seasons by hand). Test B: predict seasonal σ from q using the paper's finite-season simulation mapping (not the infinite-limit Eq. 2); target mean absolute relative error ≤ 10% across 2002–2025. Baseline to beat: the pure-random-season σ = 1/(2√17) ≈ 0.121 — measured-vs-random ratio quantifies how much real parity exceeds chance. Stretch check: if feasible, reproduce q ≈ 0.364 on 1922–2004 PFR data to validate the pipeline end-to-end.

## 13. Acceptance / rejection gate
Adopt the q-index as a standing GSE parity diagnostic if Test A passes (stable q, std < 0.03) AND Test B achieves MARE ≤ 10%. Adopt the Eq.-(2) inversion as a quick standings→predictability estimator only if the NFL-specific q − qmodel bias seen in the paper (0.055) shrinks on modern data; otherwise keep q as a descriptive measure and drop the theoretical mapping for NFL use. Reject entirely if q computed with vs without the first-half-of-season exclusion differs by more than the paper's 0.007 bound (would indicate the pipeline, not the paper, is broken).

## 14. Improvement experiment
Generalize the fixed-q model exactly as the paper suggests: estimate q as a function of the record differential |x_fav − x_dog| (e.g. a fitted monotone curve, logistic or isotonic) on NFL data, plus a second parameter for home/away underdog splits — the paper lists both (variable upset probability; game location) as the prominent unmodeled aspects. The resulting empirical curve is a calibration prior: compare GSE engine win probabilities against the record-gap-implied baseline; systematic divergence at small gaps would reveal where tracking/EPA features add real information over standings alone. Second experiment: fit q separately by week-of-season to test whether early-season "unestablished records" really are negligible (the paper's aggregate <0.007 claim) at the weekly resolution GSE bets on.
