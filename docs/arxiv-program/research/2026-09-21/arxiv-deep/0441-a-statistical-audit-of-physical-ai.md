# [0441] A Statistical Audit of Physical AI Benchmark Redundancy (arXiv:2608.25940v2)

**Citation:** Zaruhi Navasardyan, Hrant Davtyan (2026). *A Statistical Audit of Physical AI Benchmark Redundancy*. arXiv:2608.25940v2. URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2608.25940v2
**Ledger completed:** 2026-09-21. **Read:** full text (§§1–5, Appendices A–C, references; 2,435 text lines).
**Verdict:** ADAPT — the physical-AI domain doesn't transfer, but the benchmark-redundancy audit machinery (pairwise Spearman + LOO ridge predictability + greedy utility forward-selection + Bradley–Terry over benchmarks-as-judges) ports directly to pruning GSE's own metric catalog and to auditing which of GSE's model variants actually add unique signal.

## 1. Research question
The paper asks two measurement questions about physical-AI evaluation: (RQ1 — redundancy) how much information do the 12 physical-AI benchmarks share, and how strongly does that redundancy inflate pooled averages used in vendor reporting? (RQ2 — sufficiency) how small can a benchmark suite be while still separating models and covering the non-redundant abilities of the full suite? The motivation: vendors report each new model on hand-picked, barely-overlapping benchmark sets, and headline averages silently double-count capabilities measured repeatedly (e.g., pointing evaluated 3–4 times within one model report).

## 2. Dataset / schema
- Registry of 51 physical-AI benchmarks and 152 models from model cards, papers, official blogs. Selection filters: density (benchmark reported for ≥5 models), recency (recurs across recent reports), diversity (spans key tasks). Final: 51 models × 12 benchmarks.
- The 12 benchmarks (all 0–100 scale; 10 multiple-choice, 2 coordinate-pointing): VSI-Bench (5,130 items, n=51), EmbSpatial (3,640, n=51), RefSpatial-Bench (200, n=51), Where2Place (100, n=50), ERQA (400, n=49), CV-Bench (2,638, n=49), SAT (150, n=49), RoboSpatial (350, n=47), RealWorldQA (765, n=42), OmniSpatial (1,533, n=42), MindCube (21,154, n=42), BLINK (3,807, n=41).
- 51 models released 2024–2026 from 15 providers (open and closed, 1B–241B params), incl. robotics/spatial post-trained variants; models retained if ≥8/12 scores reported. Matrix completed by the authors' own runs under each benchmark's official protocol (159 additional cells, greedy decoding, benchmark-native parsing); published cells not systematically re-verified.
- Plus 9 general benchmarks on the same models for the shared-capability analysis (Appendix B).
- Project home: https://metric-ai-lab.github.io/metabench/

## 3. Method / model
- Preprocessing: z-score every benchmark column before multivariate steps; use Spearman rank correlations as the default pairwise measure (immune to difficulty differences); difficulty re-enters later via saturation penalties.
- RQ1 (redundancy): (a) pairwise Spearman ρ with average-linkage hierarchical clustering on distance 1−ρ; (b) benchmark-level predictability: ridge regression of each benchmark on the other 11, scored by leave-one-model-out cross-validated R²; uniqueness = 1 − LOO R²; (c) demonstration of inflated pooled averages: replace each substitute pair with the mean of its two columns, recompute equal-weight averages and rankings.
- RQ2 (sufficiency): greedy forward selection under utility U(b|S) = g(b)·(1 − R²(b∼S)) (eq. 1), where g(b) = Gini coefficient of the benchmark's score distribution (discrimination) and R²(b∼S) = OLS fit of candidate on selected set (marginal information); product form so a benchmark failing either criterion gets zero utility. Selection starts from S=∅ (first pick = most discriminating), runs through all 12, cumulative utility reported as % of the all-12 total.
- Ranking on the minimal suite: Bradley–Terry fit in the preference-arena style (each benchmark = one judge; for every model pair and every shared benchmark, one binary vote for the higher scorer; 4,231 observations), P(i≻j) = σ(ri − rj) by MLE with L2 penalty 1e-3, strengths centered, mapped to Elo_i = 1500 + 400·ri/ln10 (eq. 2).
- Appendix B: single principal component analysis of shared variance; residualize each benchmark on the general-capability axis (estimated from the 9 general benchmarks).

## 4. Equations & assumptions
- Greedy utility (eq. 1): U(b|S) = g(b)·(1 − R²(b∼S)), g(b) = Gini coefficient of benchmark score distribution; R²(b∼S) = OLS regression fit of b on selected set S. Product (not sum) so failure of either criterion zeroes utility.
- Bradley–Terry (eq. 2): P(i≻j) = σ(ri − rj); Elo_i = 1500 + 400·ri/ln10.
- Assumptions: (a) aggregate benchmark scores are comparable after z-scoring (difficulty differences handled by rank correlations, then z-scores for multivariate steps); (b) linear predictability (ridge/OLS R²) is an adequate measure of shared information; (c) the 51 models with ≥8/12 scores are representative; (d) Gini spread = discrimination quality (untested against other discrimination measures — the authors note the core is untested against a different definition of g); (e) binary benchmark-as-judge votes (ignoring margin size) are adequate for ranking; (f) greedy forward selection accepted without optimality guarantee.

## 5. Features / target
- No ML features in the usual sense; the "features" are the 12 benchmark score columns themselves. Target of the predictability analysis: each benchmark's column predicted from the other 11. Target of the selection: minimal subset. Target of the ranking: Bradley–Terry strengths (Elo) over the 4-benchmark core.

## 6. Validation design
No train/test splits in the ML sense — this is an observational audit. Design elements: (a) benchmark inclusion criteria (density ≥5 models, recency, diversity) fixed a priori; (b) pairwise-complete Spearman correlations with 95% CIs on the substitute pairs; (c) LOO cross-validated R² for predictability (each model held out once); (d) the ranking-inversion demonstration (collapse substitute pairs → recompute rankings); (e) forward-selection stability check (Appendix C, Table 8): forcing VSI-Bench or BLINK into the opening slot returns the identical four benchmarks in a different order (4/4 kept, 77.8–78.3% utility vs 78.5% unconstrained); forcing Where2Place — the substitute of the natural opening pick — does not keep RefSpatial-Bench out (it re-enters at step 4 on dispersion, U=0.343×0.248=0.085, displacing BLINK to fifth; 74.5%); forcing rejected benchmarks (RoboSpatial, SAT) displaces one core member to fifth (69.2–71.6%). MindCube and VSI-Bench enter within the first four in every run; RefSpatial-Bench is never kept out. (f) general-capability residualization via 9 external benchmarks.

## 7. Numerical results / baselines
- Pairwise redundancy: average Spearman ρ = 0.487 across all pairs, all positive. Substitute pairs above 0.8: EmbSpatial↔CV-Bench ρ = 0.876, 95% CI [0.78, 0.93], n=49; Where2Place↔RefSpatial-Bench ρ = 0.860, [0.73, 0.93], n=50. Other notable: ERQA+RealWorldQA ρ=0.758; RealWorldQA+OmniSpatial ρ=0.748. BLINK is the most isolated (own dendrogram branch).
- LOO predictability (Table 2): Where2Place R²=0.727 (uniqueness 0.273), RefSpatial-Bench 0.721 (0.279), ERQA 0.706 (0.294) — most redundant; RealWorldQA 0.319 (0.681), RoboSpatial 0.351 (0.649), BLINK 0.378 (0.622) — least predictable. Median benchmark has roughly half its variance reconstructible from the other 11. MindCube is the most frequent top predictor of other benchmarks (7 of 11).
- Ranking impact: collapsing the two substitute pairs moves 22 of 51 models by ≥3 places under equal weighting; MiMo-Embodied-7B drops 9 places, Gemini Robotics-ER 1.5 drops 8; GPT-4o gains 9, Claude-Sonnet-4 gains 8.
- Minimal suite: forward selection picks RefSpatial-Bench → MindCube → VSI-Bench → BLINK; these 4 reach 78.5% of the all-12 utility. Where2Place (second-highest Gini) is passed over three times and enters 5th at 85.0% cumulative; the final four benchmarks add only 4.4% between them.
- Bradley–Terry leaderboard (Table 3, top 10 Elo): HY-Embodied-0.5 MoE-407B-A32B 2251; Qwen3.5-397B-A17B 2032; Qwen3-VL-235B-A22B-Instruct 1828; Seed 2.0 1790; Kimi K2.5 1750; HY-Embodied-0.5 MoT-4B-A2B 1744; Gemini 3.0 Pro 1716; Qwen3-VL-32B-Instruct 1662; Gemini-2.5-Pro-preview-05-06 1643; RoboBrain-32B-2.0 1630. Domain post-training shows gains (3 of top 10 are embodiment/spatial post-trained); size is monotone only within a recipe.
- Appendix B: one principal component explains 55.2% of suite variance (all 12 benchmarks load positively, 0.48 RealWorldQA to 0.88 CV-Bench) and tracks general vision-language capability at Spearman ρ=0.952 (general PC1 of the 9 anchors, n=32, 95% CI [0.82, 0.97]; 0.950 vs MMStar, 0.942 vs Video-MME); residualizing on that axis halves mean pairwise correlation from 0.487 to 0.250 and drops the number of pairs above 0.5 from 34 of 66 to 6 — roughly half the shared variance is general capability, not physical-AI-specific. Three specific bonds survive at near-full strength (EmbSpatial↔CV-Bench 0.876→0.784 residual, Where2Place↔RefSpatial 0.860→0.801, ERQA↔MindCube 0.656→0.613 — the first two are the substitute pairs flagged in RQ1, duplicated because they measure the same specific ability). Suppression finding: VSI-Bench and BLINK are uncorrelated in raw scores (ρ=0.002) but correlate at −0.363 in the residuals (−0.425 in the dense block) — among models of equal general capability, video-spatial strength trades off against multi-image perception.

## 9. Leakage & limitations
- Benchmark-level aggregates only; item-level redundancy/localization impossible (no response-level data, unlike Metabench/Kipnis et al.).
- 159 of the matrix cells are the authors' own runs under official protocols, but there is no systematic reproduction study; published-vs-published inconsistencies were already observed; possible heterogeneity and noise across cells.
- Low R² for a benchmark can mean "measures something distinct" OR "is noisy"; distinguishing requires repeated evaluation under resampled prompts/decoding seeds — data current reporting doesn't provide.
- 51 models × 12 benchmarks is thin for some analyses; authors lean on statistically significant findings.
- Forward selection is greedy, no optimality guarantee; core depends on the utility and is untested against other definitions of discrimination g(b).
- Purely observational: no intervention on training data/objectives, so the dominant axis is correlated with, not proven causal of, benchmark performance.
- None of the 12 benchmarks measures downstream physical-task success, so the "physical signal" residual may not transfer to manipulation/navigation.
- External validity to NFL: physical-AI benchmarks don't transfer; the audit machinery transfers to any model×evaluation matrix.

## 10. GSE overlap
Per existing-research-map.md: GSE's corpus has a 26-metric catalog (v1) + 9 more lanes, 15 metric families computed in gse-lab (29 CSVs), plus benchmarks against 86+ X analyst accounts and metrics like benbbaldwin objective ratings. No redundancy audit of any kind is documented in the map — the catalog is inventoried, never pruned. This paper's machinery is therefore NEW capability: (a) a pairwise-correlation + LOO-predictability audit of GSE's own metric suite (EPA/play vs success rate vs DVOA vs QBR etc. — likely heavily correlated), (b) a greedy minimal-metric-subset selector retaining most discriminating power (useful for the weekly edge-sheet graphics: fewer, non-redundant metrics), (c) the Bradley–Terry benchmarks-as-judges ranking scheme as an alternative to averaged model leaderboards for comparing GSE engine variants. Not a duplicate of anything in the map. Also relevant to the "benchmark completeness audit" (156-item, 2026-09-17) — this gives that audit a quantitative pruning rule.

## 11. GSE implementation spec
Run a "GSE metric-redundancy audit": (1) build a team×metric matrix (32 teams × the 15–26 computed metric families) from gse-lab CSVs over 2015–2025; (2) compute Spearman correlations (z-scored columns) and LOO ridge R² per metric to find the metric-suite's substitute pairs (expected: EPA/play vs success rate vs DVOA-adj variants will cluster); (3) collapse substitute pairs and show how team rankings (e.g., power ratings) shift — the analogue of the 22/51-models-moved demonstration — to justify de-duplicating the published edge sheet; (4) greedy forward-select a minimal "edge-sheet metric core" (target: ≤6 metrics retaining ≥75% of the utility U = Gini-spread × (1−R² on selected)); (5) optionally rank GSE engine variants via Bradley–Terry with metrics-as-judges instead of averaged CLV. Data: existing gse-lab CSVs + nflverse; effort ~1 week for the audit, ~1 week for edge-sheet integration.

## 12. Reproducible test
Dataset: the 2026-09-17 gse-lab CSVs (15 metric families, 32 teams, 2015–2025). Baseline: the full 15-metric equal-weight power rating. Metric: (a) Spearman correlation matrix and LOO-R² uniqueness per metric; (b) rank displacement: Spearman correlation between full-suite team ranking and minimal-core ranking; (c) out-of-sample check: minimal core selected on 2015–2022 must predict 2023–2025 team strength (correlation with subsequent-season EPA/play) at ≥95% of the full suite's level. Time window: fit 2015–2022, evaluate 2023–2025.

## 13. Acceptance / rejection gate
ADOPT the pruned metric core if: (a) the core (≤6 metrics) achieves ≥95% of the full suite's out-of-sample predictive correlation on the 2023–2025 window, and (b) ≥2 substitute pairs with ρ>0.8 are identified and collapsing them moves ≥3 teams by ≥3 places in the power rating (demonstrating the redundancy is decision-relevant). REJECT if the minimal core needs >10 metrics to reach 95% (suite is already non-redundant), or if LOO-R² values are all <0.35 (no meaningful shared structure to prune), or if the core selected on 2015–2022 fails to replicate on a second split (2018–2025 fit / 2023–2025 eval).

## 14. Improvement experiment
Beyond the paper: replace the Gini-based discrimination g(b) with a task-relevant discrimination measure — a metric's Gini spread across teams is not what GSE cares about; use instead each metric's univariate out-of-sample predictive R² for next-season EPA/play (or ATS cover rate). Re-run the greedy selection with U(b|S) = predictive-R²(b) × (1 − R²(b∼S)): this yields a minimal suite optimized for forecasting power rather than spread, directly answering "which metrics earn their place on the edge sheet." The paper's authors note they never varied g; this is the natural first variation, and it's strictly more decision-relevant.
