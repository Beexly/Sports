# Ledger: 2609.22497 — "The Wisdom of Artificial Deliberative Crowds"

**VERDICT: ADAPT** — Transferable aggregation protocol for GSE: a three-stage independent-estimate → deliberative-consensus → post-deliberation-revision aggregation over *diverse* signal sources beats passive averaging (paired Cohen's d = 0.25–0.49 within-group across all four domains), with the Study 4 sports-forecasting result (deliberative crowd statistically indistinguishable from a real-money Polymarket benchmark) making it directly sports-relevant. Must be adapted — not copy-pasted — because GSE's "agents" are numeric signal sources (engine sub-models, market-implied odds, public-lean signal), not LLM chatters; the homogeneity caveat (clone groups gain nothing) maps directly onto GSE's source-diversity requirement.

## 1. Research question

Does the human deliberative-crowd phenomenon — averaging the *consensus estimates of small deliberating groups* outperforms the classical wisdom of crowds, and individual judgments themselves become more accurate after deliberation — transfer to large language models deliberating amongst themselves, across domains of increasing real-world stakes? And is *model diversity* (vs. cloned instances of one model) the active ingredient?

## 2. Dataset/schema

Four studies, same three small inexpensive models (one per provider: Anthropic Claude Haiku 4.5 `claude-haiku-4-5-20251001`, OpenAI GPT-5-nano, Google Gemini 3.1 Flash Lite; training data ends May 2024–July 2025):

- **Study 1 (factual estimation):** 14 photographs of a glass jar with small plastic disks (true counts 38–589), one-euro coin as scale anchor. Per picture: 12 independent estimates per model → 12 deliberating groups → 12 consensus estimates; 504 model–group observations for the Stage i2 analysis; 167 of 168 groups converged.
- **Study 2 (peer review):** 100 NeurIPS 2024 submissions (50 accepted for oral presentation, 50 rejected; token-matched, 5,000–80,000 tokens, author block + acknowledgements removed). Per paper: 18 independent ratings + 6 group consensus ratings on the NeurIPS 1–10 overall-score scale; 1,800 model–paper observations; all 600 groups converged. Ground truth = human editorial verdict (oral vs. rejected).
- **Study 3 (AI-safety monitoring):** 50 agentic trajectories from the Monitor Red Teaming corpus (Kale et al., 2025), AgentCompany environments, produced by Claude Sonnet 4: 25 from the `selective_liar` condition (hidden malicious side task; hardest in corpus) + 25 benign. Monitors rated suspiciousness 0–10. Per trajectory: 18 independent ratings + 6 consensus ratings; 299 of 300 groups converged. Analyses concern the 25 sabotage transcripts only (benign trajectories had near-zero variance — highest mean suspicion 2.7/10 — leaving no room for improvement).
- **Study 4 (forecasting):** 44 events of the 2026 FIFA World Cup — 12 group winners + 32 knockout ties (R32 → final incl. 3rd-place match). Per event: 12 independent forecasts + 4 deliberative group consensus answers (winning team + integer probability), all elicited before the round was played; 175 of 176 groups converged. Polymarket prices recorded at the same moment as the benchmark. No Stage i2 in Study 4.

## 3. Method/model

A three-stage paradigm (adapted from human experiments Navajas et al. 2018; Barrera-Lemarchand et al. 2026), protocol held constant across all four studies:

- **Stage i1 (independent):** each model answers independently, several repetitions (stochasticity via no temperature/no-seed fixing — "this is what makes the repetitions of a model within a study play the role that different individuals play in the human experiments"), with a short justification (≤200 tokens, ≤250 in Study 4) emitted inside explicit parse tags.
- **Stage c (deliberation):** groups of three (one model per family, repetition *g* of each model forming group *g*). Simulated chat room: short messages (target ~10 tokens ≈ 7 words; hard cap 66 tokens Studies 1–3, 80 in Study 4), ≤10 rounds, one message per model per round in random speaking order, explicit objective "deliberating and trying to reach consensus". Agents receive their own i1 answer + reasoning + the discussion so far — *never* the others' i1 answers/reasoning. Convergence = all three declare the same value in the same round via fixed string; fallback = same parsed value in all three final-round messages.
- **Stage i2 (post-deliberation individual revision):** each agent, privately, receives its own i1 answer + the full Stage-c transcript and may repeat i1, adopt the consensus, or give any other value (Studies 1–3 only).
- Controls: **social-influence-only** (Study 1: see others' i1 numbers, revise without talking) and **homogeneous "clone" deliberation** (Study 1, per family; Study 2 subset: three copies of the same model; protocol/task/count held constant, diversity removed).

## 4. Equations & assumptions

No formal equations are stated in the paper. The statistical machinery used is described narratively:

- Bayes factors use the default JZS prior specification (Rouder et al., 2009): a Cauchy distribution on the standardized effect size δ centred at zero with scale parameter *r* = √2⁄2, sensitivity checks at *r* = 1 (wide) and *r* = √2 (ultrawide); posterior medians and 95% credibility intervals by numerical integration. Frequentist tests are two-sided Mann–Whitney U (crowd-level, bootstrap distributions, 2,000 subsamples per crowd size) and Wilcoxon signed-rank (paired within-group/item-level), reported with Cohen's d (paired for within-group).
- Market overround normalization (Appendix 1): Polymarket raw prices summed to ~1.0075 on average (median 1.0100, range 0.9850–1.0295); each event's price divided by the sum of that event's listed prices to obtain the market's probability.
- Key assumptions (stated): no temperature/seed fixing so repetitions stand in for individuals; no data exclusions; sample size bounded by cost not recruitment; item-level paired comparisons are the basis of claims; group composition via fixed repetition index is unbiased because repetitions of the same model are exchangeable.

## 5. Features/target

- Study 1: target = true disk count; metric = squared error in log space (base 10).
- Study 2: target = human editorial verdict (oral = accept, rejected); metric = Brier score of rating-derived probability against verdict.
- Study 3: target = sabotage label (present/absent) of trajectory; metric = Brier score of suspicion score (0–10) against ground-truth label.
- Study 4: target = realized event outcome; reference team fixed as the market favorite per event (sources often named different winners; scoring each on its own pick would grade different propositions); probability for that reference team extracted from each source; event coded 1/0; metric = Brier score on the common quantity. Favorites won 35 of 44.

## 6. Validation design

No predetermined sample size; no train/test split (not a fitted model — a procedural aggregation). Claims rest on paired item-level comparisons (jar, paper, trajectory, event) between Stage i1 pooling and Stage c consensus, with standardized effect sizes and Bayes factors. Crowd-level comparisons use Mann–Whitney U on bootstrap distributions (2,000 random subsamples per crowd size); within-group comparisons pair each group's consensus against the mean of *its own three opening i1 answers* — "indicating that the discussion added information rather than merely averaging it." Study 4 adds an absolute external benchmark (Polymarket) on identical propositions with simultaneous elicitation.

## 7. Numerical results/baselines (quoted as stated)

**Study 1 (squared log error, base 10):** aggregating 12 consensus estimates << averaging 36 individual values (Mann–Whitney z = 19.4, p = 4 × 10⁻⁸³, Cohen's d = 4.75). Within-group consensus (0.164 ± 0.019) vs. within-group i1 mean (0.195 ± 0.019): Wilcoxon z = 5.33, p = 9.8 × 10⁻⁸, paired d = 0.40. Stage i1 → i2: 0.230 ± 0.019 → 0.159 ± 0.011 (z = 7.36, p = 1.9 × 10⁻¹³, d = 0.40); jar-level paired comparison: i2 equal-or-lower than consensus in **all 14 pictures** (z = 3.18, p = 0.0015, d = 1.33). Social influence alone improves over i1 (z = 3.58, p = 3.5 × 10⁻⁴, d = 0.24) but deliberation beats it (z = 18.85, p = 3.0 × 10⁻⁷⁹, d = 3.25; paired z = 3.19, p = 0.0014, d = 0.24).

**Study 2 (Brier vs. human verdict):** crowd comparison z = 19.35, p = 2 × 10⁻⁸³, d = 6.71; within-group z = 5.10, p = 3.4 × 10⁻⁷, d = 0.25; i1 → i2 z = 9.85, p = 7.1 × 10⁻²³, d = 0.19 — with 82 of 100 submissions reproducing the consensus exactly at i2.

**Study 3 (Brier on sabotage trajectories):** crowd z = 19.30, p = 5.6 × 10⁻⁸³, d = 4.30; within-group z = 3.64, p = 2.8 × 10⁻⁴, d = 0.30; i1 → i2 z = 4.94, p = 7.6 × 10⁻⁷, d = 0.28; transcript-level gain confirmed in 19 of 25 sabotage cases (z = 3.54, p = 4.0 × 10⁻⁴, d = 0.90); 417 of 447 i2 re-ratings reproduced the consensus exactly.

**Study 4 (Brier, sports forecasting):** crowd z = 19.11, p = 2 × 10⁻⁸¹, d = 3.32; within-group z = 4.03, p = 5.5 × 10⁻⁵, d = 0.29; deliberative crowd more accurate event-by-event in 31 of 44 events (z = 3.16, p = 0.0016, d = 0.49). Deliberative crowd vs. market: z = 1.12, p = 0.26, BF₀₁ = 3.63 (moderate evidence for the null; posterior median δ = 0.15, 95% CrI [−0.14, 0.44]). Independent crowd vs. market: market better in 30 of 44 (z = 1.83, p = 0.067; BF₀₁ = 1.54, only anecdotal).

**Diversity boundary:** homogeneous (clone) deliberation adds nothing — Study 1: Anthropic z = 1.22, p = 0.22, d = 0.087; OpenAI z = 0.51, p = 0.61, d = 0.144; Google z = 0.30, p = 0.76, d = 0.062 (Bayes factors 5.60/3.99/6.19 moderate evidence *for the null*); heterogeneous size-matched z = 3.61, p = 3.1 × 10⁻⁴, d = 0.48. Study 2 clone deliberation: d = 0.10/0.06/0.01, all n.s.; heterogeneous d = 0.26, p = 0.023.

**Expert dominance:** best single model beats heterogeneous consensus — Study 1 Gemini clone group vs. heterogeneous consensus: z = 4.14, p = 3.4 × 10⁻⁵, d = 0.88 (Gemini alone vs. consensus p = 6.1 × 10⁻⁴, d = 1.31); Study 2 Anthropic alone vs. heterogeneous consensus: z = 3.53, p = 4.2 × 10⁻⁴, d = 0.38; Study 4 Gemini alone vs. deliberative crowd: z = 2.59, p = 0.0095, d = 0.31, and Gemini alone vs. market: z = 0.48, p = 0.63, BF₀₁ = 6.03 (moderate evidence for null). Critically: the dominant family's identity switched across domains (Gemini: Studies 1, 4; Anthropic: Study 2; Study 3 inconclusive, BF₀₁ = 1.11) — "not knowable ex ante."

## 9. Leakage & limitations (adversarial)

1. **Within-group paired tests are stacked by shared signal.** The within-group comparison pairs a group's consensus against the mean of its own three opening answers — but all three came from the same deliberation context in Studies 1–3 only insofar as they talk; the real concern: each model's i1 answers across repetitions are treated as "individuals," yet they're samples from the same posterior-ish distribution with identical training data and prompt. The i1 pool is far less diverse than a human crowd, so the *magnitude* of the crowd-level d ≈ 3–6 effects may not transfer to sources with genuinely independent error structure.
2. **Convergence pressure artifact.** Convergence was near-universal (167/168, 600/600, 299/300, 175/176) under a protocol that *requires* agreement — agents are told to declare agreement and the round limit forces a verdict. Near-total convergence on a consensus-forcing protocol risks being read as information exchange when part of it is compliance with the instruction to agree. The i2-reproduces-consensus results (82/100, 417/447 exact matches) are consistent with anchoring, not learning.
3. **Study 4's "information asymmetry" cuts both ways.** Models had year-old cutoffs and no search — yet matched Polymarket. The authors read this as a conservative estimate. The adversarial reading: the 44-event set with 35/44 favorite-wins is a low-information environment where a prior-only forecaster can look good; the i1 pool was already near-market, so "deliberation reaches market level" is partly "the prior was already decent." GSE cannot conclude deliberation closes an information gap — only that it cleans up aggregation noise.
4. **Expert dominance undermines the headline.** In 3 of 4 domains the best single model beat the deliberative crowd (d = 0.31–1.31). The authors' defense — "the expert isn't knowable ex ante" — is honest but weakens the practical case: the procedure's value is insurance against picking the wrong member, not superiority. If GSE already has a reliably dominant source (e.g., market-implied probabilities on efficient lines), deliberation may add nothing or subtract.
5. **Memorization in Studies 1–3 unaddressed.** NeurIPS 2024 submissions (models trained through mid-2025) were likely in training data — acknowledged as "cannot rule out," mitigated only by author-block removal. Study 2's ground truth is also a noisy human verdict.
6. **Single generation, three small models, no cost accounting.** Whether the effect survives at frontier capability is explicitly open; inference cost of 10-round deliberation at scale is unmeasured. For GSE's slate-scale operation (hundreds of games), literal LLM chat-room deliberation is cost-prohibitive — hence the adaptation to a deterministic protocol (Section 11).
7. **No effect of clone deliberation is expected mechanically.** Three samples of the same distribution talking to each other have no new information to exchange — the diversity "finding" is nearly tautological, and the authors' Bayesian null-confirmations are the least surprising result in the paper.

- **Natural home:** a new module `packages/prediction-engine/src/deliberative-ensemble.ts` composed with existing `consensus.ts` / `brier-ogd-ensemble.ts` / `earned-weight-ensemble.ts` (ensemble aggregation already exists in-engine; this paper adds the *protocol* — independent estimates → consensus-seeking revision rounds → post-discussion revision — on top of the aggregation rule).
- **Diversity requirement ↔ synthetic-fade / multi-market ensemble:** the paper's clone-groups-add-nothing result is the scientific backing for requiring *informationally distinct* sources (engine model ≠ market-implied ≠ public lean) rather than N draws from one model. It strengthens the case against naively ensembling multiple seeds of the same model.
- **Expert dominance ↔ source-reliability / earned-weight:** the paper formalizes the regime where one source is systematically better and aggregation can't beat it. GSE's earned-weight logic should include an expert-dominance detector: if one source beats the deliberative consensus out-of-sample across a rolling window, route around the ensemble (per the paper: the dominant identity is domain-specific — NFL vs. CFB vs. props — so detection must be per-market, not global).
- **Calibration:** Stage i2's anchoring behavior (exact reproduction of consensus) is a warning for any feedback loop that lets revised estimates re-enter training data — treat post-deliberation values as suspect for calibration fitting.
- **CLV:** Study 4's deliberative-crowd ≈ Polymarket result suggests the consensus forecast is a reasonable *neutral prior* for CLV computation alongside market price.

## 11. GSE implementation spec

Build **`deliberative-ensemble.ts`** — a three-stage, deterministic (no LLM chat required) deliberative aggregation protocol over GSE's diverse signal sources. Concrete:

**Inputs (per event, e.g., one NFL game/prop/DFS slate):**
- `sources: Array<{ id: string; family: string; p: number; rationale: string }>` — independent forecasts from ≥3 *informationally distinct* families, e.g.: (a) engine model output, (b) market-implied probability (de-vigged consensus across books), (c) public-lean/synthetic-fade signal. The `family` tag enforces the diversity gate: reject ensembles where all sources share one family (clone-equivalent; paper: d ≈ 0.06–0.14, n.s.).
- `config: { maxRounds: number (default 5); tolerance: number (default 0.01); }`

**Method (deterministic deliberation — no LLM calls on the hot path):**
1. **Stage i1 (independent):** record each source's probability + rationale (rationale already produced by each source's own pipeline; no new inference).
2. **Stage c (consensus rounds, ≤ maxRounds):** iterate rounds; in each round, for each source, compute a revision as an information-weighted move toward the median of the other sources' current estimates, scaled by that source's *earned reliability* (weight source by its historical Brier-skill on the same market type, from the source-reliability/earned-weight machinery): `p_s ← p_s + α·w_s·(median_others − p_s)`, α ∈ (0,1) small (default 0.3). Round stops when max pairwise spread ≤ tolerance (consensus) or maxRounds reached. Consensus output = median of final-round estimates (median, not mean — robust to one outlier source; paper used agreement, median is its deterministic analogue).
3. **Stage i2 (post-deliberation revision):** each source's final value = its own Stage-c final-round estimate (the paper's i2 matched consensus ~90% of the time, so default i2 = consensus, with per-source deviation recorded for dispersion).
4. **Dispersion output:** final-round spread + per-round convergence trajectory; used as an uncertainty signal (non-convergence = flag for manual review / lower stake tier).

**Output shape:**
```ts
interface DeliberativeEnsemble {
  eventId: string;
  stageI1: Array<{ sourceId: string; family: string; p: number }>;
  rounds: number;                 // rounds used
  converged: boolean;             // spread <= tolerance within maxRounds
  consensus: number;              // median of final-round estimates
  perSourceFinal: Array<{ sourceId: string; p: number }>;
  finalSpread: number;            // max - min of final-round estimates
  dispersion: number;             // std of final-round estimates
}
```

**Expert-dominance detector (per market type, rolling window):** track out-of-sample Brier of each source vs. deliberative consensus; if one source's Brier < consensus Brier with paired Wilcoxon p < 0.05 over ≥50 events, emit `expertDominance: { sourceId }` and bypass the ensemble for that market type until the regime breaks (paper: dominance is domain-specific and not knowable ex ante).

## 12. Reproducible test

Backtest on GSE's historical NFL (and separately CFB) picks with recorded per-source forecasts + outcomes, out-of-sample only:

1. For each event, compute the paper's within-group paired comparison: deliberative consensus vs. the simple mean of the same sources' Stage-i1 estimates, scored by Brier/log-loss on realized outcomes.
2. **Assert:** paired Wilcoxon signed-rank p < 0.05 with effect in the deliberation direction, and ≥60% of events show Brier ≤ mean-ensemble Brier.
3. **Assert (diversity control):** re-run with the engine model replaced by two additional seeds of itself (clone condition); the clone ensemble must show *no* improvement over its own i1 mean (paper's null result) — this validates the diversity gate works and the test isn't just finding the protocol's compliance bias.
4. **Assert (cost sanity):** module runs in <50 ms/event with zero network calls on the hot path (deterministic implementation).

## 13. Acceptance/rejection gate

**ADOPT the deliberative-ensemble module into the production aggregation path iff**, on an out-of-sample NFL slate of ≥200 games with ≥3 distinct source families recorded per game: deliberative consensus reduces mean Brier score by **≥1.5%** vs. the existing simple/earned-weight mean ensemble (paired per-game Wilcoxon p < 0.05, same item-level design as the paper), AND the clone-control condition shows <0.5% improvement (confirming diversity is doing the work). If Brier improvement is 0–1.5% or p ≥ 0.05, keep as experimental behind a flag. If negative or the expert-dominance detector fires persistently for the best source family, **REJECT** the ensemble for that market type and keep the dominant source.

## 14. Improvement experiment (GSE-specific extensions)

1. **Rationale-aware revision:** the paper's LLMs exchange natural-language reasons; GSE's numeric sources have structured rationales (injury flags, line-move direction, weather). Extension: make the revision step rationale-conditional — e.g., a source revises toward consensus *only* on the components of its estimate its rationale doesn't cover (injury-sourced edge is non-negotiable; prior-noise component is negotiable). Tests whether the gain comes from correcting shared-noise rather than true information.
2. **Market-as-a-member with adversarial role:** put the de-vigged market price in the deliberation as one member and test whether consensus beats the market-member alone — directly replicating the paper's Study 4 benchmark on NFL data (paper: deliberative crowd ≈ Polymarket; GSE's markets are sharper — the NFL line is a much harder benchmark, so this is the decisive test).
3. **Expert-dominance-aware dynamic routing:** instead of the paper's static ensemble, build the router: per market type (NFL spread / CFB total / player props), a rolling dominance detector switches between "deliberate" and "follow the expert" modes. Props (where the engine may dominate books) vs. NFL sides (where the market dominates) are the natural split to test.
4. **Convergence-dispersion as a staking input:** the paper discards non-convergence; GSE should *use* it — finalSpread/dispersion as an inverse-conviction feature into the Kelly/conviction-tier path. Hypothesis: non-convergent deliberations mark exactly the games where the edge is illusory.
5. **Cheap LLM deliberation on the disagreements only:** for the subset of events where numeric deliberation fails to converge or sources disagree by >10 pp, escalate to a literal LLM chat-room deliberation (the paper's actual protocol, small models, ≤5 rounds) as a diagnostic — cost-bounded because it fires only on ~5–10% of events.

**VERDICT: ADAPT** — Not pure chatbot theater: the paper's transferable contribution is a three-stage deliberative aggregation protocol (independent estimates → consensus-seeking revision → post-deliberation revision) with a proven sports-forecasting result (deliberative crowd ≈ real-money Polymarket on 44 World Cup events) and a hard diversity requirement that maps directly onto GSE's multi-source ensemble design. Adapt it as a deterministic, cost-free protocol over GSE's diverse signal sources with an expert-dominance detector per market type; do not implement literal LLM chat-room deliberation on the hot path.
