## 1. Research question
How should econometric models be evaluated for *decision making* rather than for science? Manski joins Haavelmo (1944, probabilistic structure for policy) with Wald (1939/1945/1950, statistical decision theory) and argues the field's core failure is evaluating "as-if optimization" (specify model → point-estimate parameters → act as if the estimate were true) by performance within the model space instead of by regret across the full state space of feasible states of nature. Applied to two decision problems: prediction and treatment choice, with new computational findings.

## 2. Dataset / schema
No new dataset. Illustrations: (i) Utah juvenile sentencing/recidivism data (Manski & Nagin 1998): males born 1970–1974, convicted before age 16; 11% confined (treatment a), 23% of those with no reoffense in 2 years; 89% non-confinement (treatment b), 41% no reoffense; outcome y=1 if no new offense. (ii) Numerical maximum-regret tables computed by STATA programs (Manski & Tabord-Meehan 2017; Litvin & Manski 2021): Monte Carlo over 5,000 simulated samples per state, grids of 100 (prediction) or 25 (treatment) values per Bernoulli parameter, N=25–100.

## 3. Method / model
Wald's statistical decision theory: planner chooses a statistical decision function (SDF) mapping data → action; evaluate SDFs ex ante by state-dependent risk/regret across the state space S (all feasible states), using criteria like minimax regret (MMR). Key distinction: model space (distributions consistent with the assumed model) vs state space (all distributions deemed feasible — models may be wrong). As-if optimization = plug-in/two-step decision making; the paper's theme: evaluate it by regret across S, not by optimality within the model. Applications: (a) prediction under square loss with missing data: midpoint predictor of the identification region E(y|δ=1)P(δ=1)+½P(δ=0); (b) treatment choice: empirical success (ES) rule (pick treatment with best sample mean) vs MMR rules; fractional allocation z_MMR and deterministic-singleton/AMMR rules; STATA-implemented numerical max-regret computation over user-constrained state spaces.

## 4. Equations & assumptions
State space S, choice set C, welfare w(c,s); SDF c(ψ) over sample space Ψ; sampling distributions (Q_s, s∈S). Decision criteria without data: (1) max_{c∈C} ∫w(c,s)dπ (subjective average welfare); (2) max_{c∈C} min_{s∈S} w(c,s) (maximin); (3) min_{c∈C} max_{s∈S}[max_{d∈C} w(d,s)−w(c,s)] (MMR). With data: (4) max ∫E_s{w[c(ψ),s]}dπ; (5) maximin over E_s{w}; (6) min max over s of oracle gap (MMR). Binary choice: regret = R_{c(∙)s}·|w(a,s)−w(b,s)|, eqs. (7)–(8). Model space S_m replaces S; as-if optimization: c[s(ψ)] ∈ argmax_{c∈C} w[c,s(ψ)], eq. (9). Set-estimate as-if problems (1′)–(3′) act as if state space is S(ψ). Hodges–Lehmann (1950) MMR predictor for [0,1] outcomes: (μ_N√N + ½)/(√N + 1). Missing-data midpoint predictor max regret: ¼[P(δ=1)²/K + P(δ=0)²]. MMR fractional allocation (eq. 10): z_MMR = E[y(b)|δ(b)=1]·p + {1−E[y(a)|δ(a)=1]}(1−p); max regret z_MMR(1−z_MMR) (fractional) vs min(z_MMR,1−z_MMR) (deterministic singleton). Feasible means (11a–b): E[y(a)]∈[E[y(a)|δ(a)=1](1−p), E[y(a)|δ(a)=1](1−p)+p], similarly for b. Regret of deterministic rule: R_cs·|E_s[y(b)]−E_s[y(a)]|. Sample analog z_N (eq. 13); randomized singleton 1[u≤z_N]. Assumptions: outcomes in [0,1]; individualistic treatment response; state space subjectively specified (can bound nonresponse ≤80%, bound distribution differences, etc.); Monte Carlo/grid approximations of max regret are lower bounds on true max regret; model space S_m may or may not intersect S (Box: S_m∩S=∅ means "all models are wrong"; usefulness = high expected welfare across S).

## 5. Features / target
Features: sample data (realized treatments/outcomes with possible missingness). Target: a *decision* (predictor value; treatment allocation), not a parameter estimate. GSE mapping: features = engine probabilities + market odds; decision = bet/no-bet + stake size; welfare = bankroll growth; state space = plausible true-probability worlds (engine miscalibration bounds).

## 6. Validation design
Numerical: maximum regret of candidate SDFs approximated by Monte Carlo (5,000 samples/state) maximized over discretized state-space grids (100 or 25 points per parameter), N=25–100, observability rates 0.1–1.0 (prediction) / p=0.5–0.9 (treatment). No real-data validation beyond the Utah illustration.

## 7. Numerical results / baselines
Prediction: midpoint predictor always beats the sample-average-of-observed-outcomes predictor when all distributions are feasible — max MSE ≈¼ the size when observability ≤0.8, ≈½ at observability 0.9 (Tables 1A vs 2A); identification (bias from missing data) dominates imprecision when observability <0.7 (max MSE ≈ ¼P(δ=0)²); the Panel-B constraint on distribution differences does not change Table 1 entries (max-MSE states satisfy the constraint). Treatment: Utah example — max regret of mandate-confinement (a) = 0.45 vs mandate-no-confinement (b) = 0.55, so (a) minimizes maximum regret, yet the ES rule picks (b) because 0.41>0.23 — as-if optimization selects the MMR-inferior treatment. AMMR rule: max regret invariant in p, rises from ≈0.34 (N=25) to ≈0.40 (N=100) — more data makes the deterministic rule *less* randomized and *more* regrettable, approaching ½ (footnotes: N=1 gives max regret ¼ for all p; N→∞ approaches the deterministic MMR rule with max regret ½); ES rule max regret = max(p,1−p) (known-distribution case and finite-sample case coincide; fn. 10). Appendix Tables (exact maxima): Table 1A — max MSE of midpoint predictor, e.g. N=100: 0.1952 at P(δ=1)=0.1, 0.0620 at 0.5, 0.0025 at 1.0; N=25 at P(δ=1)=0.5: 0.0658. Table 2A — max MSE of average-observed-outcome predictor, e.g. N=100: 0.7779 at P(δ=1)=0.1, 0.2404 at 0.5, 0.0025 at 1.0 (midpoint beats it ≈3–4× at low observability). Tables 3A/3B — AMMR max regret: N=25: 0.3416–0.3469 (unrestricted) / 0.2772–0.3313 (Panel-B constraint); N=100: 0.4011–0.4026 / 0.3402–0.3927; roughly invariant in p. Tables 4A/4B — ES max regret = exactly p (Table 4A: 0.50/0.60/0.70/0.80/0.90 for p=0.5..0.9 at all N); with the Panel-B constraint (Table 4B) at N=100: 0.3381/0.3375/0.3500/0.4000/0.4500. Appendix A.1: Wald's criterion (4) = minimization of Bayes risk; conditional-Bayes equivalence (Fubini) holds only for unconstrained feasible SDFs (Kitagawa & Tetenov 2018, Athey & Wager 2019 counterexamples); Berger (1985) calls minimizing r(π,δ) "bizarre"; Manski rebuts (experimental design requires ex-ante frequentist reasoning; credible priors are often unavailable). A.2: maximin vs MMR differ except when optimal welfare is state-invariant; Savage (1951) called maximin "ultrapessimistic" (ignores trial data — Manski 2004 shows this for treatment choice); Chernoff (1954) IIA-violation critique of MMR dismissed (MMR decisions are generically undominated). All numbers are the paper's claims.

## 9. Leakage & limitations
Adversarial notes: (a) the paper is primarily philosophical/methodological — its "new findings" are narrow (midpoint predictor, binary-treatment MMR rules); (b) maximum regret is computed on coarse grids, hence is a *lower bound* on true max regret (paper's own footnote); (c) the state space is subjective — MMR conclusions inherit whatever bounds the analyst imposes, so "robustness" can be gamed by widening/narrowing S; (d) minimax regret is extremely conservative (optimizes the worst case) — for positive-expectation betting it will systematically under-bet vs Kelly/expected-log-growth; (e) the Utah illustration's punchline (ES picks the wrong treatment) depends entirely on the no-knowledge-of-counterfactuals state space, which is a maximal-ignorance assumption, not a finding; (f) GSE transfer: sports outcomes are not "treatments" and the engine's probabilities are not "missing data" — the mapping is analogical, and the paper gives no betting-specific results.

## 10. GSE overlap
Extension (conceptual). The existing map has staking/Kelly entries and pick-evaluation by backtest PnL, calibration, and CLV — all of which evaluate decision rules *within the model space* (under the engine's own probabilities or realized outcomes). What is NEW: the explicit Wald prescription to evaluate a *betting rule* (threshold + stake sizing as an SDF) by maximum regret across a *state space* of plausible true-probability worlds — e.g., "engine win prob ± calibration error bounds" — rather than by expected value under the point estimate. This directly challenges GSE's as-if optimization habit (plug engine prob into Kelly as if true). The treatment-choice results map onto bet/no-bet: the ES rule analog is "bet if engine edge > 0"; the paper shows such rules can be MMR-inferior. Cite: staking/Kelly dossier entries, pick-evaluation methodology.

## 11. GSE implementation spec
1. **Regret-based staking audit**: define a state space for each bet as {true win prob ∈ [engine prob − ε, engine prob + ε]} with ε from the calibration analysis (e.g., ECE-by-slice bounds); compute worst-case regret (vs oracle Kelly) of current staking rule vs fractional-Kelly alternatives across this state space on 2023–2025 bets. Effort: ~1 week. 2. **Decision-rule scorecard**: add "maximum regret across calibration-error states" alongside EV/ROI/CLV in the pick evaluation dashboard. 3. Do NOT replace Kelly with minimax-regret sizing outright — MMR is worst-case conservative; use it as a risk overlay (cap stakes where max regret exceeds a threshold).

## 12. Reproducible test
Dataset: GSE 2023–2025 posted picks with engine probabilities, odds, outcomes. Construct per-pick state spaces from empirical calibration error (binned |engine prob − realized frequency|). Compare three staking SDFs: (a) full Kelly on engine prob (as-if optimization), (b) half-Kelly, (c) MMR-capped Kelly (stake reduced where worst-case regret across the state space exceeds 2× expected log-growth). Metric: realized log bankroll growth AND maximum regret across the state space (computed ex post). Test protocol: walk-forward by season.

## 13. Acceptance / rejection gate
Adopt the regret-overlay if: on 2024–2025 walk-forward, the MMR-capped rule achieves ≥90% of full-Kelly log growth while cutting worst-case (state-space) regret by ≥30% — i.e., it buys robustness cheaply. Reject if: the robustness costs >15% of log growth (MMR conservatism too expensive for a +EV bettor) or if results are insensitive to the state-space bounds (then the framework adds judgment calls without decision value).

## 14. Improvement experiment
Go beyond the paper: (i) the paper's MMR is worst-case over *all* feasible states — for betting, use *weighted* minimax regret with weights from the engine's own posterior over calibration states (a "soft" Wald criterion between Bayes and minimax), which the paper does not develop; (ii) extend the Litvin–Manski numerical max-regret program from binary treatments to the continuous stake-size decision (fractional Kelly is already continuous — derive the closed-form MMR stake under interval-uncertain win prob, the direct analog of eq. 10); (iii) apply the Dominitz–Manski missing-data midpoint logic to CLV analysis where closing lines are missing (e.g., steam moves), bounding the identification region for true CLV edge.
