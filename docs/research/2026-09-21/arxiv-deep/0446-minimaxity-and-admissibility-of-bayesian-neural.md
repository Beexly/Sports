# [0446] Minimaxity and Admissibility of Bayesian Neural Networks (arXiv:2604.04673v1)

**Citation:** Daniel Andrew Coulson, Martin T. Wells (2026). *Minimaxity and Admissibility of Bayesian Neural Networks*. arXiv:2604.04673v1. URL: https://arxiv.org/abs/2604.04673v1
**Ledger completed:** 2026-09-21. **Read:** full text (main text §§1–6 plus Appendices S1–S4 and references; 57,780 text lines).
**Verdict:** ADAPT — the fixed-scale BNN non-minimaxity mechanism (heavy over-shrinkage of large signals from too-thin prior tails, with depth delaying but not removing the failure) and the BetaPrime hyperprior repair are directly portable to GSE's high-dimensional shrinkage problems: team/player effect estimates and multi-market probability blending where a thin-tailed global prior would over-shrink the biggest true signals; the radial shrinkage rule δ(Y)=(1−E[(1+W)^{−1}|Y])Y is a reimplementable calibrator, not abstract theory.

## 1. Research question
When does a finite-depth, finite-width Bayesian neural network (BNN) with a fixed prior scale induce a Bayes estimator of a multivariate normal mean that is minimax (worst-case risk no worse than the MLE's risk p) and admissible (not strictly dominated), and can a heavy-tailed hyperprior on the network's effective output scale repair the failure? The paper works in the normal location model Y = θ + Z, Z ~ N_p(0, I_p), under quadratic loss — a deliberately simple setting that isolates the tail-behavior mechanism — then extends the minimax/admissibility result to Bayesian predictive densities under KL loss (George–Liang–Xu machinery).

## 2. Dataset / schema
No real-world dataset. Pure theory plus Monte Carlo simulation of the normal location model (§5): dimensions p = 5, 50, 100; signal-magnitude grid r = ||θ|| from 0 to 500; for each grid point, N_mc = 50,000 Monte Carlo samples of Y, repeated over K_dir = 10 independent signal directions, risk averaged across directions. The mixing (variance) distribution is approximated with M_v = 200,000 i.i.d. draws {V_m}_{m=1}^{M_v}. Network configuration for all BNN rules: depth d = 3, hidden widths n_1 = n_2 = 20, layer scales σ_1 = σ_2 = σ_3 = 1. Dropout rule: keep probabilities q_1 = q_2 = 0.8 with inverted dropout (giving V = 6.25∏T_ℓ). Simulations conducted in R [24]. Schema is synthetic by construction — nothing proprietary, fully re-runnable.

## 3. Method / model
A deep ReLU BNN with fixed layer scales induces, on its scalar/vector output, an exact finite mixture of normal scale mixtures: conditional on latent Gamma variables T_ℓ ~ Gamma(k_ℓ/2, 1), the output is Gaussian with effective variance V_k = U·S_k where S_k = ∏_{ℓ=1}^{d−1} T_ℓ and U is a deterministic scale factor (Appendix S1 proves this representation). The fixed-scale Bayes rule is radial: δ_BNN,fixed(Y) = E[V/(1+V) | ||Y||² = ||y||²]·y = (1 − ψ(||y||²))y with ψ(u) = E[1/(1+V) | U = u]. The paper then replaces the fixed scale with a hyperprior on the effective output variance W ~ BetaPrime(1, b), b = p/2 − 2, so that the prior is θ | W ~ N_p(0, W I_p) and the induced Bayes rule is δ_BNN,hyper(Y) = E[W/(1+W) | Y]Y = (1 − E[(1+W)^{−1} | Y])Y. Heavy tails of the BetaPrime let large signals be explained by increased variance rather than forcibly shrunk.

## 4. Equations & assumptions
Stated assumptions: normal location model Y = θ + Z, Z ~ N_p(0, I_p); quadratic loss R(θ,δ) = E_θ||δ − θ||²; ReLU activations; fixed finite depth d ≥ 2 and widths; output dimension p ≥ 3 (non-minimaxity) and p ≥ 5 (hyperprior minimaxity/admissibility, since b = p/2 − 2 > 0 requires p ≥ 5 — "the minimum dimension for the existence of proper minimax Bayes estimators").

Key equations, quoted faithfully:
- Prior predictive stretched-exponential tail bound (S2.3): m(y) ≤ K_5 exp{−κ||y||^{2/d}}. The tail exponent 2/d depends on depth — deeper nets have heavier tails.
- Radial Laplacian: Δu = φ″(r) + ((p−1)/r)φ′(r), r = ||y||.
- Lower bound for superharmonic radial q with q(r) → 0 (S2.2): q(r) ≥ c·r^{2−p}. Combined with the upper bound, √m cannot be superharmonic for p ≥ 3 (S2.4) — the heart of the non-minimaxity result.
- Fixed-scale Bayes rule (S2.5): δ_BNN,fixed(y) = E[V/(1+V) | ||Y||² = ||y||²]·y.
- ψ′(u) = −(1/2)Var(1/(1+V) | U = u) ≤ 0; Stein excess-risk integrand: B(u) = ψ(u)²u − 2pψ(u) − 4uψ′(u), with R(θ,δ) = p + E_θ[B(U)].
- Concentration mechanism (S2.6): with m = d−1 and x_u = (u/(2C))^{1/(m+1)} = (u/(2C))^{1/d}, the posterior of the geometric-mean scale concentrates near x_u, giving ψ(u) ≥ (1/(4c_2))u^{−(1−1/d)}, hence u·ψ(u) ≥ (1/(4c_2))u^{1/d} → ∞ as u → ∞. For u ≥ u_0, B(u) ≥ (p/(2c_2))u^{1/d − 1}, and choosing the event E_θ = {||Z|| ≤ (1/2)||θ||} with a chi-square MGF bound P(E_θ^c) ≤ exp{−||θ||²/16} yields E_θ[B(U)] ≥ (C_*/4)||θ||^{2/d − 2} > 0 for large ||θ||. So sup_θ R(θ,δ) > p: the fixed-scale rule is not minimax.
- Hyperprior construction (Theorem 3.1 / S3.1): W ~ BetaPrime(1, b), h(w) = b(1+w)^{−(b+1)} = b(1+w)^{1−p/2}, b = p/2 − 2. **Discrepancy flagged faithfully:** the main-text prose in §3 says "We choose a BetaPrime(1, p/2−1) prior", but Theorem 3.1's statement and the entire S3.1 proof (b := p/2 − 2; density check; Corollary 3.2 tail exponent a = −(b+1) = 1 − p/2 < −1) use BetaPrime(1, p/2 − 2). The operative construction is p/2 − 2; the "p/2−1" in the prose reads as a typo, since every derived quantity depends on b = p/2 − 2.
- Hyperprior marginal (radial, r = ||y||): m(r) = C·I_0(λ), I_0(λ) = ∫_0^1 u^{p−3} e^{−λu} du, λ = r²/2, C = Nb(2π)^{−p/2} > 0.
- Superharmonicity of √m under the hyperprior: Δ√m ≤ 0 ⟺ 2mΔm ≤ (m′)² ⟺ γ(a,λ)(2γ(a+2,λ) − (a+2)γ(a+1,λ)) ≤ γ(a+1,λ)² with a = p − 2, which reduces (via γ recurrences) to 0 ≤ F(λ) := λ^a e^{−λ} + (2λ − a)γ(a,λ); F′(λ) = λ^a e^{−λ} + 2γ(a,λ) > 0 and F(λ) → 0 as λ → 0, so F > 0 for all λ > 0. Hence √m superharmonic everywhere, and the Bayes rule δ_π(Y) = Y + ∇m(Y)/m(Y) is minimax by Fourdrinier–Strawderman–Wells [11] Thm 3.1 (needs E_θ||∇m(Y)/m(Y)||² ≤ p + ||θ||² < ∞, verified since ||∇m/m|| = ||y||·E[1/(1+W)|Y] ≤ ||y||).
- Admissibility (Corollary 3.2): prior tail h(w) ~ b·w^{−(b+1)} gives exponent a = −(b+1) = 1 − p/2 < −1 for p > 4, so by [11] Thm 3.15 the rule is admissible.
- Predictive density (Theorem 4.1, S4.1): Bayes predictive density p̂_π(y|x) under KL loss is minimax; scaling identity Δ_z √m_π(z;v) = v^{−p/4−1} Δ_w √m_{π^{(v)}}(w;1) preserves superharmonicity for all v > 0, and George–Liang–Xu [14] Thm 1(ii) gives minimaxity. Corollary 4.2: admissible w.r.t. all proper densities (Brown–George–Xu [4]; finiteness of B_KL shown via log⁺/log⁻ split).

## 5. Features / target
Not a feature/target prediction paper. "Input" is the observation vector Y ∈ R^p; "target" is the mean vector θ (quadratic loss) or the future observation density p(y|θ) (KL loss). The network architecture (depth d, widths n_ℓ, scales σ_ℓ, input norm ||x||) enters only through the effective variance factor 2^{d−1}||x||²∏σ_ℓ² and the latent product ∏T_ℓ — the BetaPrime hyperprior absorbs all of it, so individual layer scales never need fine-tuning.

## 6. Validation design
Theory validated by Monte Carlo risk curves (§5), not by train/test splits on real data. Risk R(θ,δ) estimated on a grid of ||θ|| = r ∈ [0, 500]; at each r, N_mc = 50,000 samples of Y, repeated over K_dir = 10 random directions and averaged; mixing distribution from M_v = 200,000 draws of V. Baselines compared: MLE (risk = p, the minimax benchmark), fixed-scale BNN rule δ_BNN,fixed, dropout BNN rule δ_BNN,fixed,dropout, BetaPrime hyperprior rule δ_BNN,hyper, and the horseshoe posterior mean (second simulation, sparsity regimes k ∈ {1,2,5,10,25,50} at p=50 and {1,2,5,10,20,50} at p=100; horseshoe computed per-observation since it is not radial, with a single risk estimate from a 1-sparse reference vector). No time ordering (simulation).

## 7. Numerical results / baselines
All numbers quoted from the paper's §5 prose and captions; the paper reports curves (Figures 1–6), not tables:
- Across Figures 1–3 (p = 5, 50, 100): δ_BNN,hyper "tracks the minimax boundary almost exactly". For p = 5, its risk "begins well below 5 near the origin, rises to the minimax level (up to Monte Carlo error), and then remains essentially flat at that level"; at p = 50 and p = 100 "its risk approaches [p] from below and does not exhibit any systematic exceedance".
- In contrast, δ_BNN,fixed risk "rises above" p. For the dropout version δ_BNN,fixed,dropout "these non-minimax departures are much smaller on the scale of the plots, but the qualitative distinction remains the same: the risk of [δ_BNN,hyper] approaches the constant risk level [p] from below, whereas the risks of [δ_BNN,fixed and δ_BNN,fixed,dropout] can exceed it."
- Horseshoe comparison: horseshoe posterior mean is competitive for sparse configurations, but "increases substantially as k grows and, for sufficiently dense signals, exceeds the minimax benchmark by a wide margin. In particular, in the dense case k = p, the Horseshoe risk increases to approximately 7.5 for p = 5, 66 for p = 50, and 130 for p = 100" (paper's numbers; minimax benchmarks are 5, 50, 100).
- Interpretation (paper's): simulations are consistent with the theory — BetaPrime hyperprior yields a minimax Bayes rule; fixed-scale and dropout-augmented fixed-scale priors do not.

## 9. Leakage & limitations
- No empirical data, so no lookahead/survivorship leakage — but external validity to NFL is the entire question and is untested: the normal location model with identity covariance is a toy setting; real GSE parameter vectors have correlated, heteroskedastic, heavy-tailed noise, and the p ≥ 5 requirement is trivially satisfied but the identity-covariance proof machinery may not transfer.
- The non-minimaxity proof is asymptotic in ||θ|| (failure shows up at large signal norms); for the signal magnitudes GSE actually sees, the fixed-scale rule may be fine — the paper's own risk grid runs to r = 500, far beyond typical standardized effect sizes.
- The "exact finite-width mixture" claim is exact only for the ReLU BNN's induced output prior, not for any trained network — there is no training, no posterior over weights given data in the empirical sense; "BNN" here means "prior induced by random network", which is much weaker than what practitioners mean by Bayesian deep learning.
- Width affects only constants, depth affects the exponent (2/d) — but the paper fixes d = 3, widths 20/20, scales 1 in all simulations, so the depth/width dependence is illustrated theoretically, not empirically stress-tested.
- Horseshoe comparison uses a 1-sparse reference vector for the hyperprior rule's single risk estimate while computing horseshoe risk per-observation — an asymmetric protocol that favors the radial rule's presentation.
- The BetaPrime prose/theorem discrepancy (p/2−1 vs p/2−2) is a red flag for careful reading even though the proof is internally consistent at p/2−2.
- Monte Carlo error is acknowledged ("up to Monte Carlo error") but no standard errors or replication seeds are reported.

## 10. GSE overlap
New capability, no duplication. The existing-research-map's only nearby item is the nflfastR `cp_model` note ("CPOE as Bernoulli residual + shrinkage ... feature list UNVERIFIED") — ordinary empirical shrinkage, unrelated to minimax Bayes theory. GSE has no global-local shrinkage machinery for its high-dimensional parameter estimates (team strength vectors, player effect estimates across markets) and no decision-theoretic calibration of its probability blending. This paper's contribution to GSE is foundational, not a plug-in: it gives the theoretical justification for using heavy-tailed global-scale hyperpriors (horseshoe-class) when shrinking many noisy estimates toward a baseline, plus a concrete radial shrinkage rule with a minimax certificate.

## 11. GSE implementation spec
Concrete, minimal port — a shrinkage calibrator for GSE's per-entity effect estimates:
1. Data: nflverse play-by-play 2020–2025; derive per-team per-season offensive/defensive EPA effects (or per-player CPOE-style residuals) as the noisy observation vector Y ∈ R^p (p = number of teams/players, comfortably ≥ 5).
2. Model: replace the current point estimate with the radial shrinkage rule δ(Y) = (1 − E[(1+W)^{−1}|Y])Y, W ~ BetaPrime(1, p/2 − 2). Implementation: Monte Carlo over W (the paper's own M_v = 200,000-draw protocol) to compute the posterior shrinkage factor per observation; no MCMC needed since the rule is a one-dimensional integral against the marginal m(r) = C∫_0^1 u^{p−3}e^{−r²u/2}du, evaluable by quadrature.
3. Where it plugs in: (a) preseason team-strength priors blended with early-season estimates — the hyperprior's heavy tails let genuinely elite/terrible teams keep their signal instead of being over-shrunk to league average; (b) multi-outcome probability blending for same-game markets — shrink raw model probabilities toward the market consensus with a heavy-tailed scale so sharp disagreements survive.
4. Serving: precompute the shrinkage curve s(r) = E[W/(1+W) | ||Y|| = r] on a grid once per p; inference is a table lookup — microseconds.
5. Effort: ~2–3 days for the quadrature implementation + backtest harness; the theory is fully specified.

## 12. Reproducible test
Dataset: nflverse 2021–2024 regular seasons (train/calibrate through 2023, test on 2024). Metric: mean squared error of preseason team offensive-EPA-per-play estimates vs. end-of-season realized values, plus Brier score of win probabilities derived from them. Baseline: current GSE empirical-Bayes/OLS shrinkage (or plain sample means). Protocol: for each team-season, compute the noisy early-season estimate Y (weeks 1–4), apply δ_BNN,hyper shrinkage toward league average, and score against the full-season realized value; compare MSE and Brier against the baseline on the 2024 test season, paired by team. Runnable entirely from the local nflverse parquet — no new data licensing.

## 13. Acceptance / rejection gate
ADOPT the hyperprior shrinkage rule iff on the 2024 test window it reduces team-EPA MSE by ≥ 3% relative to the baseline point estimate AND does not worsen Brier score on derived win probabilities (paired t-test, α = 0.05, non-inferiority margin 0.001). REJECT (keep current shrinkage) if either condition fails, or if the shrinkage curve's heavy-tail behavior produces any team-season estimate more than 4 standard deviations from the baseline — a sanity cap against hyperprior misbehavior on real, non-Gaussian noise. Gate stated before any run.

## 14. Improvement experiment
Go beyond the paper: the paper's W is a single global scale (all coordinates shrunk by one factor). GSE's team effects are grouped (offense vs defense, or position groups). Try a grouped BetaPrime hyperprior — independent W_g ~ BetaPrime(1, p_g/2 − 2) per group g with a shared top-level tail index — which keeps the paper's minimax certificate within each group (the proof is per-dimension p_g ≥ 5) while letting offense and defense shrink at different rates. Test whether grouped shrinkage beats the single-scale rule on the §12 protocol; the paper's theory suggests the within-group guarantee survives, and real sports data almost certainly has group-heterogeneous signal scales.
