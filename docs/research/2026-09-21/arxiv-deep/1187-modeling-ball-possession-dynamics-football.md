## 1. Research question
What minimal agent-based dynamics explain the statistics of ball-possession intervals (BPIs) in soccer — possession time, pass length, number of passes — and can the model's dynamics be mapped onto a well-understood physical process (Wiener process with drift and an absorbing barrier)?

## 2. Dataset / schema
- L. Pappalardo et al. (2019) "Events" dataset: all spatiotemporal events from the 2017–2018 season of five European leagues (Spain, Italy, England, Germany, France). 3,071,395 events; 625,195 BPIs; 1,826 games; 98 teams; 2,569 players.
- BPI = set of consecutive events generated by the same team; only BPIs with ≥2 events used. Distances normalised per-game to the game's mean distance.
- Empirical regularities used to justify the model: Pass = most common event (1.56M, ~2× Duels); ~75% of Duels trigger possession change; most common BPI involves exactly 2 players (0.27M) and 2 event types (0.4M).

## 3. Method / model
Three-agent minimalist simulator: two teammates + one defender, discrete time steps Δt=1, 2D motion. Step length R drawn from exponential P_a(r) = (1/a)e^(−r/a) with action-radius scale a; direction uniform for teammates, toward-the-ball for the defender. Ball carrier accepts a move only if it increases distance to the defender; free player and defender always accept. Pass attempted with probability p if the defender's action radius does not intercept the teammate-to-teammate line. Boundaries: inner radius R1 (initialisation: agents at distance R1 from centre, 120° apart), outer radius R2 (field edge; forbidden moves rejected). A BPI ends when d(ball, defender) < a. Parameters (p, a, R1, R2) = (0.3, 1, 2.25, 16) chosen by minimising the sum of Jensen–Shannon divergences of the three observable distributions vs data, over 10^5 realisations (a≈2m → R1≈5m, R2≈32m in real units). Theoretical mapping: reduce to 1D distance defender→ball, a random walk with drift μ and absorbing barrier at x_b=a.

## 4. Equations & assumptions
- Fokker–Planck (Eq. 1): (σ²/2)∂²p/∂x² − μ∂p/∂x = ∂p/∂t, with p(d₀,x;0)=δ(x), p(d₀,x_b;t)=0.
- First-passage-time density (Eq. 2): g(τ) = (x_b/√(σ²2πτ³))·exp(−(x_b−μτ)²/(2σ²τ)), linked to P(T).
- Empirical: P(T) ∝ T^(−γ), γ = 5.1 ± 0.1 (authors note too large for a genuine power law); ⟨T⟩ = 13.72 s; ⟨N⟩ = 3.1 passes/BPI; linear ⟨N⟩(T) = ω_p·T with ω_p = 0.19 ± 0.03 (R²=0.99) for 0<T<60s (~0.2 passes/sec). Fit: μ = 0.09 ± 0.02, σ = 0.39 ± 0.03, r² = 0.97; ⟨δ⟩_P(δ) = −0.07 matches drift magnitude. Assumptions: exponential (memoryless) step lengths; neglect field geometry (circular); pass probability constant; defender strategy = always head at ball.

## 5. Features / target
Not applicable in the ML sense: this is a mechanistic physics model. No features/target for prediction; the "targets" are three distributions (P(T), P(Δr,Pass), P(N)) reproduced, not predicted out-of-sample.

## 6. Validation design
Model-simulated distributions compared to the same dataset used to fit parameters, via Jensen–Shannon divergence — descriptive fit, not out-of-sample prediction. No train/test split, no baselines beyond the trivial sample mean, no outcome (match result) prediction at all.

## 7. Numerical results / baselines
- JSD: P(T) DJS = 0.017 (good; mean shifted ≈−20%, misses hump at T≈30s); P(Δr) DJS = 0.008 (very good, captures bimodality, misses long-pass tail from goal kicks/crosses); P(N) DJS = 0.0007 (excellent).
- Failures: model allows unbounded growth of ⟨N⟩ with T whereas data saturates (finite-size effect).
- No match-outcome, spread, or betting results reported.

## 9. Leakage & limitations
- Parameters fit and evaluated on the same single season of data — descriptive, not predictive. Single-season, soccer-only.
- The theoretical mapping assumes constant step δ; the true step distribution is variable.
- No notion of score, match state, team strength differences, or tactics; no link from possession micro-dynamics to match outcomes.
