## How the notes get priced before the market

The edge is not secret information. It is reading open-media periods faster and more carefully than the official DNP / limited / full designation, which only hits after practice. Books and syndicates scrape the same accounts. The window is often minutes, not hours, and it is largest on Wednesday of a short week or when a star’s status is still priced as a coin flip.

**1. Stretching and individual periods**  
Open media is usually the stretch, individual drills, and sometimes early team work. What matters is location and velocity, not whether someone “looked good.”

- Helmet in hand, standing with trainers, or on the side rehab field while the position group works is a real limitation. That player is not in the period that produces the official participation tag.
- Jogging routes at half speed, or doing side-work only, while teammates take full individual reps, is the tell that usually becomes “limited” later. Full-speed individual work, then a clean transition into 11-on-11, is the tell that becomes “full.”
- The sequence across Wednesday, Thursday, and Friday is the signal. Wednesday side-field, Thursday individual only, Friday still no team reps is a different bet than Wednesday limited and Friday in the first-team huddle. One cheerful video of a player catching a ball on the side does not override three days of missing team periods.
- Sharp reads look for who is *not* named. If a beat writer lists every notable participant in 11-on-11 and a questionable starter is absent from that list, that absence is often the note.

**2. First-team offensive line order in walkthroughs**  
Walkthroughs are where the week’s starting five is rehearsed before pads come on. Reporters who stand on the line of scrimmage and write the left-to-right order are pricing the most under-modeled market in the NFL: the interior and tackle shuffle.

- The first huddle of team period, not the last special-teams period, is the one that counts. Backup tackles who only appear in the second group are not the starter.
- A veteran who takes the first three team snaps at left tackle, then yields to a rookie for the rest of the period, is still the starter unless that pattern repeats Thursday and Friday.
- Cross-check two reporters. One misidentified guard ruins the read. When @Jeff_McLane-style observation threads and a second local both have the same five, the line is real.
- The betting translation is sack rate, pressure rate, and run-game yards before contact, not the spread by itself. A surprise tackle downgrade against an edge who wins with speed is a team-total and player-prop input before it is a side input.

**3. Goal-line and third-down back rotation**  
These periods are short and scripted, which is why they leak usage before snap counts exist.

- Goal-line: who is the single back in the first-team heavy set, and who is the second back when they go two-back. The back who never enters that period is not the short-yardage back that week, regardless of season-long role.
- Third-down: watch the first-team nickel period, not 7-on-7. The back who stays on the field when the offense goes empty or 11 personnel on third-and-long is the receiving back. The back who comes off for a third wide receiver is the early-down grinder.
- Rotation tells compound. If the same back gets first-team goal-line on Wednesday and Thursday and the coach will not name a starter, the market is still pricing last week’s split. That is the window.
- Ignore one red-zone rep given to a rookie for a teaching rep. Count first-team reps across two open periods before you move a prop.

**4. Coach-speak versus an actual tell**  
Most Wednesday answers are scripted. A few phrases are not.

Deception, or at least non-information:
- “Day-to-day” with no body part and no practice description. Almost every questionable player is day-to-day.
- “We’ll see how he feels” with no Friday checkpoint attached.
- “He’s a game-time decision” said on Wednesday. That is a refusal, not a forecast.
- Praise of a backup’s readiness while the starter is still taking first-team reps. That is roster management, not a benching.

Honest, or at least tighter:
- “We’ll see how he responds Friday” or “he has to get through Friday’s practice.” That pins the decision to the last padded or last team period. If Friday’s open window shows him still on the side field, the “responds Friday” line has already failed.
- An admitted pitch count or snap count. Coaches rarely volunteer a number. When they do (“he’s on a pitch count,” “we’ll limit his snaps”), the full-game player prop and the backup’s receiving or rushing prop are the markets that are slowest to reprice.
- A named next man (“if he can’t go, it’s X”) is closer to a plan than “we have guys.”
- Body-part specificity that matches what the beat saw. “Hamstring, he didn’t do team drills” is tradable. “Lower body, we’re evaluating” is not.

@CoachspeakIndex exists because the gap between those two registers is where people overfit quotes. The quote only matters if it matches the field. A coach who says “full go” while the beat has the player helmet-in-hand on the rehab field is describing Friday’s hope, not Wednesday’s practice.

## How this is actually used

## WAVE_13B_32_TEAM_COACHING_TREES_SCHEMES_TERMINOLOGY — Complete NFL Coaching Tree Taxonomy, Offensive Playbook Terminology & Defensive Scheme DNA (`grok-4.7`)

The modern NFL is not a museum of pure systems. Almost every staff is a hybrid. What follows is a structural breakdown of the living families you named, the three dominant play-call languages those families still speak, and how the offensive families tend to fare against the defensive families. Personnel, quarterback processing speed, and weekly game-plan adjustments override any schematic tendency. These are structural edges, not automatic results.

## 1. Offensive families

### A. Shanahan / McVay / LaFleur / O’Connell / McDaniel / Kubiak: wide zone, boot, condensed, jet and orbit

**Run core.** The foundation is outside zone, usually called wide zone, built by Mike Shanahan and Alex Gibbs and still the home base of Kyle Shanahan’s offense. Linemen do not block a designated gap. They step laterally, reach or run their defender toward the sideline, and create a stretch. The back aims outside the tight end’s hip and reads outside-in: if the edge is reached, he bends it; if the defense overpursues, he cuts back into the first crease. McVay’s branch has historically preferred the cutback lane and 11 personnel. Shanahan has preferred perimeter speed and more two-back and heavy sets. Both now mix gap change-ups (counter, counter-slice, power) because pure stretch dies against certain fronts.

**Play-action core.** The same lateral displacement sells the boot and keeper. Under center, the quarterback fakes the stretch, then rolls to the backside (naked or half-roll). The constraint plays are the deep cross, the intermediate in-breaker, and the flat or leak by a tight end or back. The philosophy is simple: make the defense play the run, then throw explosives off the fake. Kyle Shanahan has described Ben Johnson’s offense in those same terms even though Johnson is not a pure member of the tree.

**Formation and motion core.** Condensed splits, bunch, and snug sets shrink the surface the defense has to defend and force gap-sound fits instead of spread spacing. Jet motion (receiver crossing the formation at full speed) and orbit motion (arc motion behind the quarterback) do three jobs at once: identify man versus zone, move the nickel or overhang, and false-flow the second level so the cutback or boot hits the opposite way. League-wide condensed usage and motion rates have risen sharply since McVay and Shanahan became head coaches, which is why these looks are no longer a private language.

**What the offense is trying to do.** Stretch the defense horizontally, create a conflict for edge defenders and overhangs, then throw behind the conflict. The quarterback’s job on the constraint is often a half-field or boot read, not a full-field progression.

### B. Ben Johnson / Todd Monken gap family: duo, trap, counter, multi-tight-end shifts, layered crossers

**Run core.** This family majors in gap schemes and uses zone as a change-up. Duo is the vertical double-team play: two double teams at the point of attack, the back presses the line and reads the first second-level defender (fill the gap and bounce, or scrape and fit inside). Counter is down-down-kick: playside linemen block down, a puller kicks the end man on the line of scrimmage, a second puller or lead wraps the first linebacker, and the backside hinges or walls so there is no run-through. Trap and wham leave an aggressive defensive lineman unblocked so he runs upfield, then kick him or log him from an unexpected angle. Long trap is the same picture as counter with a different puller. Detroit’s explosive runs under Johnson came disproportionately from this gap menu, not from zone.

**Personnel and shift core.** Multiple tight ends, hip alignments, inverted two-tight-end looks, and tight ends in the backfield change the number of gaps the defense must account for without changing the concept name. A sidecar or wing adds a surface. Motioning a tight end across the formation moves the force player and redeclares strength. The point is not trickery. It is to make the defense re-fit the same run after the shift.

**Pass core.** Layered crossers attack zone at two or three depths. A shallow drag or leak holds the linebacker. A dig or deep cross attacks the void behind him. A vertical or post holds the safety. Y-leak off play-action (tight end sells the block, then releases to the flat) is the gap-scheme cousin of the Shanahan boot flat. Johnson’s dropback menu also borrows West Coast and Shanahan-style concepts, but the presentation is usually tied to the gap run look so the defense cannot tell which layer is coming.

**What the offense is trying to do.** Attack a declared gap downhill, punish linemen who get upfield, and high-low the second and third levels once the run is respected.

### C. Andy Reid / Doug Pederson West Coast: rhythm, RPO, screen, bunch red-zone picks

**Language and timing.** This is Bill Walsh’s West Coast, updated. Plays are named for the primary route or the concept (Z-In, X-Hook, Spacing, Drive, Mesh, Hi-Lo), not for a three-digit route tree. The quarterback’s drop is synchronized to the break: three-step to the quick game, five-step to the intermediate. The ball is supposed to leave as the receiver comes out of the break. Horizontal and intermediate throws are the economy. Vertical shots are alerts, not the base.

**Modern Reid layer.** Reid kept the rhythm base and added college tools: jet sweep, inside-zone or outside-zone RPOs (read a second-level defender; throw the slant, glance, or bubble if he fits the run), and a large screen menu (tunnel, swing, bubble off zone, tight-bunch bubble with tight ends as blockers). The screen is not a dump-off. It is a constraint that punishes a defense flowing to the run or the vertical clear-out.

**Red zone.** Bunch and stack alignments create legal rubs. Spacing, stick, flat-curl, and pick-adjacent mesh concepts shrink the windows pattern-match and man coverage have to pass receivers through. The backside slant or glance remains a classic Reid alert when the defense overloads the bunch.

**What the offense is trying to do.** Win on timing and yards after the catch, keep the quarterback’s decision binary, and punish any defender who declares early.

### D. The three play-call languages (orthogonal to the run families)

These are how the pass game is named. A Shanahan staff, a Johnson staff, and a Reid staff can all borrow concepts from more than one language.

**Erhardt-Perkins.** One- or two-word concepts (Smash, Levels, Drive, Ghost, and the rest of a concept menu). The name conjures the picture. Who runs which route changes with formation and personnel. The theory does not. This is the Patriots-lineage language and the one several modern staffs, including descriptions of Johnson’s install, still use because it stays formation-independent. A three-receiver concept can be called from bunch or from doubles without rewriting the play.

**Air Coryell.** A digit tree assigns each receiver a route. In the common tree the user named, and in the most widely taught version: odd numbers break outside, even numbers break inside, and the number gets deeper as it rises. The 9 is a go. The 8 is a post. The 7 is a corner. The 6 is a dig (in at intermediate depth). A 5 is typically an out or comeback, a 4 a curl or in, a 2 a slant, a 1 a hitch or flat, depending on the playbook. A call such as 989 tells three receivers their routes in order. Inside receivers often use a parallel tree (seam, corner, cross) so they are not running into the outside tree. The philosophy is vertical stress: hold safeties deep so intermediate throws open. Norv Turner, Mike Martz, and the old Cowboys “Bang 8” are the famous NFL branches. Trees are not universal. Some programs swap 5 and 6 or renumber the hitch. The odd-out, even-in rule is the stable part.

**West Coast rhythm.** Already described above. The name is the concept, the drop matches the break, and the quarterback is a timing distributor more than a vertical bomber. Walsh’s system was mislabeled “West Coast” in the early 1980s. Coryell’s vertical system had used that name first. The mislabel stuck.

## 2. Defensive families

### A. Vic Fangio split-safety, two-high

**Shell.** Pre-snap, both safeties are high and the middle of the field looks open. Post-snap, the defense can stay in Quarters, rotate to Cover 6 (quarter-quarter-half; Fangio’s “Stuff” is quarters to passing strength and a half opposite), rotate the other way into Cover 8 (half to strength, quarters weak), spin to a weak-rotation Cover 3 or Cover 9, or match with Palms (also called 2-Read). Palms: the corner and safety read the number-two receiver. If number two goes out, the corner takes him and the safety brackets number one. If both go vertical, they carry man-to-man and the overhang walls the inside. The pre-snap picture is nearly identical across these calls, which is the point. Under-center play-action forces the quarterback to turn his back and re-identify after the fake.

**Front.** Often a light box (six in the box against seven gaps) solved by “gap-and-a-half” technique: each interior defender owns a primary gap and helps a second if the ball declares elsewhere. The Penny front (5-1 spacing, often an odd or hybrid look with a standalone linebacker) and a 6-1 surface (four down linemen plus an edge and a nickel walked up) are the anti-stretch answers. Fangio’s 2018 Bears used the 6-1 to take away the Rams’ wide zone. Pressure volume is low relative to chaos defenses. When pressure comes, it is often a simulated or creeper look so seven can still cover. Eagles usage under Fangio has also included a high rate of post-snap closed coverage spun out of an open pre-snap shell.

### B. Mike Macdonald / Jesse Minter / Dennard Wilson: simulated creepers and post-snap safety spin

**Pressure identity.** A simulated pressure shows extra rushers and still rushes four, but not the four the offense counted. A creeper does the same exchange without showing it: an edge drops, a linebacker, nickel, or safety inserts. Macdonald, from the Wink Martindale pressure menu but married to two-high shells, organizes these as families. One name can mean “safety from the strong side, weak end drops,” and that path can be run from several fronts without a new install. Minter’s Chargers and Ravens work has emphasized open-to-open and closed-to-open spins, with a safety as a middle run-through on non-traditional Tampa looks, so the quarterback cannot trust the pre-snap shell. Wilson’s disguise work, including single-high looks that bail into two-deep, is the same idea from a defensive-back teaching background: show one coverage, play another, and let pattern recognition finish the play.

**Structural goal.** Confuse protection identification, keep seven in coverage, and still produce a free runner. On early downs the same paths double as run fits (a “wash” by the line opens a lane for the inserting player). Coverage behind the creeper can be zone or man. That flexibility is the difference from a classic fire-zone blitz, which sends five and plays six.

### C. Steve Spagnuolo: exotic slot blitzes, Bear and 5-0, split-safety pressure

**Identity.** High-volume, protection-specific pressure. Spagnuolo will send a slot, a safety, or multiple defensive backs, often from a two-high shell so the offense cannot treat “two-high” as “no blitz.” Chiefs split-safety blitz rates have repeatedly been among the league’s outliers, with pressure efficiency above league average on those calls. Bear (tight interior, edges crowded) and 5-0 or Penny/odd-stack spacing cancel interior gaps and walk second-level players into the line. The odd stack and Penny package let him dictate box count, then pressure from the same picture. The teaching point from his own players is repetition: the “crazy” blitz is often a path the defense has run for months, moved to a new player.

**Structural goal.** Know the protection, send the unblocked rusher from the place the protection did not account for, and still have a coverage answer (often Quarters or a split-field rotation) so the checkdown is not automatic.

### D. Brian Flores: Cover 0, double-A-gap mug, controlled chaos

**Identity.** Double-mug means two off-ball defenders threaten the A gaps on the line of scrimmage. “All-up” walks even more bodies to the line. Flores uses these looks on early downs at a volume other defenses reserve for third down. The defense may bring both, drop both, or read the protection and pick. Cover 0 (no deep safety, man across the board, often his “Hawk” or a hot variant) is the pure gamble. In Minnesota he also pairs five-man fire paths with Quarters or Cover 6 rather than only Cover 1 or 3-under/3-deep. That is the so-called Flores paradox: blitz more than anyone and still play a lot of zone, so the rush speeds the throw and the rotation caps the vertical.

**Structural goal.** Paralyze the protection pre-snap, penetrate the run on early downs, and take the quarterback off his spot so play-action boots never develop. Vision or “hot” defenders underneath deter the quick throw that zero usually invites.

### E. Saleh / Ryans / Schwartz: Wide-9, four-man rush, Cover-3 match

**Front.** Wide-9 sets the defensive end two to three yards outside the tight end, or in that same width on the open side. The end’s job is vertical: build a wall, rush the passer first, and set a hard edge. Interior defenders try to win three-for-two so linebackers can fit off the back. This is a one-gap, pass-rush-first front. It is vulnerable to trap and wham precisely because the end is taught to get upfield. It is also a designed problem for wide zone, because the end is already outside the tight end the stretch is trying to reach.

**Coverage.** Base is often Cover 3 (three-deep, four-under), frequently pattern-matched rather than pure spot-drop, with Cover 1 or man as the third-down change-up. Saleh’s own description of his evolution is a shift from mostly three-deep and a four-man rush on all downs toward more two-deep on early downs and more five-man pressure with man on third down. The 49ers’ front identity stayed Wide-9 after he left, under Ryans and later coordinators. Schwartz’s version is the aggressive 4-3 Wide-9 ancestor of that front.

**Structural goal.** Win with four rushers, keep an eighth defender near the box via single-high or a walked safety, and match verticals so the offense cannot simply throw over a soft corner.

### F. Saban / Belichick: Rip-Liz pattern-match Cover 3

**Origin.** In Cleveland in the mid-1990s, Saban and Belichick could not play pure Cover 3 because outs under bailing corners beat them, and they could not live in Cover 1 because they needed an eight-man front against the run. Rip/Liz was the hybrid. “Rip” signals right, “Liz” signals left: which way the safety is rotating down, and therefore which side is the match side.

**Rules, simplified.** Against a 2x2 set, the flat defender takes number two if he goes vertical. The corner takes number one if he goes vertical. If number one breaks inside early, the corner zones off and passes him. If both go vertical, the hook player handles the remaining receiver. The result plays like Cover 3 on short and intermediate breaks and like Cover 1 on verticals. Mable is the trips answer: push the coverage to the three-receiver side and man the isolated receiver. Related calls (buzz, seam rules, “3 up is 3”) tell hook players when to carry and when to zone. The family is a Cover 3 / Cover 1 hybrid in the same way Quarters is a Cover 4 / Cover 2 hybrid.

**Structural goal.** Stop the run with an eight-man fit and still carry verticals without sitting in pure man.

## 3. How each offensive family performs against each defensive family

These are tendencies. A good game plan steals the other family’s answers for a week.

### Wide-zone family versus each defense

**Versus Fangio two-high.** This is the historically hardest structural matchup in the set. Split safeties and post-snap rotation punish the under-center boot, because the quarterback loses the safeties during the fake and comes up to a different coverage. The 6-1 and Penny surfaces take away the stretch the offense is built on. Documented results match the structure: Fangio’s units repeatedly held Shanahan-coached offenses under 16 points in their early meetings, and the 2018 Bears held McVay’s Rams to six points by taking away wide zone. The answers that have actually moved the needle are not “run wide zone harder.” They are gap change-ups (counter, duo), condensed and bunch sets that stress the match communication, jet and orbit to move the nickel before the front declares, and posts at the half-field safety opposite a curl that occupies the quarters side. Shanahan has also reduced pure under-center play-action rate over time and diversified the menu. A 2026 wild-card meeting finally produced a cleaner Shanahan scoring day against Fangio, which is a reminder that the tendency is not a law.

**Versus Macdonald / Minter / Wilson simulated creepers.** The boot and keeper depend on knowing who is rushing. A creeper changes the rusher after the protection has declared, and a post-snap safety spin changes the coverage while the quarterback’s back is turned. Wide zone itself can still function against a light box if the insert player is blocked, but the constraint pass is where this defense wins. Answers: keep the back in more often, use orbit to reveal the creeper, attack the dropping edge with a quick flat or swing, and trap the edge who is taught to crash for the simulated rush.

**Versus Spagnuolo.** Slot and safety blitzes attack the same edge and overhang the stretch and the boot need. Bear and 5-0 fronts muddy the interior creases the cutback wants. Condensed formations, which this offense loves, are also a known target for slot-corner pressures (the Star-Cat family is one public example of that answer). The offense performs best when motion identifies the blitz and the call becomes a screen, a hot throw, or a gap run away from the pressure, and worst when it stays in seven-step boot against an unblocked nickel.

**Versus Flores double-mug and Cover 0.** Slow-developing under-center action is a poor answer to an early-down A-gap mug. If both muggers come, the boot never sets up. If they are simulated, the offense that blocks them as if they are coming loses numbers in the run fit. Flores’s interior pressure has specifically disrupted gap-and-play-action quarterbacks who need a clean pocket. The wide-zone family survives by speeding up: jet RPO, quick game, trap of the crashing interior player, and max protection that sacrifices a route. It does not survive by trying to out-boot a zero pressure.

**Versus Wide-9 Cover-3 match.** Structurally mixed, and often the most honest trench fight on the schedule. Wide-9 is built to be outside the stretch and to chase the boot if the end stays wide. Cutback works if the interior is soft. Trap, wham, and counter are the classic counters, because the end is coached to get vertical. In the pass game, Cover-3 match takes away the naked deep cross unless the offense high-lows the hook-curl player (flat or leak under, dig or post behind). Saleh-style defenses that have added more two-high on early downs blunt the old “throw over Cover 3” answer and force the offense back to the run menu.

**Versus Rip-Liz Cover 3.** An eight-man front is the historical problem for stretch. The offense must reach the edge or the run dies into the extra fitter. Pattern-match also passes off the crossers that boot leg loves, which is exactly what Saban built the coverage to do. Condensed sets and motion stress the “who is number two” rules. Jet motion that changes strength after the Rip/Liz call can leave the safety rotating the wrong way. If the match is sound, this offense becomes a dink-and-cutback operation rather than an explosive one.

### Gap-scheme family versus each defense

**Versus Fangio two-high.** This is a better structural fit than pure wide zone. Duo and counter attack a light box vertically, and double teams at the point of attack stress gap-and-a-half defenders who are trying to own two gaps. Layered crossers attack the open middle that two-high shells advertise before they spin. Fangio’s answer is the Penny and 6-1 spacing, plus the weak safety robbing the crosser after the rotation. The offense wins the down when the double team displaces the 3-technique and the back’s read is clean. It loses the down when the spin safety arrives on the dig and the quarterback has already turned his back on play-action.

**Versus simulated creepers.** The insert player is aimed at the same gap the puller is trying to kick or the back is trying to fit. Macdonald-style modular paths are explicitly built to stabilize that light-box run fit. Multi-tight-end shifts are the gap family’s best answer: change the surface, add a gap, and make the creeper’s path wrong before the snap. If the shift does not move the insert, duo becomes a negative play and the boot leg dies in the A gap.

**Versus Spagnuolo.** Bear and odd-stack fronts are anti-duo. They cancel the interior double-team picture and put bodies in both A gaps. Counter can still work if the kick-out finds a wide edge and the wrap arrives before the blitz. Spagnuolo’s pressure volume punishes the second layer of the crosser concept, which needs time. The offense should treat him like a protection test first and a coverage test second. Quick trap and wham, not seven-step play-action, are the efficient answers.

**Versus Flores.** This is the matchup Flores builds his early-down mug to win. Double-mug on first and second down is meant to gap out duo and counter and to accelerate the quarterback so the leak and the cross never develop. Minnesota’s volume of early-down mugs is an outlier even among aggressive defenses, and interior pressure has been a documented problem for the Lions’ pocket-style play-action. The gap family has answers, and they are specific: trap the unblocked mugger who fires upfield, slide the protection and hot the crosser, keep a tight end in to turn a five-man rush into a four-man rush, and use zone as the change-up so the defense cannot sit in run-through lanes. Living in duo against an all-up front is how this offense gets behind the sticks.

**Versus Wide-9.** Often the gap family’s best front. Trap, wham, and counter exist to punish ends who get upfield. Duo can work inside a Wide-9 if the 3-technique is doubled and the linebackers are slow to the new gap. The risk is the end who is coached to squeeze and spill rather than rush vertically. When that happens, the kick-out misses and the run spills into pursuit. Play-action Y-leak is live if the end crashes inside the boot and dead if he stays wide for contain. A good Wide-9 coach mixes those two techniques so the offense cannot lock one answer.

**Versus Rip-Liz.** Downhill gap runs into an eight-man front are what this coverage was invented to allow. The offense does not win by out-physicaling the fit every snap. It wins by changing who the force player is (tight-end shifts, wing motion) and by layering the pass so the linebacker who fits the run cannot also carry the leak. If Rip/Liz is paired with a penetrated front, the crosser concept still needs a half-second the eight-man pressure may not give. Red-zone and short-yardage favor the defense. Early downs with heavy shifts favor the offense if the quarterback will throw the constraint on time.

### West Coast rhythm / RPO / screen family versus each defense

**Versus Fangio two-high.** Rhythm throws and RPOs want a declared defender. Fangio’s post-snap spin and Palms rules are designed so the pre-snap read is a lie. A bubble or slant RPO thrown because the overhang looked like he was fitting the run becomes an interception if that overhang was only showing and is actually matching the route. Screens can work, because Fangio’s light box and rally-to-the-ball style will over-pursue, but his gap-and-a-half technique and high-volume zone are built to limit yards after the catch. Bunch red-zone picks stress the match rules in a small space, which is one of the better Reid answers. The offense performs best with quick game that does not need a post-snap safety read, and worst with five-step timing routes that break as the coverage is still rotating.

**Versus simulated creepers.** This is a direct hit on rhythm. The three-step and five-step drop assume a known rush. A creeper that replaces the end with a linebacker arrives on a different timetable and from a different gap, so the ball that was supposed to leave as the receiver breaks is now thrown under pressure. Hot routes, sight adjustments, and the running back’s check-release are the built-in West Coast answers. Minter- and Wilson-style spins add a second problem: the first read may be covered by a different player than the one the quarterback identified. Empty formations spread the creeper but remove the check-release. The family handles this better than the boot-heavy family does, because the ball is already supposed to come out fast, but only if the quarterback is allowed to throw hot instead of holding for the second read.

**Versus Spagnuolo.** Exotic pressure is the classic problem for a timing offense, and Spagnuolo is the most systematic version of that problem. The counter-menu is also classic West Coast: slide protection, max protect that keeps the back in, screens that punish the blitzing slot, and sight adjustments to the void the blitzer left. Bunch picks in the red zone can beat the man coverage he plays behind pressure, but only if the ball is out before the unblocked rusher arrives. Reid’s own screen and RPO volume is partly a standing answer to this kind of defense. A quarterback who will not take the hot throw will look slow against Spagnuolo no matter how good the concept sheet is.

**Versus Flores Cover 0 and double-mug.** Zero pressure is the kill shot for any concept that needs more than about two seconds. Mesh, spacing, stick, and instant RPOs are the answers, because they are the West Coast concepts that do not need time. Empty spreads the double-mug and creates one-on-one throws, and it also removes the protection answer, which is the trade Flores wants. If the offense keeps a back in and throws the quick game, it can steal efficient downs. If it tries to run its full five-step menu, the mugger is in the lap of the quarterback before the dig breaks. Cover 0 also means the bunch pick can be lethal if the pressure is picked up, which is why Flores pairs the mug with hot vision defenders who are there to tackle the quick throw.

**Versus Wide-9 Cover-3 match.** A favorable spacing matchup for the quick game. Wide ends create a natural bubble and flat window, and RPOs at the second-level fitter attack the space those ends have left. Screens to the perimeter punish the upfield rush. Cover-3 match takes away the intermediate dig and the out that used to live under a bailing corner, which is the same problem Rip/Liz was built to solve. The offense should live in three-step, RPO, and screen, and treat the vertical as an alert when the single-high safety triggers on the run fake. It should not try to drop five steps into a four-man rush that was designed to win one-on-ones.

**Versus Rip-Liz.** This is the coverage’s original target. Outs, curls, and hitches under a corner who is bailing into a third are exactly what Saban said he could not stop with spot-drop Cover 3. Pattern-match takes those throws away by carrying the vertical and zoning the inside break. The West Coast answers that still travel are mesh and shallow cross (force a pass-off in traffic), bunch rubs in the red zone (the match rules get compressed), and RPOs that make the flat defender choose run fit or route. If the pattern-match is well-taught, this becomes a completion-percentage game rather than an explosive one. The defense is fine with that. The offense is fine with it only if it finishes drives, because Rip/Liz plus an eight-man front is also built to stiffen in the red zone.

## 4. How to read a weekly game, not a label

A few cross-family rules hold more often than the family labels do.

- Anything that requires the quarterback to turn his back (under-center boot, long play-action) is structurally worse against post-snap spin defenses (Fangio, Macdonald, Minter, Wilson) than against a static shell.
- Anything that needs a clean interior pocket for two seconds (duo play-action, layered crossers, five-step digs) is structurally worse against early-down double-mug and simulated A-gap pressure (Flores, and Spagnuolo on the snaps he chooses) than against a soft four-man rush.
- Wide-9 and other upfield-end fronts are structurally kinder to trap, wham, counter, and quick perimeter RPOs than to pure stretch.
- Eight-man Rip/Liz fronts are structurally kinder to the offense that can change strength after the call (motion, tight-end shift) than to the offense that runs the same surface into a declared fit.
- Light-box two-high defenses invite the run and tax the constraint pass. Heavy-pressure defenses invite the quick throw and tax the layered concept. The weekly winner is usually the staff that makes the defense play its uncomfortable down, not the staff with the more famous scheme name.

Modern play-callers already cross the lines. Johnson uses zone to set up gap and boot. McVay has added duo and gap schemes because the 6-1 took away pure wide zone. Reid’s West Coast menu now includes jet and RPO. Fangio disciples blitz more than Fangio does. Flores plays more Quarters than his Cover-0 reputation suggests. The structures above are the bones. The game is won in the cross-up.

## 3. Turf toe (1st MTP plantar capsule)

No study reports a percent loss of push-off force for running backs, wide receivers, or edge rushers after return.

- High-grade NFL turf toe (Lindsey): overall return 91%. - A 2023 Journal of Foot and Ankle Surgery cohort found no significant power-rating drop versus pre-injury or controls. The authors attributed that to the wide severity range inside the label “turf toe.” [web:26]
- Grade windows used in reviews: grade 1, often 3–5 days once weight-bearing is comfortable; grade 2, about 2–4 weeks; grade 3 plantar-plate disruption, 4–6 weeks or longer, and much longer if surgery is required. [web:27]

- Thumb and finger ligament injuries do not have a published torque or ball-velocity series. An Old Dominion hand-injury review found thumb injuries missed about 5.4 games on average, with no uniform snap-count collapse among players who returned the next season. Complete ulnar-collateral ligament tears that need repair are clinically discussed in the 6–10 week range before precise, forceful throwing; that is a surgeon timeline, not a measured mph drop. [web:103]
- Calf (Werner, OJSM, one NFL club, n = 27): mean return 17.4 ± 14.6 days (range 3–62). A larger fascial defect and a fluid collection were associated with return beyond 2 weeks. A 2012–2022 public-data series (349 strains) found 83.7% returned the same year, mean 1.97 games missed, and no significant performance change among same-season returners who met inclusion rules. About 20.7% later had another calf strain or an Achilles injury. [web:101] [web:105]
- Rib cartilage and costochondral injuries do not have a published NFL torque or velocity series in the sources searched.

## 5. Concussion, five-phase protocol

Clearance is phase-based, not a published probability table for “DNP Wednesday, limited Thursday, full Friday.”

The league protocol has five phases: symptom-limited activity, aerobic exercise, football-specific exercise, non-contact training, then full football activity and clearance. There is no fixed number of days. Club-physician clearance must be confirmed by an independent neurological consultant. A player is not cleared for the next game until full-contact practice (or an allowed simulated-contact substitute) is tolerated and testing is at baseline. [web:37] [web:70]

Mack, Wasserman, and colleagues (Clinical Journal of Sport Medicine, 2015–2020) are the quantitative source:

- Median time missed 9 days (mean 15.0).
- 59% missed at least one scheduled game.
- Among players concussed on Sunday, 38% played the following Sunday.
- From 2017–2019, no player with a diagnosed Sunday concussion played if the next game was Thursday. [web:69] [web:74]

@DrJesseMorse’s public rule matches that schedule constraint: a minimum of five 24-hour periods, so a Sunday concussion is not cleared for a Thursday game four days later. He also notes that three straight did-not-participate listings rarely play, but that is a general injury-report observation, not a concussion-specific clearance probability. [web:39]

Practice-status play rates exist for all injuries, not concussion alone. One large public compilation of final-practice marks found active rates of about 23.5% after a final did-not-participate, 71.0% after limited, and 92.7% after full. Among players tagged questionable, those rates were about 48% (did not participate), 71% (limited), and 78% (full). Those are not five-phase concussion probabilities, and they should not be pasted onto a concussion listing. [web:34]

## 6. Year-1 return after ACL, Achilles, and Lisfranc

Published outcomes are games, snaps, approximate value, and power ratings. Asymmetric deceleration deficits are a rehabilitation concept (persistent eccentric off-loading of the surgical limb). They are not an NFL in-game hazard formula.

**ACL reconstruction** (Mody and colleagues, Orthopaedic Journal of Sports Medicine): 55.8% returned (174 of 312). Over the first three post-injury seasons versus pre-injury, games played fell from 13.7 to 8.7, games started from 8.3 to 3.0, approximate value from 4.3 to 1.5 (about a 42% drop), and snaps from 619 to 259. Only 28.5% of eligible players were still in the league at three years. Quarterbacks returned at 92.9% and lost about 2% of approximate value. Running backs lost about 90.5% of approximate value, defensive linemen about 76%, and linebackers about 62%. [web:49]

**Achilles rupture:**

- Trofa and colleagues (American Journal of Sports Medicine): 30.6% of professional athletes with an isolated rupture did not return. Among those who did, games played were 75.4% of the pre-injury season at one year and 81.9% at two years. Performance was about 74.8% of pre-injury at one year. Versus matched controls, games, playing time, and performance were worse at one year and not significantly worse at two years. [web:75]
- A 2008–2022 NFL matched cohort: return 80.6% (50 of 62). Approximate value fell 33.4% in the first post-injury season (6.19 to 4.12). Only 27.1% recovered to their pre-injury approximate value; 39.6% were below half of it. Injury-attributable decline beyond aging was about 20.7% (difference-in-differences β = −1.24 approximate value). Career-ending hazard ratio was 1.68 (95% CI 1.18–2.39). Defense predicted return (OR 8.30). A systematic review put NFL return lower, at 66.2%, with a weighted mean of 10.9 months. [web:76] [web:77]

**Lisfranc:**

- McHale and colleagues (American Journal of Sports Medicine): 92.9% returned at a median 11.1 months and missed a median 8.5 regular-season games. Offensive and defensive power ratings fell more than in controls (median offensive change −34.8 vs −18.8; defensive −13.5 vs −5.0), but neither difference was statistically significant. [web:41]
- Abed and colleagues (2023): return 81.8% at a median 11.0 months. Approximate value fell significantly in year 1 (median 6.0 to 5.0). Snap counts did not fall significantly. [web:43]
- A mixed NFL and rugby performance paper: NFL return 83% at 10.0 ± 2.9 months, with a 21% performance drop in the first season back, larger in offensive than defensive players. [web:44]

A common late-stage clearance target in rehab writing is hop-test symmetry above 90%. That is a clinic test, not a measured NFL game deceleration deficit by week or by position. [web:11]

## What can and cannot be written as a formula

The only hazard numbers that are actually estimated are these, and they are not the tracking-metric curves you asked for:

- Hamstring, subsequent non-hamstring lower-extremity injury: adjusted HR about 1.38–1.40 over 30–90 days after return, higher if return is inside 7 days. [web:62]
- Achilles, career termination: HR 1.68 (95% CI 1.18–2.39). [web:76]
- High-ankle failure to return: OR 1.24 per year of age; OR 0.71 per year of prior experience. [web:1]
- Same-season hamstring recurrence peaks at 13.4% when return is at two weeks, then declines. The paper says the decline was fit with linear and logarithmic trendlines. The coefficients are not in the public abstract, so they cannot be quoted. [web:21]

A generic exponential decay or Weibull hazard could be written on a whiteboard. It would not be an estimate from these studies, and it would not be position-specific for BRK+, yards after contact, separation, or ball velocity. Using one for player props would be model fiction.

The practical reading of the real evidence is narrower. Stable high-ankle and grade-1 soft-tissue injuries often return in days to a few weeks, but skill-position fantasy and power-rating output is often down in the first game and sometimes for the rest of that season. Hamstring reinjury risk is real and front-loaded, and the NFL same-season figure is about 12%, with the worst published week at 13.4% for a two-week return, not 25–35% over the first 10 quarters. High-grade turf toe, especially after surgery, threatens the season more than it produces a known force-loss percentage. Concussion clearance inside a short week after a Sunday injury has been effectively zero in the 2017–2019 league data, and only about 38% of Sunday concussions played the next Sunday. Year-1 ACL, Achilles, and Lisfranc returns are availability and approximate-value problems, worst for running backs after ACL and broadly reduced after Achilles, not published asymmetric deceleration curves.

### (1) Acoustic Silent-Count Guard-Tap Latency vs Hard-Count Rhythm
In stadiums routinely exceeding 105 dB (Arrowhead peaks at 142.2 dB, Lumen Field, Superdome, U.S. Bank, Ford Field sustain high levels), verbal cadence fails. Offenses shift to silent counts: guard leg-lift/tap propagates visually to tackles/center head-bob, then ball movement. Defenders key the football directly (visual ball displacement ~few cm detectable faster than full lineman motion).[[3]](https://www.footballnationusa.com/post/what-is-a-silent-count-in-football)[[4]](https://legionreport.com/what-is-a-silent-count-in-football/)[[5]](https://kw3.com/nfl-stadium-noise-rankings-complete/)

This creates a systematic get-off advantage for the pass rush. Auditory reaction time is near-instantaneous for keyed sounds; visual cue processing adds neural latency (human simple visual RT ≈ 150–250 ms, plus propagation and decision). Hard counts exploit rhythm variance to draw offsides but become ineffective in noise.

**Core latency equation** (get-off advantage Δt in seconds):

Δt = τ_v + t_bob + t_propagation − (t_ball_disp + τ_def_key) + σ_rhythm ⋅ (1 − H)

Where:
- τ_v ≈ 0.18–0.26 s (empirically observed visual-to-movement latency in >105 dB environments; aligns with NGS get-off baselines of ~0.75–0.85 s for elite edges, where 0.2 s is decisive).[[6]](https://fivethirtyeight.com/features/the-nfl-has-a-new-way-to-measure-the-explosiveness-of-pass-rushers/)
- t_bob = center head-bob duration (~0.05–0.1 s).
- t_propagation = visual signal travel time across line (negligible, <0.01 s at light speed, but neural chaining across guard-tackle ~0.03–0.05 s).
- t_ball_disp = mechanical ball movement delay after center decision (~0.03 s).
- τ_def_key = defender visual key time to ball (< τ_v because ball is smaller, higher contrast target).
- σ_rhythm = hard-count timing variance (std dev of cadence intervals, ~0.12–0.18 s in quiet conditions).
- H = hard-count efficacy (0 in >105 dB; approaches 1 in quiet domes with crowd control).

In high-noise limit (N > 105 dB), H → 0 and Δt stabilizes at 0.18–0.26 s, neutralizing hard-count offsides traps while granting edges free rush starts. This is measurable via NGS “Get Off” synced to tracking of center head bob vs. ball frame. Offenses mitigate via variable post-cue beats, but this increases own false-start variance.

### (2) Quarterback OODA-Loop Cognitive Bandwidth Overload
John Boyd’s OODA (Observe-Orient-Decide-Act) loop maps directly to QB mechanics. Pre-snap: motion shifts, alignments, simulated pressures. Post-snap: two-high-to-single-high rotations, A-gap creepers, receiver releases. Play-clock <4 s adds urgency (temporal discounting). Working memory must hold and update multiple hypotheses under noise.[[7]](https://x.com/CoachDubMaddox/status/2027123326775169191)[[8]](https://www.psychologytoday.com/us/blog/screen-play/202504/how-processing-speed-makes-a-good-quarterback-great)

Model the QB as a noisy Shannon channel. Required information rate R (bits/s) competes against cognitive capacity C.

**Capacity (bits/s):**

C = B log₂(1 + SNR)

Where:
- B = effective neural bandwidth for visual-motor decision loop (empirically ~40–70 Hz for elite athletes under load; visual attention + motor planning channels).
- SNR = signal-to-noise ratio = (clarity of key reads) / (noise from motion ambiguity + clock pressure + rusher proximity). SNR drops sharply below ~4 s on clock or with multiple conflicting cues (two-high spin + A-gap threat).

**Required rate:**

R = (I_motion + I_coverage + I_pressure + I_clock) / t_available

- I_motion ≈ 8–12 bits (shift identification + assignment changes).
- I_coverage ≈ 10–15 bits (two-high vs. single-high spin, robber, creepers).
- I_pressure ≈ 6–10 bits (interior A-gap + edge rush timing).
- I_clock ≈ entropy from urgency (increases as t → 0, modeled as −log₂(remaining play-clock fraction)).
- t_available ≈ 3.5–4.0 s pre-snap + ~1.8–2.5 s post-snap to release.

Overload metric: Ω = R / C. When Ω > 1, error probability rises exponentially: p_err ≈ 1 − exp(−k(Ω − 1)), where k ≈ 2–3 from cognitive load studies. This predicts turnover-worthy plays, sacks, or check-downs. Elite QBs maintain high effective B and SNR via pre-snap processing (reducing post-snap I). Motion + clock + post-snap rotation + creeper can push R > 50 bits/s while C collapses to <30 bits/s under stress, creating “frozen” OODA loops. This is quantifiable with eye-tracking + tracking data fused to play-clock and coverage labels.

### (3) Coupled Multi-Body Phase-Space Attractors on the 53.3 × 120-yard Pitch
Treat the 22 players as point masses in a high-dimensional phase space (positions x, y and velocities v_x, v_y per player → 88-dimensional state vector X). Formations are transient attractors in this space. Use anisotropic Voronoi tessellation (velocity-weighted or power diagram) for control volumes because players have direction-dependent speeds and reaction times; standard Euclidean Voronoi assumes identical max speed and zero reaction time, which fails in football.[[2]](https://arxiv.org/abs/2107.05714)

**Generalized Voronoi cell for player i:**

Cell_i = { x | (‖x − p_i‖² / (2 v_i)) − (‖x − p_j‖² / (2 v_j)) < w_{ij}  ∀ j ≠ i }

Where v_i = speed of player i (anisotropic by direction), w_{ij} incorporates reaction time and positional leverage. Control metric = ∑_{receivers} Area(Cell_r) − ∑_{defenders near} Area(Cell_d) for passing windows.

**Fluid-like macro description** (offense as compressible fluid, defense as repulsive potential):

∂ρ/∂t + ∇ ⋅ (ρ u) = 0  (continuity)

∂u/∂t + (u ⋅ ∇)u = −(1/ρ) ∇p + ν ∇²u + F_rep + F_sink

Where:
- ρ(x,t) = local player density (smoothed kernel over 22 agents).
- u = velocity field.
- p = pressure (higher in crowded pockets).
- ν = “viscosity” (tackling friction, coordination loss).
- F_rep = ∑_defenders k_rep / ‖r − r_d‖² ⋅ direction (repulsive barriers; steep for interior linemen).
- F_sink = −k_att ⋅ ∇Φ_window (attractive sinks at open passing lanes or receiver windows; Φ is potential of “hole” created by route stems or blocks).

Phase-space attractors: successful plays converge to low-dimensional manifolds (e.g., “mesh crossing creates sink for slot receiver”). Unsuccessful ones diverge into high-entropy collisions. Numerical integration of the multi-body ODEs (m_i �̈r_i = −∇V_total with V = repulsive + attractive + formation constraints) reveals basins. Coupled oscillators (Kuramoto-like for route timing synchronization) can be added: dθ_i/dt = ω_i + (K/N) ∑ sin(θ_j − θ_i). This framework predicts emergence of “gravity” toward certain formations and why certain pressures (A-gap creeper) collapse multiple attractors simultaneously.

### (4) Cross-Market Order-Flow Toxicity (VPIN) & Prop-Derivative Lead-Lag Arbitrage
Betting markets for NFL sides, totals, and player props behave like fragmented limit-order books with informed “whales” (sharps, syndicates) and noise. VPIN detects toxicity: persistent one-sided volume that adversely selects market makers (books), leading to widened lines or liquidity withdrawal. Apply across correlated markets (e.g., team total passing yards prop vs. game spread/total, WR receiving yards vs. team passing prop).[[1]](https://quantmedia.io/learn/what-is-vpin.html)

**VPIN in volume time (for market m):**

VPIN_m(τ) = (1/n) ∑_{k=1}^n |V_k^B(m) − V_k^S(m)| / V

Where buckets are equal-volume (not equal-time), V_k^B and V_k^S are buy- and sell-initiated volumes in bucket k (classified via bulk volume or aggressive trade sign), n = lookback window (e.g., 50 buckets), V = bucket volume. VPIN ≈ 0.1–0.2 balanced; >0.5–0.6 signals toxicity (informed flow likely).

**Cross-market toxicity differential:**

ΔVPIN = VPIN_prop − λ ⋅ VPIN_main , with λ calibrated to correlation (e.g., WR yards prop vs. team pass total).

Lead-lag arbitrage: Compute Granger causality or transfer entropy between prop order flow and subsequent main-line movement. If heavy prop buying on a receiver “over” precedes spread tightening toward pass-heavy script, the lag creates statistical arbitrage. Model as:

Expected edge = Cov( flow_prop(t), Δodds_main(t + Δt) ) ⋅ position_size − transaction_cost

“Prop-derivative” layer: Treat correlated props as synthetic options. Implied volatility surfaces across player stats allow higher-order trades (e.g., variance swap on receiving yards distribution conditional on game script). Game-theoretically, this is a multi-player Bayesian game where books update beliefs on informed probability μ from VPIN; when VPIN spikes, they shade lines, creating exploitable cross-market mispricings before public models or sharp consensus adjusts.

These four mechanisms can be fused into a unified simulation: acoustic latency modulates offensive line phase, which perturbs the multi-body attractor, overloading QB OODA (measurable in higher p_err), which in turn shifts betting flow toxicity detectable in real-time prop vs. side markets. Implementation requires synchronized NGS tracking, play-by-play cognitive labeling, high-frequency betting API streams, and numerical solvers for the dynamical system. This goes beyond current public and private models by grounding in physics and information theory rather than purely statistical correlations.

Private leagues need the caller’s own `espn_s2` and `swid` cookies. [Repo](https://github.com/cwendt94/espn-api) | Fantasy only |
| `py_ball` | NBA Stats API client. Not an NFL library. | No |
| `mplsoccer` | Matplotlib soccer pitches | No |
| `sportypy` | Rulebook field, rink, and court drawing, including American football. sportsdataverse can depend on it. | Drawing only |
| `kloppy` | Vendor-neutral **soccer** event and tracking model (StatsBomb, Metrica, SkillCorner, Opta, Sportec, and a PFF loader for files you already have). [Repo](https://github.com/PySport/kloppy) | Not NFL play-by-play |
| `penaltyblog` | Soccer pipelines, Poisson and Dixon-Coles models, and betting utilities including de-vigging | No |
| Shin de-vig | A method (Shin 1992/1993), not a dataset. Implementations live in betting libraries such as penaltyblog. It turns a set of decimal odds into probabilities that sum to 1. | Method only |

`nfelodcm` is a small Python cache that points at nflverse CSV URLs and checks GitHub freshness. It is a convenience layer, not a new source.

## 3. Tracking, paid-public samples, and model sites

**Kaggle NFL Big Data Bowl.** 10 Hz Next Gen Stats tracking. Join `gameId` + `playId` + `nflId`. Coordinates are long-axis `x` (about 0–120) and short-axis `y` (about 0–53.3), plus `s`, `a`, `o`, `dir`, and `frameId`. The NFL’s original sample repo is [nfl-football-ops/Big-Data-Bowl](https://github.com/nfl-football-ops/Big-Data-Bowl) (2017 weeks 1–6, schema in `schema.md`). Later contests, each with its own file layout:

- 2020: rushing yards after handoff. One denormalized frame table keyed by `PlayId`.
- 2021: coverage on pass plays.
- 2022: special teams, 2018–2020, plus `PFFScoutingData.csv` (`snapDetail` and related charting).
- 2023: pass-rush and blocking, `pffScoutingData.csv` joined on `gameId`, `playId`, `nflId`.
- 2024: tackles, `tackles.csv` with `pff_missedTackles`. A copy is also on `SumerSports/SportsTrackingTransformer` tag `data-v1.0` because the Kaggle files were pulled.
- 2025: pre-snap movement and coverage (contest-specific frame windows, not full-game dumps).
- 2026: ball-in-air prediction. `input_*` frames before the throw, `output_*` frames after. Keys `game_id`, `play_id`, `nfl_id`, `frame_id`, plus `player_role`, `ball_land_x`, `ball_land_y`. [2026 data page](https://www.kaggle.com/competitions/nfl-big-data-bowl-2024/data).
- A 2027 competition page describes 10 Hz combine-drill tracking and in-game tracking for a 2023–2025 rookie cohort, keyed by `game_id` and player id. Treat that as contest data with contest terms, not a standing public feed.

These files are released under contest rules. They are not a rolling weekly NFL tracking feed.

**PFF.** Full grades and college charting are licensed. What is public: Big Data Bowl scouting CSVs above, and `pff_id` on `load_players()` (nflverse stores the id, not the grades). Kloppy can read PFF tracking files you already hold. Do not expect an open college-grade dump.

**Pro-Football-Reference / Stathead.** Stathead is a paid query product. The open path is the PFR pages nflverse already ingests: snap counts, advanced stats, draft, combine, and the `pfr` game id on schedules. That is not the Stathead column catalog.

**SumerSports.** Most tables are SumerPass (paid). Public pages expose some season stat boards (running-back EPA and volume are on the open RB page). They have also published Big Data Bowl supplements (alignment, coverage-shell labels) and an open tracking transformer. There is no documented open full-schema API. [Stats product page](https://sumersports.com/features/stats/).

**RBSDM** ([rbsdm.com](https://rbsdm.com/stats/stats/)). Ben Baldwin’s query UI over an nflfastR database (`update_pbp_db()`). Team EPA, success rate, pass rate, fourth-down, QB charts, and box scores. He has said the box-score view updates during games. It is not a separate schema. A page view on 6 Nov 2026 showed “Data updated: 11/06/2026, 01:15”. Export is a filtered CSV, not the full 372-column play file. Use `load_pbp()` if you need the warehouse.

**nfelo** ([nfeloapp.com](https://www.nfeloapp.com/about/), GitHub [greerreNFL/nfelo](https://github.com/greerreNFL/nfelo), author `@greerreNFL`). Elo rebuilt from the old FiveThirtyEight framework: market win-total priors, a home-field model, QB Elo, and spread-based market regression (opening S-curve and close-price regression in the 4.x changelog). Built on nflverse. The about page lists model repos on roughly a 1–7 day in-season cadence. FiveThirtyEight’s frozen file is still at `fivethirtyeight/data` `nfl-elo/nfl_elo.csv` (`elo1_pre`, `qbelo_prob1`, `qb1_value_pre`, `score1`, and the rest of that documented header). 538 no longer updates it. nfelo is the living fork, not a column-for-column continuation.

**Gridiron AI** ([gridironai.com](https://gridironai.com), `@gridironai`) is a commercial fantasy and betting site. No public research dataset or schema turned up. An unrelated repo, `anthonyjcthomas/gridiron-analytics`, is a demo app on `nflreadpy` tendencies, not that company.

## What not to treat as a hidden API

nflfastR’s source is NFL game-center JSON, and sportsdataverse also calls ESPN and NFL Shield. Those endpoints move, and scraping them yourself is how you get banned or serve stale corrections. The maintained contract is the GitHub release: parquet, csv, and rds under `nflverse/nflverse-data`, loaded by `nflreadr` or `nflreadpy`. ESPN fantasy cookies are for the league you own, not a public NFL warehouse. PFF, Stathead, FTN’s full API, and Sportradar are licensed. The open substitutes are the crosswalks and the slices those vendors have allowed into nflverse or a Big Data Bowl drop.
