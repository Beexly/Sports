Sinkhorn algorithm
Sinkhorn solves the entropy-regularized optimal transport problem. You are given a cost matrix CCC and two marginal distributions rrr and ccc. The algorithm finds a transport plan PPP that moves mass from rrr to ccc while minimizing expected cost plus an entropy penalty that keeps every entry of PPP strictly positive.
The iteration is simple matrix scaling:
u←r⊘(Kv),v←c⊘(K⊤u)u \leftarrow r \oslash (Kv),\qquad v \leftarrow c \oslash (K^\top u)u←r⊘(Kv),v←c⊘(K⊤u)
where K=exp⁡(−C/ε)K = \exp(-C/\varepsilon)K=exp(−C/ε) and ⊘\oslash⊘ is element-wise division. After convergence the plan is recovered as P=diag⁡(u) K diag⁡(v)P = \operatorname{diag}(u)\,K\,\operatorname{diag}(v)P=diag(u)Kdiag(v).
In the GSE injury overlay, rrr is the sealed pre-injury target-share vector, ccc is the post-injury vector with the injured player’s mass removed, and CCC is the sealed scheme interchangeability matrix. The resulting plan becomes the new execution weights. Because τ\tauτ and the other coach parameters never enter the iteration, the reallocation cannot change them.
Deeper details
* The entropy term −εH(P)-\varepsilon H(P)−εH(P) makes the problem strictly convex, so a unique solution exists for any ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0.
* Larger ε\varepsilonε produces a smoother, more uniform plan; smaller ε\varepsilonε concentrates mass on the lowest-cost pairs and approaches the unregularized optimal transport solution.
* The algorithm is globally convergent for any positive kernel. In practice a few dozen iterations suffice for the precision needed by target-share reallocation.
* After the plan is obtained, the column sums of PPP are the feasible post-injury shares. Those shares are then multiplied by the sealed EPA dropoff factors before they are passed to the game model.
* The only quantities that change are the execution weights. assertTauSealed confirms that the recovered τ\tauτ is bit-identical before and after the overlay.

Simpler terms
You have two lists of numbers that must add up to the same total. One list is the target shares before the injury. The other is the target shares after the injury (the injured player’s share is gone). You also have a table that says how expensive it is to move share from one player to another.
Sinkhorn repeatedly multiplies and divides these lists until the new shares match both the “before” totals and the “after” totals while keeping the total cost as low as possible. The entropy term just keeps every player from being completely zeroed out, so the solution stays smooth. When it finishes you have a new set of execution weights that respect the injury but do not change any sealed coach parameter.
Entropy-regularized optimal transport
The problem is
min⁡P≥0⟨C,P⟩−εH(P)subject toP1=r, P⊤1=c,\min_{P \ge 0} \langle C, P \rangle - \varepsilon H(P) \quad\text{subject to}\quad P\mathbf{1}=r,\ P^\top\mathbf{1}=c,P≥0min​⟨C,P⟩−εH(P)subject toP1=r, P⊤1=c,
where H(P)=−∑ijPij(log⁡Pij−1)H(P)=-\sum_{ij}P_{ij}(\log P_{ij}-1)H(P)=−∑ij​Pij​(logPij​−1) is the entropy of the plan. The first term is the expected cost of moving mass according to PPP. import math


def sinkhorn(r, c, C, eps=0.05, max_iter=200, tol=1e-6):
    """
    Entropy-regularized optimal transport.
    r = source marginal (pre-injury shares)
    c = target marginal (post-injury shares)
    C = cost matrix
    Returns the transport plan P.
    """
    n, m = len(r), len(c)
    K = [[math.exp(-C[i][j] / eps) for j in range(m)] for i in range(n)]
    u = [1.0 / n] * n
    v = [1.0 / m] * m
    for _ in range(max_iter):
        u_new = [r[i] / max(sum(K[i][j] * v[j] for j in range(m)), 1e-12)
                 for i in range(n)]
        v_new = [c[j] / max(sum(K[i][j] * u_new[i] for i in range(n)), 1e-12)
                 for j in range(m)]
        if (max(abs(u_new[i] - u[i]) for i in range(n)) < tol and
            max(abs(v_new[j] - v[j]) for j in range(m)) < tol):
            u, v = u_new, v_new
            break
        u, v = u_new, v_new
    P = [[u[i] * K[i][j] * v[j] for j in range(m)] for i in range(n)]
    return P

Sinkhorn algorithm
Sinkhorn finds a transport plan that moves mass from a source distribution to a target distribution while keeping the cost low and every entry of the plan strictly positive.
You are given:
* a source marginal rrr (for example, the pre-injury target shares),
* a target marginal ccc (the post-injury shares, with the injured player’s mass removed),
* a cost matrix CCC that says how expensive it is to move share from one player to another.
The algorithm repeatedly scales two vectors uuu and vvv so that the plan
P=diag⁡(u) exp⁡(−C/ε) diag⁡(v)P = \operatorname{diag}(u)\,\exp(-C/\varepsilon)\,\operatorname{diag}(v)P=diag(u)exp(−C/ε)diag(v)
has row sums equal to rrr and column sums equal to ccc. The parameter ε\varepsilonε controls smoothness: larger values spread the mass more evenly; smaller values concentrate it on the cheapest pairs.
When the iteration converges, the column sums of PPP are the new feasible shares. In the GSE injury overlay those shares are then multiplied by the sealed EPA dropoff factors. The sealed coach parameters, including τ\tauτ, never enter the iteration, so they cannot change.

Sinkhorn in layman terms
Imagine you have a pile of targets that used to belong to several players. One player gets hurt, so his targets have to be given to the others. You also have a chart that says how awkward it is to give a particular player’s targets to another player (same role is easy, different role is hard).
Sinkhorn is the procedure that hands out those targets so that:
* every remaining player ends up with a fair amount,
* the total number of targets stays the same,
* the awkward hand-offs are kept as small as possible,
* no player is completely left out.
It does this by repeatedly adjusting two simple lists of numbers until both the “before” totals and the “after” totals match. When it finishes, you have a new set of target shares that respect the injury. The coach’s original risk preference stays exactly the same; only the target shares change.

Math behind Sinkhorn
Sinkhorn solves the entropy-regularized optimal transport problem
min⁡P≥0⟨C,P⟩−εH(P)subject toP1=r, P⊤1=c,\min_{P \ge 0} \langle C, P \rangle - \varepsilon H(P) \quad\text{subject to}\quad P\mathbf{1}=r,\ P^\top\mathbf{1}=c,P≥0min​⟨C,P⟩−εH(P)subject toP1=r, P⊤1=c,
where H(P)=−∑ijPij(log⁡Pij−1)H(P)=-\sum_{ij}P_{ij}(\log P_{ij}-1)H(P)=−∑ij​Pij​(logPij​−1) is the entropy of the plan, CCC is the cost matrix, rrr is the source marginal, and ccc is the target marginal. The first term is expected cost. The second term keeps every entry of PPP strictly positive and makes the problem strictly convex, so a unique solution exists for any ε>0\varepsilon > 0ε>0.
The dual is unconstrained. Its first-order conditions produce the fixed-point iteration
u←r⊘(Kv),v←c⊘(K⊤u),u \leftarrow r \oslash (Kv),\qquad v \leftarrow c \oslash (K^\top u),u←r⊘(Kv),v←c⊘(K⊤u),
with kernel K=exp⁡(−C/ε)K=\exp(-C/\varepsilon)K=exp(−C/ε). After convergence the primal plan is
P=diag⁡(u) K diag⁡(v).P=\operatorname{diag}(u)\,K\,\operatorname{diag}(v).P=diag(u)Kdiag(v).
Algorithm step by step
1. Build the kernel K=exp⁡(−C/ε)K=\exp(-C/\varepsilon)K=exp(−C/ε).
2. Initialize scaling vectors uuu and vvv to uniform values.
3. Repeat until the change in uuu and vvv is smaller than a tolerance:
   * u←r⊘(Kv)u \leftarrow r \oslash (Kv)u←r⊘(Kv)
   * v←c⊘(K⊤u)v \leftarrow c \oslash (K^\top u)v←c⊘(K⊤u)
4. Recover the plan P=diag⁡(u) K diag⁡(v)P=\operatorname{diag}(u)\,K\,\operatorname{diag}(v)P=diag(u)Kdiag(v).
5. The column sums of PPP are the new feasible shares.
Difference from other methods
Unregularized optimal transport can place all mass on a few cheapest pairs and can be non-unique. Linear programming solvers find that solution but are slower and produce sparse plans that can zero out players. Network-flow or auction algorithms have the same sparsity. Sinkhorn adds the entropy term so the plan stays strictly positive, the iteration is only matrix-vector products, and the solution is unique and smooth. In the injury overlay that smoothness is exactly what is wanted: every healthy player keeps a positive share, and the sealed coach parameters never enter the iteration.

function sinkhorn(r::Vector{Float64}, c::Vector{Float64}, C::Matrix{Float64};
                  eps::Float64=0.05, max_iter::Int=200, tol::Float64=1e-6)
    n, m = length(r), length(c)
    K = exp.(-C ./ eps)
    u = fill(1.0 / n, n)
    v = fill(1.0 / m, m)
    for _ in 1:max_iter
        u_new = r ./ max.(K * v, 1e-12)
        v_new = c ./ max.(K' * u_new, 1e-12)
        if maximum(abs.(u_new - u)) < tol && maximum(abs.(v_new - v)) < tol
            u, v = u_new, v_new
            break
        end
        u, v = u_new, v_new
    end
    return Diagonal(u) * K * Diagonal(v)
end

Divergence properties
The Sinkhorn divergence is the entropy-regularized transport cost between two distributions. It is symmetric, non-negative, and zero only when the two distributions are identical (up to the regularization). Unlike the unregularized Wasserstein distance, it is differentiable everywhere and can be computed by the matrix-scaling iteration above. Larger ε\varepsilonε makes the divergence smoother and more uniform; smaller ε\varepsilonε makes it closer to the true Wasserstein distance but can slow convergence. In the injury overlay the divergence is used only as the objective that the scaling iteration minimizes; the sealed coach parameters never enter it.

Sinkhorn normalization math
The transport plan must satisfy two marginal constraints:
P1=r,P⊤1=c.P\mathbf{1}=r,\qquad P^\top\mathbf{1}=c.P1=r,P⊤1=c.
Sinkhorn enforces them by left- and right-multiplying the kernel K=exp⁡(−C/ε)K=\exp(-C/\varepsilon)K=exp(−C/ε) with positive diagonal scaling matrices. Equivalently, it maintains two vectors uuu and vvv such that
P=diag⁡(u) K diag⁡(v).P=\operatorname{diag}(u)\,K\,\operatorname{diag}(v).P=diag(u)Kdiag(v).
Substituting into the row-sum constraint gives
u⊙(Kv)=r,u\odot(Kv)=r,u⊙(Kv)=r,
where ⊙\odot⊙ is element-wise multiplication. Solving for the new scaling vector yields the normalization step
u←r⊘(Kv).u\leftarrow r\oslash(Kv).u←r⊘(Kv).
The same substitution into the column-sum constraint yields
v←c⊘(K⊤u).v\leftarrow c\oslash(K^\top u).v←c⊘(K⊤u).
Each step is therefore a simple normalization: the current matrix-vector product is divided element-wise into the desired marginal so that the next plan exactly matches that marginal. Alternating the two normalizations drives both residual vectors to zero. Because the kernel is strictly positive, the iteration is well-defined and converges to the unique entropy-regularized solution.

Sinkhorn normalization code
The normalization steps are the two lines that update the scaling vectors:
u_new = [r[i] / max(sum(K[i][j] * v[j] for j in range(m)), 1e-12)
         for i in range(n)]
v_new = [c[j] / max(sum(K[i][j] * u_new[i] for i in range(n)), 1e-12)
         for j in range(m)]
Each line computes a matrix-vector product with the current scaling vector, then divides the desired marginal element-wise by that product. The max(..., 1e-12) guard prevents division by zero. After these two updates the plan formed by the new uuu and vvv matches the row and column marginals more closely than before.
Convergence proof (sketch)
The entropy-regularized problem is strictly convex, so a unique minimizer exists. The dual is unconstrained and concave. The Sinkhorn iteration is coordinate ascent on the dual: each normalization step maximizes the dual with respect to one scaling vector while holding the other fixed. Because the dual is strictly concave and the kernel is strictly positive, the dual objective increases at every step and is bounded above by the primal value. Therefore the sequence of dual objectives converges, the scaling vectors converge to a unique fixed point (up to scaling), and the recovered plan converges to the unique primal optimum. The marginal residuals go to zero at a linear rate that depends on ε\varepsilonε.

Convergence proof in more detail
The entropy-regularized problem is strictly convex in PPP because the entropy term is strictly concave and is subtracted. A strictly convex continuous function on a compact convex set attains a unique minimum, so a unique optimal plan P∗P^*P∗ exists.
The Lagrangian dual is an unconstrained concave function of the two scaling vectors (or, equivalently, of their logarithms). Sinkhorn is coordinate ascent on that dual: the uuu-update maximizes the dual with respect to uuu while holding vvv fixed, and the vvv-update does the opposite. Each coordinate step therefore raises (or leaves unchanged) the dual objective.
Because the dual is concave and bounded above by the primal value, the sequence of dual objectives converges to some finite limit. The kernel is strictly positive, so the dual is strictly concave. A strictly concave function has a unique maximizer. Consequently the sequence of scaling vectors converges to that unique maximizer (unique up to the usual scaling invariance u←αuu\leftarrow\alpha uu←αu, v←v/αv\leftarrow v/\alphav←v/α).
At the fixed point the marginal residuals are exactly zero, so the recovered plan is feasible. By strong duality the plan is optimal. The linear convergence rate follows from the fact that the dual Hessian is negative definite in a neighborhood of the optimum; the contraction factor depends on ε\varepsilonε and on the smallest entry of KKK.

Sinkhorn iterations
Each iteration consists of two normalization steps that alternately enforce the row and column marginals.
1. Row normalization. Compute the current column-weighted kernel-vector product KvKvKv. Divide the source marginal rrr element-wise by that product to obtain the new scaling vector uuu. This forces the row sums of the plan to match rrr.
2. Column normalization. Compute the current row-weighted kernel-vector product K⊤uK^\top uK⊤u. Divide the target marginal ccc element-wise by that product to obtain the new scaling vector vvv. This forces the column sums of the plan to match ccc.
After both updates the plan formed by the new uuu and vvv satisfies the two marginal constraints more closely than before. Because the kernel is strictly positive and the dual is strictly concave, each iteration strictly improves the dual objective (until the fixed point is reached). The residuals on both marginals decrease, and the scaling vectors converge to the unique optimum of the entropy-regularized problem.
In practice the loop stops when the change in uuu and vvv falls below a small tolerance or a maximum iteration count is hit. The final plan is then recovered as Pij=uiKijvjP_{ij}=u_i K_{ij} v_jPij​=ui​Kij​vj​.

Sinkhorn algorithm details
Sinkhorn finds that unique plan by repeatedly normalizing two scaling vectors so the row sums match the source shares and the column sums match the target shares. Each iteration is just a matrix-vector product followed by an element-wise division. Because the kernel is strictly positive, the iteration is well-defined and converges to the unique optimum of the regularized problem. The sealed coach parameters never enter the iteration, so they cannot change.

Scaling vectors
uuu and vvv are positive vectors that left- and right-multiply the kernel. uuu is adjusted so the row sums of the plan match the source marginal rrr. vvv is adjusted so the column sums match the target marginal ccc. Sinkhorn algorithm convergence theory
The entropy-regularized problem is strictly convex, so a unique optimal plan exists. The dual is unconstrained and strictly concave. Sinkhorn is coordinate ascent on that dual: each update of uuu (respectively vvv) maximizes the dual with respect to that vector while holding the other fixed. Because the dual is strictly concave and bounded above, the dual objective increases at every step and converges. The scaling vectors therefore converge to the unique dual maximizer (unique up to scaling). At the fixed point the marginal residuals are zero, so the recovered plan is feasible and, by strong duality, optimal. Convergence is linear; the rate depends on ε\varepsilonε and on the smallest entry of the kernel.

Sinkhorn algorithm in simple terms
