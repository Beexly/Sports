        <p>The Seahawks sent plenty of mixed signals this week regarding the question of whether running back Zach Charbonnet would play on Sunday. On Friday, the decision came.</p><p>He will not play.</p><p>Charbonnet, who suffered a torn ACL in the divisional-round win over the 49ers in January, returned to practice on October 1. He was moved to the active roster this week, and he fully participated in practice on Wednesday, Thursday, and Friday.</p><p>“Zach is going to have a full week’s worth of reps, so that’s exciting," coach Mike Macdonald told reporters on Monday. "There is a possibility that you can see him on Sunday in some capacity, so that is exciting.”</p><p>Speaking to reporters on Friday, Macdonald's explanation created the impression that Charbonnet not playing on Sunday was the plan all along.</p><p>"We’re going along with the plan," Macdonald said. "Plan was to get a great week’s worth of work and see where we’re at. We’re going to take it into next week. It’s part of the plan. Zach’s doing great and we’ll go into next week and see how it goes.”</p><p>The situation doesn't violate the injury-reporting rules, even if the Seahawks arguably created the impression Charbonnet would play. </p><p>Actually, the Seahawks could have milked the uncertainty for two more days. Charbonnet could have been listed as questionable on Friday, with the decision announced on Sunday that he won't be among the active players.</p><p>Still, it feels odd. Especially since Ian Rapoport of ESPNFL said on Friday's <i>Pat McAfee Show</i> that Rapoport expects Charbonnet to "play and play a lot" on Sunday night.     <div class="RichTextArticleBody RichTextBody">
        <p>Entering Week 7, three teams are undefeated. If three teams exit Week 7 undefeated, it will be a rarity.</p><p>The 49ers take their 6-0 record to Atlanta. The 5-0 Chiefs face the Seahawks in Seattle. The 5-0 Vikings play the Colts in Minneapolis.</p><p>Via NBC Sports research, only twice since the merger have three or more teams been undefeated through seven weeks of a season (with the exception of the 1982 strike year).</p><p>In 2009, three teams were unbeaten through seven weeks: the Saints, Colts, and Broncos. In 2015, five teams were perfect through seven weeks: the Panthers, Packers, Broncos, Patriots, and Bengals.</p><p>On both occasions, two of the unbeaten teams through seven weeks made it to the Super Bowl. In 2009, it was the Colts and the Saints. In 2015, the Panthers and the Broncos played in the Super Bowl.</p><p>The 49ers last went 7-0 in 2019. That year, they made it to the Super Bowl, losing to the Chiefs. The Chiefs last went 6-0 in 2024, when they made it to the Super Bowl for the third straight year, losing to the Eagles. The Vikings last went 6-0 in 2009. They lost to the Saints in the NFC Championship.</p>

    <div class="RichTextArticleBody RichTextBody">
        <p>Given the recent talk about the medical care provided to NFL players (I'll now wonder whether any "calf" injury is actually a blood clot), more attention will be paid to whether the practices match the principles that were stridently articulated earlier this week by chief medical officer Allen Sills.</p><p>"The notion that there's external factors that any of our physicians would put a player in harm's way -- that's nonsense," Sills said, among other things, in defending the handling of Eagles quarterback Jalen Hurts in London nearly two weeks ago.</p><p>The realities of professional football introduce plenty of external factors that make the situation necessarily and inherently different than the usual patient-care scenario. </p><p>Last night, for example, Bears running back D'Andre Swift took a helmet to the knee while being tackled during the first quarter against the Patriots. Swift immediately was in pain. He got up and limped off the field.</p><p>Not long after that, Prime Video cameras provided a closeup view of Swift's knee. The impact had ripped a large hole in his pants, and blood was plainly visible on his skin. While he wasn't bleeding profusely, Swift was definitely bleeding.</p><p>Prime Video sideline reporter Kaylee Hartung explained that Swift had received no medical attention on the sideline.</p><p>That's unusual, to say the least. Cuts are addressed, in order to stop and/or conceal the bleeding. The player's blood becomes a potential biohazard for others. The exposed wound puts the player at risk of external contamination.</p><p>In this specific case, the doctors on the sideline provided no treatment to Swift. While it's possible he refused it, the disposition of the player-patient is one of the obvious "external factors" that can compromise care. The patient, in that setting, can at times be uncooperative.</p><p>Sills opened the door to this kind of scrutiny, through his insistence that no football factors (such as the player wanting to play, the team wanting him to be available, and the team and the league hoping to justify the decision not to revise the injury report, which included no suggestion that Hurts possibly wouldn't play) never influence any medical decisions. </p><p>We all know that the context makes the medical care given to NFL players different. The still-unanswered questions regarding the treatment and advice given to Hurts flows from the basic recognition that a doctor who is disconnected from the shared urgency to play should have final say over whether the player will be permitted to take the field with a potentially serious health condition.</p><p>Those questions shouldn't be shouted down or ignored.
