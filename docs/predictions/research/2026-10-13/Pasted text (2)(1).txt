# The Positive Case for Data Centers

## The honest framing

A persuasive defense of data centers starts by admitting the obvious: they do use substantial electricity and, depending on climate and cooling design, can use meaningful amounts of water. In the United States, Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory’s update for DOE estimates data-center electricity use rose from about 58 terawatt-hours in 2014 to 176 terawatt-hours in 2023, or 4.4% of total U.S. electricity consumption, and could reach 325 to 580 terawatt-hours by 2028. At the global level, the International Energy Agency says data centers account for about 1% of electricity generation today and could rise to about 3% by 2030 in its base case. citeturn0search5turn9view1turn41view0

But the anti-data-center narrative usually stops too early. The IEA also says that, with the right mix of policy and infrastructure investment, rising data-center demand does **not necessarily** increase electricity prices, and it reports that proven AI applications can reduce energy costs in energy-intensive industries by 3 to 10 percentage points. DOE has framed near-term data-center load growth as an opportunity to accelerate clean-energy buildout, improve demand flexibility, and modernize the grid while maintaining affordability. In other words, the real question is not whether data centers consume resources; it is whether a project converts those resources into public value while internalizing its costs instead of offloading them onto neighbors. citeturn41view1turn27view0turn12view0

## Jobs and workforce

If you want to make the workforce case honestly, the key is to distinguish **construction-phase employment** from **steady-state operating employment**. Virginia’s Joint Legislative Audit and Review Commission found that data centers provide positive economic benefits mostly during their initial construction. JLARC also reported that a typical 250,000-square-foot data center may have around 50 full-time workers, but a single building usually takes 12 to 18 months to construct and can have roughly 1,500 workers on site at peak from multiple construction trades. That same JLARC review estimated the Virginia data-center industry contributes 74,000 jobs, $5.5 billion in labor income, and $9.1 billion in GDP annually. That is a strong pro-data-center argument, but it is strongest when phrased as “large construction and supply-chain engines, plus smaller but high-skill operating staffs,” not as if every facility becomes a giant long-term employer on the scale of a large factory. citeturn9view0turn10view0

Operator-specific studies show what that can look like on the ground. Google’s Texas data-center impact study estimated that its Texas data centers supported about 4,690 annual jobs and contributed about $601 million annually to Texas GDP during 2021–2023, with job support spanning construction, engineering, networking, security, utilities, and services. Google’s Finland impact study reported about 3,590 annual jobs supported and about €430 million in annual GDP contribution during 2021–2023. These are company studies, but they matter because they capture the broader ecosystem around a hyperscale campus: electrical contractors, mechanical trades, renewable-energy projects, service firms, security, logistics, fiber, and local suppliers. citeturn32view0turn15view0

The deeper workforce upside comes when a campus is paired with a training pipeline. Google says its Workforce Development and STAR programs work with nonprofits and community colleges to prepare local and underinvested talent for careers in construction, carpentry, mechanical, electrical, and fiber-optic work. Microsoft’s Datacenter Academy works with local vocational schools and community colleges on curriculum alignment, scholarships, simulation labs, mentorship, and work experience. Amazon says its AWS Grow Our Talent program places entry-level and nontraditional candidates into data-center technician and operations roles with on-the-job training. Microsoft’s 2026 Community-First AI Infrastructure plan goes further, saying new data centers “typically” create thousands of construction jobs and hundreds of operations jobs, and it points to Washington state, where more than 1,300 skilled trades workers are building Microsoft data centers and more than 650 full-time employees and contractors are expected across operational facilities by the end of next year. citeturn18view0turn18view1turn18view2turn18view3turn24view2

This is where the most compelling workforce message lives: data centers are not just server rooms, they are also a labor market for electricians, pipefitters, welders, HVAC technicians, controls specialists, fiber crews, facility technicians, network staff, and technical operations teams. If a project sponsors apprenticeships, simulation labs, and community-college pathways, it becomes much easier to argue that the benefits are local and durable rather than purely corporate. citeturn18view2turn24view2turn20view1

## Communities and public finance

The community impact story is often stronger on **local public finance** than most critics acknowledge. JLARC found that, in five Virginia localities with relatively mature data-center markets, revenue from data centers ranged from less than 1% to 31% of total local revenue. It also found that localities with data centers can collect substantial revenue primarily from real property and business personal property taxes. That matters because taxes are what turn a private project into upgraded roads, school capacity, parks, libraries, and local services. citeturn9view0

Loudoun County is the clearest example. Loudoun’s official economic-development fact sheet says data-center taxable real property reached $42.4 billion for the 2025 assessment, equal to 75% of the county’s total assessed commercial real-estate value. It reports that actual FY24 data-center tax revenues totaled $875 million, which was $35 million more than the Loudoun County government’s general operations budget, and that data-center revenue is expected to exceed $1 billion while using less than 3% of the county’s land. The same fact sheet says Loudoun receives $26 in tax revenue for every $1 it spends on public services for data centers. Whether or not another county replicates Loudoun’s exact scale, the basic point is powerful: in some jurisdictions, data centers are large recurring tax engines, not just short-lived construction events. citeturn9view3turn10view2

Operators are also increasingly trying to formalize the “good neighbor” argument. Microsoft’s 2026 Community-First AI Infrastructure framework explicitly says it wants data centers to add to the tax base for local hospitals, schools, parks, and libraries while investing in local AI training and nonprofits. Google’s Texas data-center impact study said the company invested about $1.4 million in communities around its Texas data centers in 2022 and 2023, including funding for public libraries, digital literacy, and school STEM programs. Meta’s Community Action Grants program provides direct funding to schools, nonprofits, and community organizations in places where it has data centers, with a focus on community benefit, sustainable communities, and STEAM education. citeturn24view3turn20view1turn32view0turn20view2

The introspective version of this argument should add one more sentence: **these benefits depend on the deal structure**. JLARC notes that some localities lower business-personal-property tax rates to attract data centers, which also reduces the revenue they can collect. So the strongest defense is not “every data center is great for public finance”; it is “a transparent, well-negotiated project can materially strengthen the public tax base.” That distinction makes the positive case more credible. citeturn9view0

## Environmental upside depends on design

The environmental case becomes strong when you move from “a data center” in the abstract to **specific design choices**. Efficiency gains are not theoretical. Google’s Finland impact study reports 98% 24/7 carbon-free energy and an average PUE of 1.09 in 2023, versus an industry-average PUE of 1.58; its Texas impact study reports 79% 24/7 carbon-free energy and a 1.13 PUE versus the same 1.58 benchmark. Google’s 2024 environmental report says it signed contracts for about four gigawatts of clean-energy generation capacity and replenished an estimated 1 billion gallons of water, equal to 18% of its 2023 freshwater consumption. Microsoft says it has contracted 34 gigawatts of new renewable energy across 24 countries, while Meta says its supported wind and solar projects are adding almost 29 gigawatts of clean and renewable energy globally. The broad pattern is that leading operators are not merely buying electricity; they are increasingly acting as anchor customers for new clean generation and more efficient compute infrastructure. citeturn34view0turn32view0turn14view0turn39view1turn39view2

Water is where design innovation is becoming especially visible. Microsoft says its direct-to-chip cooling can save more than 125 million liters of water per facility each year, and that all new Microsoft data-center designs adopted since August 2024 use next-generation cooling intended to make zero-water evaporation the primary cooling method across its owned portfolio. Microsoft also says that in Quincy, Washington, its water-reuse utility partnership reduced the company’s potable-water use in the region by 97% and provides 1.5 million cubic meters of water annually for community drinking-water needs. AWS says it was 53% of the way toward water positive in 2024, already uses recycled water at 24 data centers globally, and expects expansion of water recycling to preserve more than 530 million gallons of drinking-water supply annually in U.S. communities by 2030. Meta says its water-restoration projects returned more than 1.6 billion gallons in 2024, and its 2024 water-restoration work followed more than 30 projects across nine watersheds. citeturn14view2turn39view1turn23view0turn23view1turn39view2

Waste heat is one of the clearest ways a data center can become a **community asset** rather than only a private facility. Google’s Hamina project is expected to cover 80% of the annual heat demand of the local district-heating network, with recovered heat supplied to households, schools, and public-service buildings at no charge other than a nominal administrative fee. Meta’s Odense campus sends 165,000 megawatt-hours of surplus heat into the local district-heating system, enough for about 9,000 households. Microsoft and Fortum say their Finnish collaboration will use excess heat from data centers to cover about 40% of district-heating demand in Espoo and nearby cities, serving about 250,000 district-heating users. Those are not symbolic gestures; they are examples of digital infrastructure directly supporting local thermal infrastructure. citeturn22view2turn14view3turn22view0

Circularity strengthens the environmental case further. Microsoft reports that in FY24 it reused or recycled 90.9% of servers and components and diverted 88.1% of operational waste, while its Circular Centers can process up to 12,000 decommissioned servers a month and route parts into internal reuse, broader electronics supply chains, or datacenter-training academies. Meta reports that 91% of owned data-center construction waste was diverted from landfills in 2024, that 100% of its owned data centers are certified LEED Gold or higher, and that more than 50% of its operational data-center campus footprint—more than 4,000 acres—is planned, installed, or preserved to support local habitats with native species. citeturn39view1turn22view1turn39view2

The user story you described around clean firm power also matches wider industry direction. DOE explicitly lists existing nuclear support programs and the Generation III+ small modular reactor pathway among tools that can provide carbon-free power for data centers. The IEA expects nuclear to play a growing role in meeting data-center electricity demand after 2030 and says technology companies have plans to finance more than 20 gigawatts of SMRs to date. And on the market side, AP reported that Meta signed nuclear-related deals that together could support up to 6.6 gigawatts by 2035. The pro-data-center case is strongest when it can say: **this project is not just plugging into the grid; it is helping reshape energy infrastructure around cleaner, firmer, more efficient supply.** citeturn27view0turn41view0turn30news46

## Why this infrastructure matters beyond tech

The fastest way to break the “pointless warehouse” stereotype is to talk about what runs inside the buildings. The National Cancer Institute says its Cancer Research Data Commons is a secure, cloud-based data-science infrastructure that makes more than 10 petabytes of data available from hundreds of NCI-funded programs and studies and lets researchers use cloud computation, analytics, and visualization tools to accelerate discoveries in cancer prevention, diagnosis, and treatment. It also provides access to the Seven Bridges Cancer Genomics Cloud, which NCI says offers more than 850 curated tools and workflows for large-scale research. That is a direct line from data centers to cancer science. citeturn25view0

St. Jude Cloud makes the life-saving argument even more tangible. St. Jude describes it as an initiative that provides global researchers with data and analysis resources to advance cures for pediatric cancer and other catastrophic pediatric diseases, and it says the platform is built from scientific expertise, engineering innovation, and cloud infrastructure in partnership with Microsoft and DNAnexus. St. Jude also describes it as the largest cloud-based genomic resource for pediatric cancer and says it is releasing real-time clinical genomics data directly from the clinic to the cloud to speed scientific discovery. That is not “the cloud” as a marketing phrase; it is compute and storage actively shortening the cycle from raw biomedical data to research insight. citeturn25view1

The same logic applies beyond cancer. Microsoft’s local community materials say datacenters enable everything from email and emergency services to hospital records. CDC reports that 95.0% of U.S. office-based physicians had adopted EHR systems in 2024, and CMS says EHRs automate access to medical information and support decision support, quality management, and outcomes reporting. NOAA’s National Centers for Environmental Information says it archives more than 229 terabytes of data each month from more than 130 observing platforms, and that its cloud migration will make large data available on demand for AI and machine learning, speed new products, and better secure vital information. These systems are why data centers are better understood as **public-interest infrastructure**: they are part of the stack behind weather data, emergency operations, modern health records, and large-scale research. citeturn23view2turn26view0turn25view3turn25view2

There is also a climate-and-productivity argument that rarely gets enough airtime. The IEA says proven AI applications can reduce energy costs in energy-intensive industries by 3 to 10 percentage points. That means the same data centers that critics see only as load growth are also a prerequisite for smarter grids, better forecasting, industrial optimization, and more efficient logistics, manufacturing, and building operations. The infrastructure is not inherently good because it is digital; it becomes good when it enables real downstream gains in health, safety, energy use, and economic productivity. citeturn41view1turn25view2

## A stronger and more introspective way to argue it

The most convincing pro-data-center position is **not** “all data centers are good.” It is: “Some data centers are much better than others, and the ones that deserve public support can prove it.” A project earns a stronger defense when it can show that it pays the full marginal cost of its power and water infrastructure instead of shifting those costs to residential customers; uses recycled water, seawater, air cooling, or zero-water evaporation where feasible; signs or co-develops new clean generation and, where appropriate, clean firm power; offers load flexibility, storage, or on-site generation that improves grid reliability; reuses heat and recycled hardware; and funds local workforce and community programs in ways that are measurable and transparent. DOE, Berkeley Lab, EPRI, JLARC, Microsoft, Google, Meta, and AWS are all effectively moving toward that framework, even if not every facility is there yet. citeturn24view1turn12view0turn27view1turn9view0turn22view2turn23view0turn39view1turn39view2

If you turn this into a deck or memo, the strongest visuals to pull are the Berkeley Lab U.S. electricity-use curve, Loudoun County’s tax-revenue and assessed-value exhibits, Google’s Texas and Finland impact-study pages, Google’s Finland environmental page showing 1.09 PUE and 80% heat recovery, Meta’s Odense fact sheet showing heat reuse and community grants, and DOE’s data-center flexibility summary. Those visuals do something important rhetorically: they move the conversation away from abstractions and toward concrete proof of jobs, tax base, efficiency, reuse, and public benefit. citeturn11view0turn10view2turn33view0turn33view2turn33view3turn34view0turn35view0turn12view0

The bottom line is that the “data centers are destroying the Earth” narrative is too blunt to be useful. The evidence supports a more serious position: poorly designed projects can absolutely create local burdens, but well-designed projects can create skilled work, tax capacity, clean-energy demand, water-reuse infrastructure, district heating, medical and research capability, and the digital backbone for emergency services and modern healthcare. The smartest argument is not blind boosterism. It is to insist that data centers be judged by measurable community outcomes—and then show that the best ones are already starting to meet that bar. citeturn41view1turn24view3turn22view2turn25view0turn25view2turn9view0
