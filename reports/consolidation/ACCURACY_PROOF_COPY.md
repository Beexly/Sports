## Section: What the GSE Rating actually is
> A pick's rating isn't a vibe or a hot take. It's a number we can be held to. We take live
> market prices, strip the vig to a fair probability, run our own independent read, and only
> publish when the two agree enough to defend. The rating you see is what survived that —
> not what sounded good.

## Section: Calibration — does the number mean what it says?
> A confidence score is only worth anything if it's *calibrated*: when we say 60%, those
> picks should win about 60 out of 100 over time. We measure that in public with a
> reliability curve — your confidence on one axis, what actually happened on the other. A
> perfectly honest model sits on the diagonal.
>
> **Honest gate:** *[loader]* — until we've settled enough learning-eligible picks, the
> curve is "building." We show the count and the threshold; we don't draw conclusions from a
> handful of games, and we never back-fill the past to look better.

## Section: Closing-line value — the benchmark most services hide
> The single hardest test in this game is beating the closing line — the final, sharpest
> price the market settles on. If your pick was better than the close, you found real value,
> win or lose that day. We grade every pick against the close and show the rate. It's the
> number tout services bury, because it's the one you can't fake.
>
> **Honest gate:** *[loader]* — CLV is shown once we have a fair sample; before that, "still
> measuring."

## Section: The 70% question (read this before you ask)
> People ask for a 70% win rate. Here's the honest answer: a *blended* 70% across all picks
> isn't real — break-even at standard juice is ~52.4%, and the best long-run bettors live in
> the mid-50s. What *is* honest is a **calibrated 70% tier**: a smaller, high-conviction
> slate where a pick labeled ~70% actually wins about 70% — proven by the reliability curve,
> CLV, and a significance test, not asserted. We're building toward that the only way that
> counts: accumulate, measure, prove. When a tier earns the label, the proof is right here.

## Section: Why we lead with CLV and calibration, not a win-rate headline
> Win-loss is the number everyone wants — and the noisiest one there is. It's
> variance-dominated; it can take *thousands* of bets to mean anything, and a hot streak
> proves almost nothing. Closing-line value converges far faster (its swing is roughly a
> tenth of win/loss's), which is why the sportsbooks themselves use *beating the close* to
> decide who's actually sharp. So that's what we lead with: did we get a better number than
> the market's final, sharpest price — graded against the no-vig close, on the same games,
> win or lose. Calibration answers the other half: do our confidence numbers mean what they
> say. A hot week without CLV behind it is luck we won't sell you; a cold week with strong
> CLV is a good process surviving variance, and we'll say so.
>
> *(Honest boundary: CLV is only meaningful in liquid, efficient markets. In thin markets —
> obscure props, niche leagues, early-season — the close itself isn't sharp, so we say less,
> not more.)*

## Section: The No-Bet gate
> Not betting is a position. When the price has already moved to fair — or the inputs aren't
> strong enough to defend — the call is "no bet," and we log it like any other. A service
> that always has a play for you isn't disciplined; it's selling volume.

## Section: Receipts
> Every settled pick carries a tamper-evident receipt — locked at pick time, gradeable
> against the result and the close. You don't have to trust the summary; you can check the
> ledger.
