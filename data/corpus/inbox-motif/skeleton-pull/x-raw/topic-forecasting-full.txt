The mathematics of forecasting: a frontier map (October 2026)
Forecasting mathematics in 2026 is defined less by new forecasting methods than by sharp boundaries: iff-theorems saying exactly which processes, spectra, distribution families and reliability notions are learnable, matched by impossibility theorems saying what no procedure can do. Across eleven clusters and 768 verified works, the same pattern recurs. The classical object, the conditional law L(Xt+h∣Ft)L(Xt+h​∣Ft​), is no longer studied only through its formulas, which are the Wiener–Kolmogorov projection, the filtering equations and the Boltzmann limit. It is studied through four questions. Can it be learned from one history? Can it be certified (calibration, coverage, anytime-valid comparison)? Is it valid over a given horizon (predictability limits, kinetic validity horizons, blowup)? And how should several versions of it be combined (pooling, markets)? The decisive 2024–2026 results sit at the boundaries between these questions, and the most contested claims concern machine-learned forecasters that perform well empirically but sit outside every existing theorem.
The document has six parts before the reviews: the map with its figures, nine cross-cutting theses, a hypothesis ledger with evidence status, ranked reading lists, open problems and an audit of the starting document. Part VII answers a follow-up question: what forecasting theory implies for scaling, and whether mainstream ML training is aligned with it. Part VIII contains the eleven cluster reviews and a review of 2025–2026 preprints from strong research groups, followed by a methods note. Inline links point to the DOI or arXiv record. "Preprint" means not refereed as of 23 October 2026, and "announcement" means a non-refereed release by a company or institution.

Part II. Nine cross-cutting theses
1. Forecastability is now a characterized property
The oldest negative result, Bailey's theorem that no procedure consistently estimates the next-step conditional probability for every stationary ergodic binary process (Györfi et al. 1998), has turned into a programme of exact characterizations. Distribution-dependent learning curves fall into three regimes: exponential, linear, or arbitrarily slow (Bousquet et al. 2021). The processes that admit optimistically universal online learning, meaning a single rule that succeeds whenever any rule can, are now characterized for arbitrary binary concept classes (Hanneke & Wang 2025). The capstone is Blanchard, Shetty & Rakhlin 2026 (COLT 2026; the seed called it a preprint). A family of adaptively chosen distributions permits VC-dependent regret if and only if it is generalized smooth, and the same condition characterizes private learnability. The dynamical analogue appeared the same year. Colbrook, Mezić & Stepanenko 2026 give convergent, certified Koopman spectral algorithms under explicit conditions. Without those conditions, they build adversarial systems on which no single-limit procedure can succeed, so the task requires nested limits. Even the closed-form classical solution has a computability boundary: the Wiener–Kolmogorov MMSE is computable for strictly positive computable spectral densities, but #P1#P1​-complete to compute, and not Turing-computable for some differentiable ones (Boche et al. 2024). The common lesson is that the question "can this be forecast?" has no answer until the class and the success criterion are fixed. The most important open direction is rates: nearly all of these characterizations are qualitative (consistency, or an iff for a regret order), and the log-loss, real-valued versions most relevant to probabilistic forecasting are missing.
2. Reliability splits into a hierarchy, each level with its own price
Statistics and theoretical computer science have converged on the same conclusion: "calibrated" names a family of guarantees with distinct costs. For binary sequential forecasting in ℓ1ℓ1​-ECE, the long-standing T2/3T2/3 barrier fell (Dagan et al. 2025, STOC 2025). The current bracket is Ω(T0.54389)Ω(T0.54389) against O(T0.662942)O(T0.662942), the upper bound from a preprint (Dai & Fishelson 2026). Changing the measure changes the answer. Distance to calibration admits O(T)O(T​) (Qiao & Zheng 2024), and calibration decision loss admits O(log⁡T/T)O(logT/T​) on average (Hu & Wu 2024). Groupwise guarantees cost strictly more. Online multicalibration is Θ(T2/3)Θ(T2/3) up to logarithmic factors (Collina et al. 2026a), and batch multicalibration needs Θ~(ε−3)Θ~(ε−3) samples against ε−2ε−2 for marginal calibration (Collina et al. 2026b, to appear at SODA 2027). Exact truthfulness of calibration measures is impossible sequentially, while approximate truthfulness is attainable (Gokul et al. 2026). Conformal prediction has the matching pattern. Distribution-free conditional coverage is impossible (Barber et al. 2021), coverage over a class of covariate shifts is the strongest achievable relaxation (Gibbs et al. 2025), and a Neyman–Pearson argument identifies the efficiency-optimal valid set (Tibshirani et al. 2026, preprint). On the statistics side, TT-calibration hierarchies (Gneiting & Resin 2023) organize the same distinctions for distributional forecasts. Synthesis. These are not competing definitions but a lattice of guarantees, priced in rounds or samples. The unanswered question is which point in the lattice a given downstream decision needs. The decision-calibration line (Hu & Wu 2024; Roth–Shi; Noarov et al.) is the only programme answering that question directly.
3. Proper scoring rules are the common currency
Strictly proper scores (Gneiting & Raftery 2007, 5,749 OpenAlex citations and the top-ranked work in this map's importance score) now appear in five other places. Robust aggregation pairs each proper score with a max-min optimal pooling rule: Brier with linear pooling, log with logarithmic pooling (Neyman & Roughgarden 2023). Cost-function prediction markets are exactly dual to follow-the-regularized-leader expert algorithms (Chen & Vaughan 2010). Operational ML weather ensembles are trained by minimizing (almost) fair CRPS (Lang et al. 2026) and evaluated by CRPS (Price et al. 2024). LLM event forecasting is ranked by Brier score (Karger et al. 2025). Signature-kernel scores extend strict propriety to laws of whole trajectories (Issa et al. 2023). Elicitability theory sets the limits of this currency: (VaR, ES) is jointly but not separately elicitable (Fissler & Ziegel 2016), tail properties cannot be ranked by expected scores (Brehmer & Strokorb 2019), and evaluating only on extremes causes the forecaster's dilemma (Lerch et al. 2017). The unifying open problem is discrimination: propriety is necessary, but finite-sample power against relevant misspecification, especially of multivariate dependence, has no general theory. Practice built on CRPS-only training and evaluation therefore rests on an untheorized step (thesis 7).
4. Evaluation has become sequential and anytime-valid
The most consequential methodological shift in evaluation is from fixed-sample tests of Diebold–Mariano and Giacomini–White type to e-values and test martingales. These are valid under optional stopping and continuous monitoring, without stationarity assumptions. The foundations are the e-value calculus (Vovk & Wang 2021) and the game-theoretic synthesis (Ramdas et al. 2023). The forecasting-specific results are e-values for comparing probability forecasts (Henzi & Ziegel 2022), confidence sequences for score differences between sequential forecasters (Choe & Ramdas 2024), and e-backtests for VaR and ES (Wang, Wang & Ziegel 2025). The game-theoretic view also reconnects evaluation to foundations, since Ville's inequality, Shafer–Vovk probability and defensive forecasting are one theory seen from different sides. The main limits are bounded-score assumptions (the log score and heavy-tailed CRPS are not covered) and power loss against a prespecified horizon.

5. Predictability limits: intrinsic versus practical, and AI is inside the limit

Lorenz's 1969 multiscale result, where the horizon stays finite as initial error tends to zero, was restated as a precise PDE question by Palmer, Döring & Seregin 2014. The best quantitative estimate for midlatitude weather is about two weeks, and a tenfold reduction in initial error adds at most about five days (Zhang et al. 2019). The 2023–2026 evidence does not support the popular hypothesis that AI breaks this barrier. The measured gains of ML weather models amount to roughly one day of practical lead time, inside the intrinsic limit. Deterministic emulators misrepresent small-perturbation error growth (Selz & Craig 2023), which would falsely imply unlimited predictability. The often-cited "23-day" improvement (Vonich & Hakim 2024) is computed through a model whose error growth is suspect for exactly this reason. Skill beyond two weeks comes from slow, boundary-forced components that physics ensembles also exploit. Underneath, the mathematics is moving. Positive Lyapunov exponents are now proved for Galerkin truncations of 2D stochastic Navier–Stokes (Bedrossian & Punshon-Smith 2024). Spontaneous stochasticity, the idea that deterministic equations with deterministic data yield an intrinsically random but universal law, has one ODE theorem (Drivas, Mailybaev & Raibekas 2023) and a renormalization-group framework for discrete models (Mailybaev & Raibekas 2023). It remains a conjecture for Navier–Stokes. If it holds, the positive side of the real butterfly effect is that individual realizations are unpredictable while their law is predictable and universal. That would make probabilistic forecasting the only well-posed kind at long horizons.
6. Validity horizons of statistical descriptions
A second sense of "how far ahead" asks for how long a statistical description, rather than a trajectory, remains a valid forecast. The landmark is the hard-sphere theorem of Deng, Hani & Ma 2024 (Annals of Mathematics, forthcoming). The Boltzmann equation is the correct forecast for as long as its regular solution exists, so the kinetic forecast's validity horizon equals the lifespan of the limit PDE. Its continuation to fluid equations, in iterated limits (Deng, Hani & Ma 2025, preprint), is the strongest current claim on Hilbert's sixth problem. Uncertainty quantification lags the mean forecast: fluctuation and large-deviation theory is still short-time out of equilibrium (Bodineau et al. 2023). For long-run statistics under parameter change, linear response is proved for Axiom A, noisy and some intermittent systems (Ruelle 2009; Hairer & Majda 2010; Dragičević et al. 2025). It remains unproved for any open class of deterministic non-uniformly hyperbolic flows, which is the setting of climate response forecasting. Berger's emergence adds a third failure mode, distinct from chaos and from non-differentiable response. The set of statistical descriptions that orbits settle into can be super-polynomially complex. The Annals construction of analytic pseudo-rotations (Berger 2026) disproves Birkhoff's 1941 conjecture and gives examples with maximal local emergence, and whether high emergence is typical is open (Berger 2026b, preprint).
7. Machine-learned forecasters work in practice but sit outside existing theorems
ML forecasting has the most empirical success and the least theorem coverage of any cluster. Probabilistic ML ensembles beat the ECMWF physics ensemble on marginal scores: GenCast was better on 97.2% of 1,320 targets (Price et al. 2024), and ECMWF's AIFS ENS has been operational since July 2025. Four theoretical gaps stand out. First, FGN trains only on per-location marginal CRPS yet reproduces joint spatial structure (Alet et al. 2025, preprint). A marginal score cannot identify a joint law, so the structure must come from the architecture, and no theorem says when this suffices. Second, operator learning faces a curse of parametric complexity: smoothness alone forces parameter counts exponential in 1/ε1/ε (Lanthaler & Stuart 2025). Whatever structure emulators exploit (dissipativity, attractor dimension, locality) is unidentified. Third, extrapolation fails at the edge of the training support, which is where warnings matter: models trained without Category 3–5 cyclones cannot forecast Category 5 storms (Sun et al. 2025). Fourth, time-series foundation models lead curated benchmarks, but reusing LLM weights adds nothing (Tan et al. 2024). For chaotic systems, a context-parroting baseline matches foundation models, and accuracy scales with the attractor's fractal dimension (Zhang & Gilpin 2025, preprint). Apparent in-context forecasting may therefore be nearest-neighbour analog forecasting. On the filtering side, guided diffusion for data assimilation cannot be both exact and efficient in general, assuming one-way functions exist (Gupta et al. 2024). The only positive theorem linking ML surrogates to assimilation needs strong inflation (Sanz-Alonso & Waniorek 2025). A related line treats large sequence models as approximate Bayes or Solomonoff mixtures. Supporting evidence comes from compression (Delétang et al. 2024) and linearly decodable belief states (Shai et al. 2024). Against it, LLMs violate martingale properties that any Bayesian predictor must satisfy (Falck et al. 2024). That hypothesis is contested and active.
8. Aggregation: simple rules win for statistical reasons; markets aggregate only under idealizations
The forecast-combination puzzle, where equal weights beat estimated weights, is understood as a bias–variance trade-off, and simple quantile ensembles were the most reliable forecasters in the COVID-19 hubs. The pooling theorems are sharp. Linear pools of calibrated forecasts are miscalibrated (Ranjan & Gneiting 2010), which motivates extremizing. Extremizing after averaging has a worst-case justification under "projective substitutes" (Neyman & Roughgarden 2022), but no scheme beats a trivial benchmark with many conditionally iid experts (Arieli et al. 2018). Market aggregation holds for separable securities and idealized traders (Ostrovsky 2012). It breaks under ambiguity aversion, and no security aggregates for every information structure (Galanis et al. 2024). The first large-scale evidence from Polymarket and Kalshi is still mostly in working papers, and it points both ways. Prices track realized frequencies (Reichenbach & Walther 2025), but identical contracts diverged across venues in 2024 (Clinton & Huang 2025), and wash-trading-like activity makes volume an invalid proxy for crowd size (Sirolly et al. 2025). In asset returns, out-of-sample predictability is weak (Welch & Goyal 2008). The "virtue of complexity" (Kelly, Malamud & Zhou 2024) is contested by the argument that P≫TP≫T random-feature forecasts reduce to volatility-timed momentum (Nagel 2025, working paper). That dispute is itself about implicit aggregation over a huge ensemble. LLM forecasters enter as highly correlated experts. ForecastBench showed superforecasters ahead in 2024 (Karger et al. 2025), and the Forecasting Research Institute's July 2026 update reports several systems statistically indistinguishable from superforecasters (FRI 2026, blog post). Leakage critiques (Paleka et al. 2025) keep this contested.
9. Three structural gaps, and AI-generated proofs as a new kind of evidence
Three weak links appear in Figure 2. First, filtration-sensitive evaluation. Adapted optimal transport gives the right geometry for comparing forecasting models that differ in when information is revealed (Bartl, Beiglböck & Pammer 2026; Backhoff-Veraguas et al. 2020). Every proper score in use, including signature scores, is blind to filtrations, so no evaluation method yet measures what adapted OT measures. Second, single-path estimation. Adapted-distance estimation, Koopman rates and universal-prediction rates are proved mostly for iid paths or reversible stochastic dynamics, whereas forecasting usually observes one long nonstationary trajectory. Third, PDE results and statistical evaluation do not meet. Validity-horizon theorems say nothing about calibration of the resulting forecasts, and no evaluation theory targets kinetic or fluid forecasts.
Separately, 2026 brought AI-generated mathematics into the evidence base. In September 2026 OpenAI released a manuscript and Lean formalization claiming smooth, finite-energy, forced blowup for 3D Navier–Stokes on R3R3 and T3T3, which corresponds to Clay alternatives (C) and (D) (OpenAI 2026, announcement). A companion manuscript claims unforced 3D Euler blowup. Clay described the problem as apparently settled, announced a deliberately unhurried evaluation, and has awarded nothing. The unforced alternatives (A) and (B) are untouched. Independent checking is partial. A Part I reconstruction of the profile construction has appeared (Lei & Ren 2026, preprint). An audit documents mismatches between the prose and the Lean code and argues that a compiling formalization certifies the formal statement, not the paper (Bastounis, Circelli & Hansen 2026, preprint). There is also a priority dispute with the Córdoba–Martínez-Zoroa and Alpöge–Buckmaster line (Alpöge et al. 2026, preprint). ML-assisted discovery of unstable singular profiles (Wang et al. 2025, preprint) belongs to the same shift. These are results whose verification standard is itself under debate, and this map labels them accordingly.
